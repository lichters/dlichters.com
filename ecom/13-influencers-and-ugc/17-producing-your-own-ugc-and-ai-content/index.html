Playbook › 13. Influencers and UGC

Producing your own UGC and AI content

Section 13.17 · 17 of 21 in this chapter · 11 min read

In short

  • Film your own product on a phone first. Pay creators only when the product makes steady profit.
  • Normal people filmed on a phone are trusted more than polished brand ads.
  • Show the product in use right away. The first 5 seconds decide whether people keep watching.
  • Reusing other people's videos is possible, but not advised.
This section has 11 parts. Tap one to open it.

Planning and Principles

1 min
  • Own UGC (user-generated content, meaning videos of real people, not brand ads) is the starting point when there is no budget. Film it yourself first. Move to paid creators only once the product shows consistent profit.
  • Phone footage is usually good enough when you test a product early. Shoot the product from several angles: held up, a slow slide across a surface, and slow zooms (the camera moves gradually closer). This gives the editor (the person who cuts the video) enough material for the story.
  • Custom videos cost time and money, because each custom UGC video needs creator credits (paid units used to pay creators) or production. Spend on them only after the original product shows consistent profit.
  • Custom content performs better than existing content on TikTok. Prioritise your own videos over reused viral clips (clips that spread widely online).
  • Custom content recorded by the owner on a phone, with simple scripts and no studio, performs better than stock drop-shipping footage (generic clips that many sellers of the same product reuse). Viewers relate to a normal person explaining the benefit.
  • Authentic creator footage filmed on a phone outperforms polished company production. Viewers trust a regular person more than a brand.
  • Strong video ads combine three things: showing how the product works, using UGC as social proof (proof from other people who used it), and a first 5 seconds that grab attention. The opening sets the base for the rest of the video.

Filming Yourself and Solo Shoots

1 min
  • Shoot solo when no helper is available. Hold the product in one hand, operate the phone with the other, and prop the phone on a towel or flat surface for static shots (shots where the camera does not move). Ask a partner to help only for shots that need two hands.
  • A UGC video can be shot by one person in an ordinary room, such as a bedroom or bathroom, when no studio is available. A plain surface is fine. The goal is to show the product clearly, not to create a clean product-photo backdrop.
  • If there is no budget for paid UGC, the creator can film the product personally, add a voice-over (spoken narration recorded over the video) and short text overlays (words placed on top of the video). Reusing other people's content is possible but not advised.
  • Warehouse or packaging videos with your own logo add a layer of trust for customers. A freelancer (a self-employed person hired per job) can create this kind of corporate video.
  • Customer or staff-filmed product videos can serve as UGC. Film real use, explain the product's feel and fit, and post them on the store and on YouTube.
  • Point-of-view (POV) content, filmed as if the viewer is the one holding the product, works well on Instagram for product marketing. Learn this format before running paid creator campaigns.
  • Clients often find it useful to see a real video of a procedure from the business itself, such as a dentist's own footage of a treatment. It adds credibility to ads.

Outsourced Filmmakers, Photographers and Creators

1 min
  • Your own creatives (ad images and videos you own) can be made quickly with photographers and videographers. Examples are people sourced from China or Fiverr (an online marketplace for freelancers). They shoot product photos and videos. Show the product in hand or in use, not only the face.
  • A product bought from Amazon can be used for sample shots if it is cheap enough. Express shipping can deliver a product to the UK within two to three days for filming.
  • A product shown being used by a creator in a reaction or try-on video usually converts better (leads to more sales) than a brand-made ad. Buyers respond to the face and reaction, not only to the product.
  • Creator reaction or try-on workflow (a fixed set of steps):
    1. Send the product to the creator first.
    2. Let the creator film a reaction or try-on video.
    3. Ask for a private draft (an unpublished first version) and review the video before it is published.
    4. Add any missing product details during the review, before posting.
  • If the person on camera does not match the target audience in age or style, outsource the on-camera part or the voiceover to a script writer or creator instead of filming it yourself. Record the script anyway. The voiceover is still the timing reference for the edit.

Partnering and Exchanging Shoots

1 min
  • Partner with friends in your network who match your target audience (the age group or type of person you sell to). Exchange UGC shoots for free.

Scripting and Shot Structure

2 min
  • Ask for a UGC piece about the product built on the problem-agitate-solution framework (a story shape: name the problem, make it feel real, then show the fix). Then refine the output with follow-up prompts (extra instructions sent after the first answer) before passing it to creators or clients.
  • Create short, exaggerated reaction clips of the presenter using the product in different settings, such as at a desk, in the bath, or at a friend's place. Use the funniest or most surprising clip as the ad hook (the first moment that stops people scrolling). Keep the others as alternative openings for testing.
  • The answer to "how would you describe the brand to a friend" contains the sales language buyers use without being asked. It also shows the order in which they rank benefits. Use these exact phrases and that benefit order in UGC scripts, testimonial copy, and word-of-mouth ads (ads that copy how friends recommend things).
  • Hooks and benefit scenes: the creator performs an action without speaking on camera. The voiceover is added in editing. Speaking on camera is needed only for scenes such as a testimonial or a call to action (the line that tells viewers what to do next).
  • Speaking to camera: videos where the seller speaks to the camera received fewer hate comments and gave better like-to-view ratios (likes divided by views). Viewers also trusted the seller more. Talking to camera was useful when the video was clean and good quality.
  • Speaker profile: the speaker should sound like someone who was skeptical (doubtful) before, and changed their mind only after trying the product.
  • Naming a personal insecurity: testimonials that describe a personal insecurity remind the viewer of their own insecurity and how it makes them feel. Selling works by solving a problem or insecurity the buyer already feels, so the speaker should name it openly.

Modular 60-Second Script Workflow

Modular means you record once, cut the recording into parts, and mix the parts in different orders.

Step Action Ids
1 Divide a 60-second script into scenes: for example five hooks of five to six seconds, several benefit scenes, and two to three call-to-action scenes.
2 Write a creative brief (a short written plan) for each scene with the exact action the creator should perform.
3 Have the creator record the full 60 seconds once.
4 Record voiceover and captions (on-screen text) as separate files.
5 Combine hooks, benefits, and calls to action in different orders to produce 10 to 20 different videos from one recording.
6 Test the combinations in the market and keep the best performers.

Voiceovers, AI Voices and Audio

1 min
  • If you are uncomfortable with your own voice, use AI voice generators (tools that turn text into a spoken voice), such as 11 Labs, with voices such as Adam or Bella.
  • 11 Labs is an AI voice generator for UGC videos. It copies the feel of UGC cheaply by combining found footage (clips you find rather than film) with natural-sounding voiceovers. It is an alternative to hiring creators.
  • Match the audience's accent with the voiceover. For an Australian audience, an Australian voiceover tested better than an American one. An American voiceover did not beat a self-recorded voice.

Sourcing Footage and B-Roll

1 min

B-roll means extra supporting clips shown while the narration plays.

  • Source footage from TikTok, Instagram, or AliExpress product videos where available.
  • Use footage of the product, such as supplier or AliExpress clips (videos from the seller or maker), for product introductions.
  • For emotional scenes, such as someone looking angry or exhausted, search a stock library (a website of ready-made clips) by emotion.
  • Reusing other people's content is possible but not advised. Prefer your own footage.
  • Re-shooting a proven competitor video (filming your own version of a rival's video that already works): keep the same hook, message, vibe and caption. Change only the setting, and optionally the music.

Testimonials, Personas and Storytelling Patterns

2 min

Testimonial Sources and Credibility

  • A named, credible person speaking for the product, such as a respected user or expert, carries more weight than an anonymous company voice. Their personal authority can make a claim believable.
  • Let a third party, such as a doctor or expert, recommend the product in the story. Do not let the brand claim its own superiority (that it is the best). This keeps the tone editorial (like neutral advice, not an ad) and makes the recommendation more credible.
  • Use testimonials (customer stories about results) from named, real customers who live in the same town or area as the prospect (the buyer you want to reach). Quote a specific outcome they experienced. Local recognition of the customer's name gives the claim more weight than the seller's own words.
  • Testimonial clips of real people describing a visible result can be reused as the proof part of a new creative (a new ad). This includes clips from ads for other products in the same category. Choose a speaker the buyer can identify with, because that is what the buyer wants to see.

Celebrity, Expert and Everyday Voices

  • Bring in other people to see the product through their eyes. Use a celebrity testimonial for a famous endorsement. Use an ordinary person's case history (their story of what happened to them) for identification. Their actions and reactions make the performance feel new.
  • Show experts reacting with surprise. Professional or sophisticated people who are impressed by the product add surprise, competition and discovery to the image. Their astonishment is more convincing than the ad's own claim.
  • Use both celebrity testimonials and ordinary people's case histories to reaffirm (confirm again) the benefits. Celebrities give status. Everyday people give the reader an easy identity to share. Both show the product from a new angle.
  • Showing envied or admired people in ads can increase desire, because readers want the status or outcome the person represents. Use the buyer's aspirations (what they hope for) to choose who appears.

Chained Persona Testimonials

  • A strong testimonial ad can chain several short creator testimonials. Each speaker uses a different persona (a character type, such as a parent, a relative of someone with a specific problem, or a professional). Each persona names a different problem or angle. One ad then covers several viewer segments (groups of viewers) while the product stays the same. Keep each speaker's story short and tied to one concrete result.

Contrast Testimonial Pattern

Pattern (a repeatable story shape): a relatable person, such as a parent or partner, describes a contrast between age or a condition and how they look or feel. They credit the product as the cause. It works because a third person's visible result feels like proof, not a sales claim.

  • Example 1: "My husband is fifty, but he keeps up with the kids on weekends since he started this protein mix."
  • Example 2: "My grandma was always cold until she began taking this iron supplement."

Faceless and AI-Generated Content

1 min
  • Faceless UGC-style videos (no real face shown) and short viral content (clips built to spread fast) can be made with AI tools. No creator has to appear on camera.
  • TopView, or similar AI platforms (online tools), creates realistic product mockups (lifelike fake images or videos) showing a person holding or using the product. This removes the need to send physical samples to influencers (people with online followers) for every visual.

AI-Generated UGC Photos

1 min

A prompt is the instruction you type into an AI tool. Prompt, paste as-is:

Write 10 image prompts for a UGC-style photo of one person holding my book. Give each prompt a different person (age, gender, natural and varied skin tone), a different camera angle and a different setting, and have the person hold the book up toward the camera. Keep the whole book cover visible and facing the camera, and do not let the person or the frame cut the cover in half. Give every prompt an authentic phone-camera look with soft focus, slight grain and natural colour, without studio gloss. Output one prompt per line, ready to paste.

UGC Photo Batch Procedure

  1. Ask Claude for 10 scene prompts with different people, angles and settings.
  2. Read each prompt and remove any that do not make sense.
  3. Paste 2 or 3 scenes into Book Scene Studio as a test.
  4. Upload the book cover, from 1 to 3 angles.
  5. Set the aspect (the frame shape) to 4:5 portrait (taller than it is wide).
  6. Run the test and check that the whole book is visible in each output.
  7. Once the test passes, shoot the full list.
  8. Upload the chosen images to the review platform manually.

Phone-Camera Style Rule

  • Stating a phone-camera style in the prompt, such as an older phone model look with slight softness, grain (speckled texture) and unedited colour, makes the image read more like a real customer photo. It looks less like studio gloss (a shiny, polished studio look).
  • Name the camera style in every shot prompt. Vary the person, angle and setting across at least three UGC variations.

Creative Hooks and Attention

1 min
  • Product-in-use is the key. Show how the product works in the first moments, then support it with UGC as social proof.
