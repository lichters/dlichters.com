Playbook › 13. Influencers and UGC

Vetting influencers and spotting fakes

Section 13.6 · 6 of 21 in this chapter · 13 min read

In short

  • Pick influencers by whether their followers are the people who would buy from you, not by how many followers they have.
  • Check engagement (likes, comments and shares compared with the audience size). Aim for at least 5 percent, and look for real comments.
  • Fake followers and fake likes are common. Check comments and past work before you send free product or pay.
  • Ask for analytics screenshots from the app before you commit. Walk away from anyone who refuses.

Choosing Influencer Tiers and Follower Ranges

3 min

An influencer is a person with an online audience who promotes products. A tier is a size group. Followers are people who subscribe to a creator's account.

Tier definitions

  • A micro-influencer (a small creator) generally has 1 to 100,000 followers. On TikTok, where follower counts run higher, a large following is worth less per follower than on Instagram.
  • A macro-influencer (a large creator) has 100,000 to 1 million followers and offers a broader reach (the number of people who see the content).
  • A celebrity influencer, such as Kim Kardashian, usually produces lower engagement (how much the audience reacts) but adds strong reputational credibility (trust the brand gains by association).
  • The follower ranges for micro (1 to 100,000) and macro (100,000 to 1 million) influencers are a working convention, meaning a rule of thumb, not an industry standard. A platform is an app such as TikTok or Instagram. Platform matters: the same follower count carries less value on TikTok than on Instagram.
  • Mid-level influencers were grouped at roughly 100,000 to 600,000 followers, and they usually expect payment rather than free product.
  • Older advice (Source A to 2022 material, as labelled in the source): the ranges above differ from the creator's own grouping, so use them only as orientation, meaning as a rough guide.

Where the sweet spot lies

A sweet spot is the follower range that gives the best value for your money.

  • The influencer sweet spot is 20,000 to 50,000 followers: they have an audience but are not yet demanding.
  • Between 50,000 and 80,000 followers, influencers know their value and may need extra cash up front.
  • Above 100,000 followers, deals usually require substantial cash flow (a lot of money paid ahead of time), and the influencer often has managers or agencies (people who handle their business).
  • Target influencers with about 10K to 50K followers, since they usually charge less and often have loyal audiences (followers who keep coming back). Verify their engagement rather than judging by follower count.
  • Newer creators who have just seen success often produce the most sales per dollar spent. Large celebrity deals were reported to give poor returns.
  • Instagram creators under about 30,000 followers gave good results, and they usually do not ask for payment. If they do ask, the fee is typically around 100 or less.
  • Best follower range in one tested mix: roughly 30,000 to 300,000, because these accounts have a more targeted (aimed at the right people) and engaged audience. Above about 1 million followers, the audience becomes too broad and conversion (viewers who actually buy) drops.

Micro-influencers versus larger publications

  • Small micro-influencers with 1,000 to 2,000 followers rarely convert (turn viewers into buyers) into customers for a new store. Affiliate partnerships (the partner earns a commission on each sale) work better with larger online publications, such as blogs, digital magazines and companies, that already reach people interested in the product. **
  • Micro-influencers with niche authority (expertise in one specific topic area) and smaller but engaged audiences are more accessible and effective than celebrities. A creator with 1,000 followers in your niche who posts regularly can drive trust-based conversions better than a celebrity with no connection to the product.
  • These two statements conflict in the number they name (1,000 followers is effective in and rarely converts in ). Apply when the creator is in your niche and posts regularly. Apply when judging a small account for affiliate sales for a new store.

Tier mix from one tested campaign

  • The influencer mix used was 20% micro-influencers, 50% mid-level and 30% high-level. Micro-influencers work for free product, mid-level usually expects payment, and high-level influencers are expensive.
  • Use high-level influencers only after testing, and only with a budget that can absorb the risk (lose money without hurting the business). Keep the first posts with micro and mid-level creators until you know which ones convert.
  • High-level influencers with very large followings tend to have lower engagement and are less likely to convert. Their posts reach only a fraction (a small part) of their followers, and they are costly and often require a manager or agency.

Choosing by Audience Fit, Not Size

2 min

Audience fit means the people following the creator are the same people who would buy from you.

Demographic match first

A demographic is a group of people described by age, gender, location and interests.

  • Choose influencers by whether their followers match your customer demographic, not by follower count. A large following does not guarantee that the audience will buy from you.
  • Assuming that a large follower count means an influencer can sell effectively to your customers is a common error.
  • Partnering with an influencer whose followers match your buyer demographic lets you reach likely customers quickly. Matching demographics matter more than raw audience size.
  • An influencer's audience must match the brand's target customer. For example, a brand selling to middle-aged women should avoid a child toy reviewer whose audience is about 10 years old.
  • Influencers in different niches can have completely different audiences, so an influencer's demographic must be compared with your target customers before any collaboration. A large following in the wrong demographic is not a sales channel (a way to sell) for your product.
  • Choosing the wrong influencers can do more harm than good to a brand, so selection matters more than volume (how many). The right creators increase engagement, awareness (people knowing your brand) and conversion rates.
  • An influencer whose audience matches your demographic can be worth working with even if you personally do not align with them. Decide in advance which content you are willing to repost (share again on your own account).
  • Very niche creators, such as eyelash or gym-focused influencers, and YouTube creators can deliver loyal audiences at lower cost. The speaker reported about a 10x return (ten times the money spent) on influencer spend when research was done carefully.

Niche and style fit

  • Do not assume an influencer successful with one brand will generate results for your product. Influencer performance is inconsistent across different brands and products.
  • Study the influencer's content on every platform they use, not just one. Check that their style, tone (way of speaking), theme and brand message align with yours. Misaligned messaging is a reason to skip the collaboration, even when the audience is large.
  • Pet-focused accounts can engage well with their followers and promote products, but some animals or accounts over-promote many brands. Assess the account's posting history (its list of past posts) before working with one.
  • Read the influencer's past promotions to judge whether the style feels authentic (real, not forced) and engaging before partnering.

Audience demographics to check

  • Review the audience breakdown (how the audience is split) by country, gender and age.
  • Compare the demographics (age, location, interests) and audience countries with your product before committing budget (the money you plan to spend).
  • Compare the demographics and engagement rates on the platform before deciding.
  • Platform data allows a data-driven (based on numbers) and informed choice of influencers.

Evaluating Engagement

2 min

Engagement means likes, comments and shares. The engagement rate compares these with the number of followers. A benchmark is a standard to compare against.

Engagement rate benchmarks

  • Assess influencer quality by evaluating followers count, engagement rate on posts, content relevance to your niche, posting frequency (how often they post), and audience demographic alignment with your target customers.
  • Use engagement rate, not follower count, as the main selection metric (the number you decide by), and require at least 5 percent. Engagement can be faked too, so verify it with a second analytics tool (a program that shows account statistics) before reaching out.
  • Healthy influencer engagement is about 5% to 10% of followers as likes. Also check that the comments are positive and that followers actually interact with the posts.
  • A high engagement rate (likes, comments and shares relative to the audience) suggests a dedicated, responsive audience (people who are loyal and react) and is a positive signal when choosing influencers. Use this metric to judge audience activity, not follower count alone.
  • Judge a creator by engagement and by the conversation under their posts, not by follower count alone. A large following on TikTok, or a celebrity with millions of followers, often comes with lower engagement per follower.
  • Check each partner's engagement before reaching out and skip those with fake engagement or overpriced rates. Not every large audience is worth paying for.

Recent engagement and views

Views are the number of times a post was watched.

  • When hiring influencers or creators on Twitter or other platforms, check engagement in the last three months rather than only follower count. Creators with good recent engagement are a better bet (a safer choice) than those with large but inactive audiences.
  • Follower count is a weak predictor (weak sign of future results) of views, because TikTok reach is highly volatile (it changes a lot). A small account can out-view a much larger one, so judge creators by actual recent views rather than by followers.
  • An account with about 50,000 followers should typically generate at least about 3,000 views per post. A sponsored post (a paid post) with a steady audience can then reach a meaningful number of people.
  • Example: a channel with 100,000 subscribers should have a recent video with at least 20,000 to 30,000 views. If it only gets views from one or two viral posts (posts that suddenly spread very widely), the channel is considered dead.

Follower-to-engagement example from a real account

  • A genuine creator (a real person, not a fake account) with about 113,000 followers had likes that varied widely, from roughly 6 to 1,500 per post, with specific and relevant comments. Uniform, inflated engagement (the same high numbers every time) with generic emoji comments is the warning sign.

Detecting Fake Followers and Comments

1 min

Principles

  • Fake or bought followers are common and can make a profile look successful. Verify engagement carefully before sending product, because some fake profiles reach out to brands constantly, asking for free product.
  • Follower count alone is not a quality signal, because about 30,000 followers can be bought cheaply on freelance marketplaces (websites where people sell services). Fake profiles built this way then pitch (offer themselves to) brands constantly for free product.
  • Many influencers buy fake likes and comments, so judging them by appearance or follower count is unreliable. Evaluate their audience engagement and comment quality instead.
  • Views and likes are easy to fake, so check the comments on an influencer's posts. Real, content-specific comments (comments about that exact post) indicate genuine engagement.

Comment and like check

Red flags (warning signs):

  • Generic emoji-only comments and irrelevant replies usually mean bots (automatic fake accounts) or bought followers.
  • Very consistent or inflated like counts can indicate fake engagement.
  • Uniform, inflated engagement with generic emoji comments is the warning sign.

Green signals (good signs):

  • Real, content-specific comments indicate genuine engagement.
  • Likes that vary from post to post and comments that are specific and relevant.
  • Comments are positive and followers actually interact with the posts.

Growth Curves and Follower Trends

1 min

A growth curve is a line on a chart that shows follower numbers over time.

  • Check the follower growth curve over recent months. A steady decline in followers, especially after a public controversy (a public argument or scandal), signals lost engagement. A small drop of around 5 percent can be accepted, while larger drops are a red flag.

Past Collaborations and Authenticity

1 min

Reviewing past brand work

  • Review past brand collaborations and check that the content looks genuine.
  • Check past collaborations to see whether the influencer's content looks genuine: people should interact with it, and their earlier ads with other brands should show how well they work with brands like yours. This indicates whether their audience will respond to your product.
  • Count how many paid placements (paid posts) a brand has run with an influencer. Three or more previous placements by different brands signal the influencer is profitable (brands keep paying them), while a single one-off promotion is a warning sign.
  • Look at the posts they made for other brands and judge how well those posts performed.
  • Ask the brands they have worked with about how reliably they executed (how well they delivered what they promised).
  • Check whether the audiences of the influencer and of similar brands overlap (share the same people).

Vetting order before outreach

Vetting means checking carefully before you agree. Outreach means making the first contact.

Vet a potential influencer in this order before reaching out:

  1. Study their content across all platforms and check their style, tone, theme and brand message for alignment with your brand.
  2. Evaluate engagement rate from likes, comments and shares; high engagement signals a dedicated, responsive audience.
  3. Review past collaborations and authenticity: look at their previous ads, how they worked with other brands, and whether their audience overlaps with the audiences of similar brands.
  4. Contact the influencer only after these checks are done.

Loyalty and trust

  • For mid-level creators, a loyal following matters more than raw reach (the total number of people who see content). Check whether followers trust the creator and want to be like them. That trust is what turns viewers into buyers.

Full Vetting Checklist

1 min

Use this checklist in order before offering product or budget.

  1. Confirm the follower count is in the range that fits your budget, for example 30,000 to 300,000 for micro-influencers.
  2. Check the post count and compare it to the follower count. A very low number of posts for a large following is a warning sign.
  3. Read the comments: generic emoji-only comments and irrelevant replies usually mean bots or bought followers.
  4. Check whether likes look organic (real, not bought). Very consistent or inflated like counts can indicate fake engagement.
  5. Look at the posts they made for other brands and judge how well those posts performed.
  6. Ask the brands they have worked with about how reliably they executed.
  7. Confirm that their audience matches your target demographic before offering product.

Requesting Analytics and Media Kits

1 min

Analytics are the statistics an account shows about its performance. A media kit is a shareable summary of a creator's audience and numbers.

Ask before you commit

  • Ask influencers to share their platform analytics before a collaboration. Most influencers provide these freely when asked.
  • Ask the creator for views from the past week and past month, plus average story views (views on temporary posts that disappear after a day).
  • Ask for a screenshot of the creator's TikTok analytics, and do not work with anyone who refuses.

Verifying TikTok analytics

  • To verify (check) a TikTok creator's performance, open TikTok Creator Tools (TikTok's built-in menu for creators) and then Analytics. Ask the creator to send a screenshot of this Analytics page covering the past week and the past month.

Media kits

  • TikTok has an in-app media kit generator (a tool inside the app) that lets creators build a shareable media kit without leaving the app. It was launched after Instagram's Edits app had introduced a similar feature.
  • The Instagram Edits app can export a one-page PDF (a document file) of your analytics. It covers audience analytics, post analytics for the past 30 days, or analytics for one specific post. Send this PDF to brands when they ask for performance data.
  • When a brand asks for a media kit, send one generated from TikTok or Instagram. Those apps hold the platform's own analytics and are the most accurate source.

Building a Shortlist from Creator Research

1 min

A shortlist is your list of the best candidates.

  1. Find creators whose audience matches your potential customers.
  2. Check the creator's recent followers and commenters (people who write comments).
  3. Check which other creators in your niche those followers follow.
  4. Record followers, posting frequency and recent views for each creator in a list.
  5. Ask the creator for views from the past week and past month, plus average story views.
  6. Ask for a screenshot of the creator's TikTok analytics, and do not work with anyone who refuses.
  7. Start contacting the creators on the list, most promising first.
