When to start and how much to spend

Section 16.2 · 2 of 24 in this chapter · 6 min read

In short

  • Retargeting means paying for ads that show again to people who already visited your store.
  • Most sources say set it up on launch day. Several say wait until the store has visitors and sales.
  • Start small. Use about 10% of your total ad money for it at first.
  • Scale up only after about two weeks of steady sales for the product.
  • With no early sales, there is nothing to retarget.

Timing: Launch Day or After Traffic and Sales

Sources differ on when paid retargeting should start. Paid retargeting means paying for ads that show again to people who already visited your store. Most sources recommend setting it up on launch day. Several sources also say the store needs visitors (traffic) and sales first, before paid retargeting gets money.

Set up at launch

  • Set up Facebook remarketing (ads for past visitors on Facebook) from the first day, next to Google Ads (Google's paid ad system). Most first-time visitors from search do not buy when they land (figure cited: about 98%), so they need to be brought back.
  • Facebook ads can be set up from day one, but only for remarketing to people who already visited the store. Not for cold prospecting (showing ads to people who never visited).
  • Set up remarketing campaigns (groups of ads with their own budget and goal) on Google, Bing (Microsoft's search ads) and Facebook at launch. Visitors who leave without buying can then be shown the products again.
  • Start remarketing campaigns on Google and Facebook early in the launch. Visitors who did not buy can be reached again.
  • Set up Facebook remarketing campaigns to start as soon as the first store traffic arrives, not later. This lets retargeting turn visitors into buyers (conversions) from the launch day.
  • Even one extra remarketing sale every few days can be very profitable early on. That is why remarketing should be live at launch.
  • Run small remarketing campaigns on both Google and Meta (the company that runs Facebook and Instagram) from the start, next to Shopping (Google's product ads). (2025)
  • On Facebook, start with remarketing only. Do not spend on cold audiences (people who never visited) or brand awareness (ads that just make people know your name) at first.

Wait for traffic, sales and performance

  • Retargeting needs store traffic and sales first. Audiences (groups of people the ads target) are built from visitors and buyers. Without early sales, there is nothing to retarget.
  • Abandoned cart emails (emails to people who left items in their cart) and exit-intent capture (a pop-up when a visitor's mouse moves to leave the page) are the first retargeting layer. Paid remarketing on Google and Facebook comes after the store has traffic.
  • Start retargeting after about two weeks of consistent performance from the product (steady visits and sales). Before that, the audiences are too small and the data too thin.

How to read the conflict

  • The launch-day sources describe setting up campaigns and capturing the first visitors (collecting them, for example as email contacts).
  • The traffic-first sources describe when paid budget should be funded and grown.
  • The only dividing line given in the sources is the two-week rule.

Budget Share of Total Ad Spend

"Share" means the part of your total ad money that goes to retargeting. "Dynamic product ads" are ads that show the exact products a visitor looked at. Your "ad budget" is all the money you spend on ads.

Source C 10% to 15% Always Undated ,
  • Source C recommends 10-15% of total ad budget, because warm customers (people who already know your store) convert (buy) more easily.
  • Source A recommends about 10%. The 10-15% range and the 10-20% range (early phase) are wider. Use about 10% as the default. Treat the higher ranges as the sources' upper limits.
  • Source B describes retargeting taking a growing share of ad spend over time. The business then relies less on paying to reach cold visitors and leads near the bottom of the funnel (the path from first look to buying; the bottom is closest to buying). Retargeting visitors who already know the brand is the way to reduce that dependence.
  • The early-phase share and the long-term growing share are different stages. The 10% figures apply at launch. Source B's direction applies once the brand has its own returning visitors.

  • Bing remarketing is also set up at launch. No Bing split is given in the sources.
  • Shift budget toward Google Ads for new prospects when remarketing takes too much of the total.

Warning Signs of Over-Weighting Retargeting

Over-weighting means giving retargeting too much of the money.

  • Putting most of the budget into remarketing is a warning sign. It means the store is not finding new prospects (people who have not visited yet).
  • Response: shift the budget back toward Google Ads for new visitors.
  • Keep the remarketing share closer to 10% in the early phase.
  • The warning sign applies while the store is still finding its first buyers. Source B's growing share describes a later stage, once returning visitors and buyers exist. No threshold (exact limit) is given in the sources.

When to Scale

"Scale" means spending more once things work.

Conditions before scaling

  • Retargeting starts after about two weeks of consistent performance from the product.
  • Scale only once traffic and sales are growing.
  • Increase the budget only once it brings sales.

Scaling steps

  1. Confirm about two weeks of consistent product performance.
  2. Confirm that traffic and sales are growing.
  3. Duplicate the campaign (make a copy) to target only 7-day website visitors (people who came to the site in the last 7 days). This audience usually converts best because those visitors were on the store recently.
  4. Increase the budget only where it brings sales.
  5. Add longer audiences, such as the 180-day audience (people who visited in the last 180 days), later for promotions (special sales offers).
