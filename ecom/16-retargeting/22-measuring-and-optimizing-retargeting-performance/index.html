Measuring and optimizing retargeting performance

Section 16.22 · 22 of 24 in this chapter · 4 min read

In short

  • Ads shown again to past visitors are called retargeting. Check them with ROAS, the money back for each dollar spent on ads.
  • Wait for enough people before you trust a result. Below 300 to 400 likely buyers, results look random.
  • Do not judge a campaign on one day's sales. Sales can arrive days later.
  • Test new visitors (cold audiences) against past visitors (retargeting). Do not assume past visitors are always cheaper.
  • If results are weak, check your store and your traffic before you change the audience.

Core KPIs

  • Retargeting (showing ads again to people who already visited your store) is measured with ROAS (return on ad spend, the money back for each unit spent on ads). ROAS is revenue from the ads divided by ad spend:
ROAS = revenue from the ads / ad spend
  • Facebook Dynamic Remarketing (ads that show people the exact products they looked at) and Google Remarketing should keep a conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) of at least 2.5% as a baseline (a starting target). These are the warmest audiences (people who already know your store). They should outperform cold traffic (people who have never visited your store).
  • Define business measurables (numbers that show how the business is doing) for each horizon (each time period you plan for): customer count, average order value (AOV, the average amount one order brings in), cost per acquisition (CPA, what it costs to win one new customer) or cost per sale, repeat purchase rate (the share of customers who buy again), and number of products sold.
  • Choose the measurables that directly drive revenue (money coming in) and profit figures.

Sample size and attribution caveats

Attribution means deciding which ad gets credit for a sale. Sample size means how many people are in your audience.

  • With fewer than 300-400 qualified people (people likely to buy) in the audience, results appear random. Larger audiences show clearer conversion patterns.
  • Retargeting audiences from Google and from Facebook or Instagram (both are ad platforms; Facebook and Instagram belong to Meta) can be built from site visitors. Include them when calculating the conversion rate for paid traffic (visitors who come from paid ads).
  • Traffic from days earlier can convert later through remarketing and abandoned cart emails (emails sent to people who left items in their cart without buying). Do not judge a campaign on the last day's orders alone.
  • Mobile shoppers (people shopping on a phone) often add products to the cart on a phone. They finish the purchase later on desktop (a computer). Retargeting brings them back. Mobile traffic should not be judged only by immediate sales.
  • If retargeting converts worse than cold-traffic campaigns in an account (your ad account), the result is unusual. Investigate it. Check audience size, lookback windows (how many past days count toward a sale), and tracking errors (broken measuring of sales).

Testing retargeting against cold audiences

  • Retargeting is often the cheapest audience, but this does not always hold. One advertiser found that a retargeting audience cost 10 to 20 percent more than a cold audience. The cold audience was made of similar businesses in a similar niche (a specific market segment) that competitors had not yet targeted. Test cold audiences against retargeting audiences rather than assuming.
  • Both results apply at once. Warm audiences should outperform cold traffic on conversion rate. But cost per result (what you pay for each sale or lead) must be tested, because retargeting can cost more than a cold audience.

  • The quality of the remarketing audience depends entirely on the quality of the traffic that creates it. The best remarketers (the people in the remarketing audience) are people who arrived through well-optimized cold traffic campaigns (ad campaigns tuned to bring in strong visitors).
  • Better cold traffic, such as traffic from Google Shopping (product ads in Google search), improves warm and hot retargeting results. Hot means the most interested visitors. Retargeting only works with the quality of the first visits that feed it.
  • Keep Meta ads running for remarketing at a small budget, even when they have not yet produced conversions. Meta is the company behind Facebook and Instagram. Remarketing only performs well when the initial traffic was qualified, so weak traffic from Google also weakens remarketing.

Learning from non-buyers

  1. Create a custom audience (a group of people you pick from your own data) of visitors who did not buy.
  2. Retarget them with a short video in which you ask why they did not buy and what would change their mind.
  3. Ask them to comment with the reason.
  4. Use the answers to improve the product, price or page, so more visitors convert.

Reviewing and improving ads on a schedule

  • Track remarketing ROAS and units sold (the number of products sold) as the retargeting KPIs. More organic visitors (people who find your store without paid ads) grow the remarketing list. Compare these numbers with the earlier month to see whether the growth is working.
  • Review results across accumulated days (the total over several days) rather than the last day's orders. Delayed conversions (sales that arrive late) from earlier traffic also count.

Scaling budgets

  1. Check that the audience is large enough for the result to mean something. Below 300-400 qualified people, results appear random.
  2. Check the return. A retargeting campaign should reach a ROAS of at least 10 (10x, meaning ten dollars back for each dollar spent) before its budget is increased.
  3. Keep the Meta remarketing budget small until conversions appear. Only increase it once the campaign has passed the ROAS threshold (the minimum ROAS target).
