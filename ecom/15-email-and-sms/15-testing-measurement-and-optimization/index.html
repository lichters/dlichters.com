Playbook › 15. Email and SMS

Testing, measurement and optimization

Section 15.15 · 15 of 17 in this chapter · 9 min read

In short

  • Test big changes in campaigns (one email sent to many people at once), not in automatic flows, which are slow to give answers.
  • Small automatic flows can take many months to show a result, so check ideas in campaigns first.
  • Change one thing at a time and write every test in a changelog (a written log of changes and results).
  • Read results only after the whole send is finished, and give each flow its own discount code so you know which one made the sales.

Where to Test: Campaigns or Flows

2 min
  • A campaign is one email sent to a whole list of people at the same time. A flow is an automatic email series that starts when a customer does something, such as leaving items in a cart.
  • Run major tests in campaigns instead of flows. A campaign sends one version to a large list at once. This gives the volume (number of people) needed for a reliable decision. Campaign tests also target differences large enough to show up statistically, meaning clear enough to be real and not just chance.
  • Typical differences worth testing in a campaign. Each one compares two versions of one element:
    • CTA (call to action, the button that tells the reader what to do) button color change
    • Lifestyle image (a photo of the product in use) versus product shot (a plain photo of the product)
    • Benefit-driven headline (says what the reader gains) versus curiosity-driven headline (makes the reader want to know more)
  • Low-volume flows, such as abandoned checkout (an email sent to a shopper who left before paying), can take many months before any decision is possible. Validate (check) ideas in campaigns first, then move them into flows.
  • Abandoned-cart sequences (a series of emails for shoppers who left items in their cart) can be split-tested (sent to two halves of the list, each half getting a different version) with different angles and hooks (the first line that grabs attention), even on a low-traffic store (a store with few visitors). Keep the flow structure that works and vary the messaging (the words and offers).
Test location Volume and time needed Units
Campaign to a large list (a big mailing list) 20,000 contacts at a 50/50 split (half to each version) gives 10,000 per variant (per version). A 50,000 to 100,000-contact list can reach roughly 50,000 per test in one send.
Abandoned-checkout flow About 1,000 per variant can take about six months, depending on the brand. Some brands never reach that volume. Around 2,000 abandoned carts in a test can take a full year.
Welcome series (the emails a new subscriber gets first) Very large volumes are needed, so results are slow. Plan for long test durations (long testing times) before acting on any result.

Test Design and Sequencing

2 min
  • Clone (copy) a working campaign. Change a few elements, such as the subject line (the title shown in the inbox) or the offer (the deal or discount), and send the copy to a different list. Comparing the two campaigns shows which version performs better.
  • Use the following sequence to move from campaign tests to flows:
    1. Run the split test as a campaign to a large list (for example 20,000 to 100,000 contacts) to reach a decisive sample size (enough people to trust the result).
    2. Choose the winning version and use it as the basis for the next campaign.
    3. Keep changing one proven element at a time and resending, so the template (the reusable email design and text) improves with each send.
    4. Once the template reliably generates revenue (money from sales) on every send, treat it as the proven template.
    5. Implement the proven winning ideas in automated flows and run the flow tests as A/B tests (two versions, each shown to half the audience).
    6. Because the ideas were already validated at scale (tested with many people), flow tests with lower volume are more likely to produce a winner.
  • Repeatedly sending a winning template keeps it as the baseline (the normal starting point you compare against) for future tests, which reduces guesswork. Flow changes then rest on campaign data rather than assumptions or long waiting periods.
  • Test almost every flow element, including timing, subject lines and offers, in your own account with your own customers. Treat any source that presents email flow advice as absolute rules with skepticism (doubt).
  • Small-batch variant testing, a direct-mail procedure (a test using printed letters sent by post):
    1. Write several letter variants with different headlines and offers.
    2. Mail each variant to a small batch of prospects (possible customers), such as 1,000 prospects per variant across 25 variants.
    3. Measure cost per response (what it costs to get one reply) or per customer for each variant.
    4. Use only the best-performing letter for the full mailing.

Baselines and Changelog Tests

1 min
  • Log every test in a changelog (a written list of changes and results) with its date, the change made, the expected result, the baseline, and the result. Record the result even if it is entered after the test end date.
  • Worked example, a sender profile picture test on Shopify Email (the email tool built into Shopify). The sender profile picture is the picture shown with the sender's name:
    • Change: on 15 November, the sender profile picture was replaced with an animated GIF (a short moving picture) instead of a static image (a still picture).
    • Expected result: open rate (the share of people who open the email) up by 5 percentage points (the gap between two percentages, so 45% to 50% is 5 points).
    • Baseline: 90-day trailing open rate of 45% (the open rate averaged over the last 90 days), so the target was an open rate above 50%.
    • Record whether the open rate stayed at 45%, rose to 50%, or rose further.
  • The sender profile picture is the small avatar (small picture) that Gmail shows next to the sender name in the inbox. Replacing a static avatar with an animated GIF is a test you can run on Shopify Email to see whether it moves the open rate.
  • The two sources for the same test state the goal differently. Use the one that matches how you write your target. Absolute means adding 5 points to 45%. Relative means adding 5% of 45%, which is 2.25 points.

Format and Copy Tests

1 min
  • Test plain text (an email with no design, only words) against HTML formatting (a designed email with images and layout) with your own audience and market. Measure click-through rate (the share of readers who click a link) as the main metric (a number you track). Keep whichever format performs better, since there are very few universal truths (rules that always work) in email marketing.
  • Improve through continuous sending. Email performance improves through sending regularly and measuring results over time. Refine your brand's tone (the way your brand sounds) and offer style through testing, since each market responds differently.
  • Review the emails before changing the audience when email traffic (visits that come from email) or email sales are weak. Check the following, then test other angles (different approaches):
    • Whether the right emails go to the right people
    • Whether the message fits
    • Whether recipients are engaged enough to buy (interested enough)
    • Whether they are at the right stage of their buying journey (how close they are to buying)

Frequency and Engagement Metrics

1 min
  • Test frequency (how often you send) over time to find the point where results plateau (stop improving). Track four metrics (numbers to watch) to decide whether the frequency is right:
    • Unsubscribes (people who leave the list)
    • Open rate
    • Click-through rate
    • Conversion rate (the share of readers who buy)
  • Test each segment's (each group of subscribers) performance using audience breakdowns (reports that split results into groups) to see which recency windows (how recently people engaged) and engagement thresholds (the level of activity needed) convert best. Then adjust future sends accordingly.
  • Review campaign performance by audience breakdown on contact age. This means groups such as days engaged buckets (groups by how many days contacts have been engaged), for example 0-14, 15-30, 31-60 and 31-90. Do not look only at the overall open rate.
  • A steady decline across older buckets is an early sign of deliverability (whether emails actually reach the inbox) or list-quality problems.
  • Example of open rate by bucket:

Reading Results by Inbox Provider and Domain

1 min
  • Campaign performance is found under Campaigns > [campaign] > Deliverability. It shows Performance by inbox provider (the email company that hosts the inbox) and Performance by domain (the part of the address after the @).
  • Audience breakdown, with an Export CSV option (a button that saves the data as a spreadsheet file), sits next to it under Audience breakdown. The campaign list shows Open rate, Click rate, Successfully Paid (paid orders) and Revenue columns.

Judging Campaigns After the Full Send

1 min
  • Do not judge a campaign while it is still sending. Campaigns in progress show queued recipients (people whose email is waiting to go out), for example 1,018 still queued. Wait until the send has finished before reading the open rate.
  • Campaigns can be scheduled to send at each recipient's local time (their own time zone), so the send is spread across several hours. Judge results only after the full send window (the whole sending period) has passed.

Attribution and Reporting

1 min
  • Attribution means tracing which email caused a sale. Use a different discount code (a code customers type at checkout to get a discount) per flow so you can see which flow produces the sales. Otherwise cart recovery and welcome offers cannot be separated in reporting.
  • The Shopify Messaging dashboard (the summary screen in Shopify's messaging area) shows, for a chosen date range (a period you pick):
    • Number of emails sent
    • Email open rate
    • Conversion rate
    • Number of sales generated by the emails

Flow Audits

1 min
  • Need at least 30 days of Klaviyo send data (records of emails sent in Klaviyo, an email tool), read through the Klaviyo MCP (Model Context Protocol, a link that lets an AI tool read Klaviyo's data). The audit (a full check) produces an audit report and a reporting calendar. The /email-draft-review command supports the review.
  • Klaviyo's preview (a test view of the email) does not make discount links clickable, so you cannot test them from there. Copy the real link and open it in a customer-style browser session (a private window that acts like a shopper) to confirm the code applies at checkout (the payment step).

Claims to Treat with Care

1 min
  • A weekly-design newsletter (a regular email to subscribers) reported open rates above 70%. The seller called this a strong engagement signal (a sign people are interested) for upselling (selling a bigger or extra product). It describes one newsletter and is not a general target.

Common Mistakes

1 min
  • Testing directly in low-volume flows, such as abandoned checkout, can take many months before any decision is possible.
  • Judging a campaign while it is still sending, before queued recipients have been delivered.
  • Testing discount links in the preview, which does not make them clickable.
  • Accepting flow advice as absolute rules instead of testing it in your own account.
