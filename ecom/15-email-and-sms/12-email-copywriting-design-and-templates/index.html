Email copywriting, design and templates

Section 15.12 · 12 of 17 in this chapter · 44 min read

In short

  • Every email needs one call to action (the single thing you want the reader to do). Do not add several.
  • The email's job is to get the click. The full sales talk goes on the website.
  • Write the subject line and the body copy separately.
  • Use a button for the main action, and text of at least 14 point (point = unit of text size).
  • Add a backup name like 'there' so an empty first name never leaves a blank greeting.
More key points
  • Start with text-led emails and only a few images. Plain emails are more likely to reach the main inbox.
  • Before launch, replace every placeholder and send a test of each email in the flow.
  • Do not design emails yourself unless you have real design skill. Use reusable blocks instead.
  • Keep the same design in every email so readers know who is writing.
  • Each email has one job: get the reader to click through to the website.
  • Keep all brand files in one permanent project. Files in a one-off chat disappear when it closes.
  • Build in order: brand rules first, then email templates, then campaigns.
  • Set up the workspace once, and always start new chats inside the project.
  • Never paste an email as one raw code block. Keep it editable in Klaviyo.
  • Test every template on phones, in dark mode and in major email services before you use it.

Copy Structure and Purpose

2 min
  • An email has two parts: the subject line (the title the reader sees first) and the body copy (the main text). Write and improve each one separately.
  • Marketing emails that aim to get the reader to visit the site need copywriting (persuasive writing). Their purpose is to push the reader toward one specific action.
  • Direct selling emails start from one core problem of the target market (the group of buyers you sell to). The product is positioned to solve that problem.
  • Named structures for writing these emails include Problem-Agitate-Solution (name the problem, make it hurt, offer the fix), the Bencivenga persuasion equation (a persuasion formula) and AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action).
  • The body copy of a marketing email has one job: generate clicks to a destination (the page the link opens).
  • The body copy must not sell inside the inbox (the reader's email app).
  • Persuasion, full proof and purchase decisions belong on the website. The website has room for images, videos and complete copy.
  • If an email tries to make the full sales case, readers who are not yet convinced do not click.
  • The store then loses the chance to convert them (turn them into buyers) on the site.
  • Send traffic straight to the website, where the full picture can be shown.
  • Do not answer the question or problem raised in the email.
  • Tell the reader they will learn the answer through the click. Otherwise there is no reason to take the action.
  • Email shares many similarities with content (articles, guides, posts).
  • Knowing customers' experiences, problems and pain points (problems that hurt) lets email address them more effectively.
  • Email marketing built on this research communicates more precisely with the customer.
  • The strategies that work in email are rooted in direct mail marketing (letters and postcards sent by post).
  • These strategies have stayed consistent for decades because human psychology has not changed.
  • Tested direct mail principles can therefore be applied to email copy and sequencing (the order of your emails).
  • Treat every email as a potential reusable template (a ready-made pattern you can copy).
  • Once an email is written, reuse it across other flows instead of writing from scratch. A flow is an automatic email series.
  • Reusing saves significant time.
  • Message matters more than format. Postage cost and fancy stationery do not increase response.
  • The offer and the opening sentences do increase response.

Persuasion Principles

1 min

The six power reasons customers buy:

  1. Ownership: use language and lifestyle images (photos of the product in everyday life) to make them feel they already own it. Use the car salesman technique: give control and choice.
  2. Logic: identify the right customer, answer why it is relevant, and confirm it solves their specific problem.
  3. Social Proof (other people's trust signals): include testimonials (written praise from customers), reviews, ratings and credible endorsements (recommendations from trusted people).
  4. Scarcity and Urgency (making the offer feel rare and time-limited): use a limited-time offer, limited stock language, show others buying, and highlight the cost of inaction (what the reader loses by doing nothing).
  5. Reciprocity (people want to give back when they receive something): frame the offer as a gift (free shipping, discount), create obligation, and provide value first.
  6. Status: identify the status the customer wants (luxury, eco-friendly, athletic) and position the product to align with and elevate that status.

Order of Work

1 min
  • Decide the call to action (CTA) before writing the body copy.
  • Typical CTAs in a copy email are learning more about a product, reading a blog post or downloading a lead magnet (a free download given in return for an email address).
  • Write the body copy after the subject line.
  • The body copy must follow on from the subject line's promise.
  • Together they should create curiosity. The body reveals only enough to make the reader want more.

Body Copy Formula

1 min

Email body formula for e-commerce copy emails, in order:

  1. Greet the reader and confirm the problem that the CTA will help them solve.
  2. Tease the solution, which is the product's primary promise (main promise) or the answer the content provides.
  3. Explain at least one benefit of taking the CTA.
  4. Offer the CTA.
  5. Sign off.
  • After the subject line, briefly name the primary value or benefit the reader gets by taking the CTA.
  • Do not explain the whole content in the email.
  • For a content CTA, one primary benefit is usually enough.
  • If there are several, list up to three primary benefits. Keep each one short, because the content itself delivers the detail.
  • For a product CTA, focus the email on the product's primary promise.
  • If the product copy already has a clear primary promise, reuse that promise in the email.
  • Open the email body with a short greeting that also introduces the sender. The reader then knows who is writing before the problem is named.
  • Confirm the problem by pointing to how often customers ask about it. For example, say the store receives many emails or questions on the topic.
  • This makes the problem feel recognised and real before the solution is teased.
  • Name the destination in the email, such as a blog post, guide, product page or download.
  • This avoids surprising readers when the page asks for something other than a simple read. This matters most when the link leads to an opt-in download (the reader agrees to receive something by giving their email).

Length

1 min
  • A copy email for e-commerce should be a few hundred words in four or five short paragraphs.
  • Longer formats are used by digital product, consulting and coaching businesses. Typical e-commerce stores that send traffic to a full site do not need them.
  • A complete copy email follows the problem, solution, benefit and CTA formula. With two benefits and one guide link, it can run about 145 words.

Body Copy Checklist

1 min

Body copy format checklist, in order:

  1. Use a minimum font size of 14 point or larger.
  2. Use short, simple sentences in easy-to-read language.
  3. Use short paragraphs of one to two lines, and single-line paragraphs are common.
  4. Make the CTA or links stand out.
  5. Use only one CTA per email.
  6. Use a personalised signature.
  • Sign emails with a real person's name, either the owner or a team member.
  • Do not sign with a generic business name or the word "Team".
  • Emails that feel personal, from a person to a person, tend to get better response rates.

One CTA, Placed in Several Spots

1 min
  • Focus most emails on exactly one call to action.
  • Mixing several offers or links in one email reduces the response to each of them.
  • Several different CTAs overwhelm readers, lower interaction and often lead them to ignore the email and not return to it.
  • Adding many calls to action to one email reduces the number of clicks for each one.
  • Avoid daily-deal style emails (emails stuffed with many offers) unless that is actually your business model.
  • Several clickable elements in one email are acceptable if they all point to the same CTA and the same content.
  • Multiple different CTAs to different content should be avoided.
  • Place the one CTA in several spots that all lead to the same page:
    • a link within the sentence that names the topic,
    • a clear click instruction in the middle,
    • a link or button near the end.
  • These are multiple clickable areas for a single action, not several different offers.

Button or Text Link

1 min
  • Use a button rather than a plain text link for the main call to action.
  • Research cited here indicates that buttons improve click-through rates (clicks per reader) and action.
  • A CTA can be a clickable button or a standard underlined text link in a different colour, such as "click here to view".
  • Test both styles, since neither is proven to be better in general.
  • The two options differ. The button is the recommended default. The text link is an accepted alternative.
  • When you cannot tell which works better for your list, run both as a test (an A/B test: two versions, see which wins).
  • The call-to-action button must use a unique colour with strong contrast against the rest of the email. Examples: bright red, bright green or bright blue.
  • Any colour works, as long as the button clearly stands out.

Pattern: Problem, Guide, Two Benefits, One CTA

1 min

Pattern:

  • A curiosity-driven subject line.
  • A greeting.
  • One sentence that names a common buying problem.
  • A sentence saying there is confusing information online.
  • A sentence promising a guide or resource that settles the question.
  • Two benefits the reader gets from clicking.
  • One repeated CTA with a clear link or button.

Why it works: the reader sees a problem they recognise, a promise of an answer and a single clear next step. So the click is the only logical action.

  • Example A (running shoes, subject: "Flat feet, which shoe?"): "Hi Sam, choosing running shoes with flat feet is a big decision. Advice online contradicts itself on stability versus cushioning, so we wrote a short guide. Click here to see which features matter most and which model our fitters recommend for your stride."
  • Example B (mattresses, subject: "Firm or soft, which is right for your back?"): "Hi Lee, picking a mattress for back pain is not something to rush. There are many mixed opinions about firm and soft, so we put together a simple comparison. Click here to see the pros and cons of each and which one our sleep team suggests for back sleepers."

Pairing a Discount With a Product

1 min
  • Pair the discount with one specific product suggestion in a single sentence. This links the saving to a second purchase.
  • This turns the coupon into a reason to add to the basket (shopping cart), rather than just a price cut.
    • Example 1: "Your 15% covers a second pair of socks, so add the matching cap while you are here."
    • Example 2: "Use your 15% on the refill pack and the saving pays for a travel size of the same cream."

Writing Process and Scannable Style

1 min
  • Keep emails easy to scan (skim quickly). Use one or two sentences per line, lots of white space (empty space) and no long paragraphs.
  • Readers scan first and read only what hooks them.
  • Short lines keep attention on the story and offer.
  • Use short, choppy sentences and short paragraphs of one to two lines.
  • Writing process for emails:
    1. Write the basic structure of the email first, including the story beats (the key moments of the message) and the offer link.
    2. Add the juicy details, emotional hooks (words that trigger feeling) and personality afterwards.
    3. If writing is slow, dictate ideas into a phone recorder and send the recording to someone for transcription (turning speech into text).
  • Email body text for flows can be drafted with ChatGPT, but the draft still needs checking.
  • Before going live, confirm the links point to the correct checkout and product pages.
  • Also confirm that the wording matches the offer.
  • Small grammar errors are acceptable in emails.
  • Small errors can make the character more human and relatable.
  • Do not delay sending because of fear of mistakes. Consistent sending matters more than perfect text.
  • The flaw is part of the bond.
  • Keep a separate swipe file (a collection of saved examples) of emails collected from competitors and from marketers in other niches.
  • Use it as a source of ideas when you lack inspiration for your own email content. Do not copy it directly.

Font Size and Readability

1 min
  • Set the email body font to at least 14 point (pt = point, a unit of text size).
  • Automatic sizes of 12 or 10 point that many email tools produce are too small and hard to read for some people.
  • Body copy should use a minimum font size of 18 pt, with up to 22 pt acceptable.
  • Bigger text is generally easier to read.
  • The two figures conflict. Use 18-22 pt for body copy where you can.
  • Treat 14 pt as the floor (the lowest allowed size) for copy emails.

Personalisation and Dynamic Content

1 min
  • Email content can use personalisation variables (placeholders that insert a reader's own details), such as {{firstName}}.
  • Use a fallback value (a backup word used when the detail is missing), like 'Friend', for customers without a stored first name.
  • Personalise the greeting and subject line with the first name.
  • Adding the subscriber's first name makes the email feel more personal.
  • Add a default such as 'there' to every personalised greeting and subject line.
  • Otherwise an empty first name in the profile leaves an empty greeting.
  • Personalisation in Klaviyo (an email tool; its template code is called Liquid) for the greeting and the subject line:
Greeting: Hey {{ first_name|default:'there' }},
Cart subject line: Still thinking it over, {{ first_name|default:'there'|title }}?

Use the fallback in both places, so an empty first name in the profile does not show up as a blank greeting.
  • The two fallback values differ. (,, )
  • 'Friend' is the general personalisation fallback for variables.
  • 'there' is the greeting and subject fallback used in the Klaviyo flow templates. (, )
  • Use 'Friend' for general content variables.
  • Use 'there' where the greeting reads "Hey there,".
  • The placeholder syntax (the exact code format) also differs between sources.
  • Klaviyo uses {{ first_name }}. ( shows it without a fallback, although requires one.)
  • Generic text uses {first_name} and {discount_code}, which you replace yourself.
  • Placeholder mapping in the flow templates:
    • {{ first_name }} and [NAME TAG] take the subscriber's first name from the Klaviyo profile.
    • [DISCOUNT CODE] takes the discount code of that flow.
    • [BUTTON] is a call-to-action button linking to the store.
    • [CART] is the block listing the products left in the abandoned cart (a basket left unpaid).
    • [BEST SELLERS] is a product block of top sellers.
    • [REVIEWS] is a block of customer reviews.
    • [TIMER] is a countdown.
    • [SIGNATURE] is the sender sign-off.
  • Dynamic content (content that changes for each reader) may not appear fully in email previews.
  • Check the preview of each campaign before sending.

Claims, Accuracy and Launch Checks

1 min
  • Vague superlatives are big claims with no proof, such as "150% more awesome." Made-up numbers count too. Remove them unless they are real. Replace them with a specific benefit you can check. Only mention a guarantee that the store really offers in its return policy (the written rules for returns and refunds).
  • Blanks (______), placeholders such as [NAME TAG], [BUTTON], [REVIEWS] or [DISCOUNT CODE] are stand-in text. If you leave them in before launch, customers see them. Check a test send of every email in the flow (a flow is an automatic series of emails) before you switch it on.
  • An email inside a flow that is still in draft status (saved, not active) is not sent. You can keep an unwanted email safe by leaving it in draft.
  • Before you use a bought email template, check it on mobile (phones) and in every email app (email client, such as Gmail or Outlook). Designs can look good on a marketplace page and still break in real inboxes.

Plain Text vs HTML

2 min

HTML is the code that builds an email's layout. Plain text is an email with no layout code, just words.

Positions in the Sources

Position Source When it applies
HTML is preferred over plain text, though the gap matters less than it used to. HTML allows clickable buttons and a logo, so use it, but keep the layout simple. When you need buttons and a logo.
Simple text-based emails are preferred for small and medium e-commerce stores. HTML remains useful when images must be shown. Small and medium stores; HTML when images must be shown.
Write emails mostly as plain text with few or no images. The wording sells, and many email clients do not display images. Default writing mode.
Plain text emails that look like a message from a friend can perform well, even compared with heavily designed templates. Any list used for many offers.
For a new store, start with text-led emails that use a few images, since they are more likely to reach the primary inbox. Compare open and click results in a test rather than relying on taste. New stores; test to decide.
  • Heavily designed HTML emails are linked to promotional mailings. People do not usually write like that to each other.
  • Text emails with a few images are more likely to land in the primary inbox (the main inbox). Image-heavy emails from the start risk the Promotions tab (Gmail's folder for marketing mail), where people open them less.
  • Heavy HTML may not display well in every inbox, device or email client. Some evidence suggests it is more likely to go to the Promotions tab or to spam (the junk folder).
  • Big stores often send three or four heavily designed HTML emails a day, full of offers. The author does not think this is the best approach for small and medium businesses.
  • A simple email with one CTA (call to action: the one thing you want the reader to do) does not need elaborate design. There is no evidence that heavy design gets more clicks (click-through rate: the share of readers who click a link). Some evidence points the other way.
  • Too many decorations, images and colours is a mistake. Email providers may strip out part of the code, and the layout can break on some devices. The email may then look broken or make less sense.
  • Build layouts with HTML and CSS (CSS is the code that sets colours, fonts and spacing). Do not use image banners (one big picture holding the text). Images can be blocked, so the layout must work without them.
  • Include links to a few website categories in most emails. Readers who do not care about the email topic may still click through to the shop.
  • Email HTML is not normal web HTML. It still relies largely on 1990s-era techniques. Each email host renders the same design differently. For example, Gmail, Yahoo and Hotmail show the same design in different ways.
  • Design for the lowest common denominator, meaning the simplest setup that works everywhere. Learn what each email client supports and does not support before you build.

Images, Colour and Visual Layout

2 min

Klaviyo is the email software used in these examples.

  • Use images sparingly. Choose only the best product images, such as the product itself or the product in use. Many images raise the risk of spam placement and of formatting being stripped. Heavy HTML can look like scam email. A product email benefits from one product image. Many email types need no images at all.
  • There is no reliable evidence that emails with images perform better than text-only emails. Add an image only when it serves the message, such as a product photo. Choose your best product images, not decorative graphics.
  • A picture and short text can be combined into one image. For example, a collage of customer review screenshots can be made in Canva (an online design tool). Place it as an image block (a single piece of an email) in a Klaviyo email. In abandoned cart emails (emails sent to people who left items in their basket), this adds social proof (signs that other people trust you, such as reviews) without making the whole email an image.
  • Include pinnable product images, so subscribers can save products to Pinterest (an image-saving site) when they are not ready to buy. This keeps the product visible and gives the customer a place to return to.
  • Put the text on a white background so it is easy to read. A text column with a coloured background on each side is fine, as long as the area behind the text stays white.
  • In a campaign email (a one-off email sent to a list), white is a clean base for product emails. Set the background colour in the editor's styling options. Add offers, text blocks and product feeds on top of it.
  • Limit colours to your brand colours, usually one or two main colours already used on the website. Use them when you need colour in an email.
  • Use one email template for all emails, so subscribers see a message and know right away that it comes from your brand.
  • Consistent design builds on trust that subscribers already have. Changing styles and layouts from email to email creates doubt about who is sending. Keep the design recognisable across all emails.
  • Keep email design simple. Most results come from strong copy, a clear message and an easy-to-spot call to action.

Modular Templates and Content Blocks

Building Blocks

  • Email modules work like building blocks (ready-made pieces you can reuse). Stack them in any order inside an email. The same modular approach works for both campaigns and flows.
  • A content block or module is one part of an email. A template is the full email built from those parts. A shared set of blocks and templates keeps campaigns and flows consistent.
  • Build a set of reusable blocks instead of one custom-designed email. You can then use the same blocks for every campaign and flow you send later.
  • Some modules, such as M19, are universal. Nearly every brand can use them. Build these first in a module library (a saved collection of modules).
  • With a ready module library, done-for-you clients (clients who pay you to build everything) can build an email in three to five minutes. They do not design each email from scratch.
  • All modules are tested on mobile and in dark mode (a display setting with a dark background). Emails built from them show correctly without extra checks. Building from tested blocks gives quick, consistent results.
  • The always-on programme covers both campaigns and automated flows. It should stay editable in your email platform. Sliced-image designs (a design cut into picture slices) look attractive but cannot grow into flows, and they break in real inboxes.

Design Method Comparison

3 min

Email design methods are compared on two things:

  • Programme fit: how well the method suits running email at scale. It covers editability (how easy it is to change later), render safety (whether it looks right everywhere) and scalability (whether it grows into flows).
  • Design wow factor: how impressive it looks.

Higher is better on programme fit. Higher on the right means more wow factor. Every method involves trade-offs, so no single option wins on every point.

  • The same comparison applies to other email platforms, such as Omnisend. The tool name changes. The trade-offs stay the same.

Marketplaces such as Envato and ThemeForest sell ready-made designs. Figma is a design tool for layouts. BeFree is a third-party tool for email design. Klaviyo Composer is a new Klaviyo design helper, here in alpha (an early test version).

Method Strength Weakness
Self-design in Canva or Figma Full control if you have real graphic and email design skill Easily looks amateur, can damage the brand, often abandoned after weeks or months
Bought template (Envato, ThemeForest, similar marketplaces) Quick start, can look good on paper Often made by graphic designers, may be image-based, mobile and cross-client rendering often unverified
Third-party tool (BeFree, later renamed LGE Studio) Polished, modular templates Two-tool workflow, pushed to Klaviyo as one large HTML block, every edit needs a re-push
Professional designer file (Figma or Canva) Pleasing, professional look Delivered as locked images, text can be changed but layout and module order cannot, does not scale into flows
Klaviyo native editor Editable, render-safe, repeatable Needs patience and skill to produce polished design
Klaviyo Composer (alpha at time of lesson) Expected to allow better native, editable Klaviyo designs Still in alpha, full extent unknown
Modular system with reusable content blocks On-brand, editable, scalable for campaigns and flows Less polished than a bespoke designer look
  • Only design emails yourself in Canva or Figma if you have professional graphic and email design skills. If you lack them, avoid this route entirely.
  • Graphic design and email design are different skills. Email design depends on specific psychology and on where modules go. A graphic designer may not know this.
  • Founders who design their own emails without the right skills often spend weeks or months on it, then give up for another route. Self-designed emails can also do real damage to the brand.
  • BeFree, later renamed LGE Studio, is a third-party tool for building modular email templates and pushing them to Klaviyo. The result is one large HTML block. Every change means going back to the tool and pushing again.
  • Professional designer files in Figma or Canva are usually locked images. Buyers can often change the text but not the layout, and the blocks do not stack in any order.
  • The Klaviyo native editor (Klaviyo's own builder) makes editable, render-safe emails. It takes patience and design skill to make the result look polished.
  • Klaviyo Composer was in alpha. It was expected to release publicly within about one month of when this was written. It is meant to help design better emails natively in Klaviyo while staying editable and scalable.
  • The modular system trades some visual polish for on-brand consistency, editability and scalability. It suits operators who send many emails and value speed over a custom look.
  • The modular email system is also a two-tool workflow, like a third-party tool setup. Its main advantage is reusable content blocks and scaling across campaigns. It is not a single-tool process.
  • The modular system is rated the most widely rendered option of the methods compared. It should work on mobile and in dark mode. It is chosen for scalability first, with polish second.
  • On programme fit, a modular system built with an AI approach and Klaviyo's native editor scores about equal. The sliced-image designer route may look more impressive. But it breaks in real inboxes and cannot grow into flows.
  • Choose a professional designer if a fully custom look is the top priority, and you accept locked, non-editable files. Choose the modular system if editability, consistent branding and scaling across campaigns and flows matter most.

Email Body Copy and Calls to Action

1 min

  • an email has one job, which is to get the reader to visit the website. Trying to close the sale inside the message misses that job. The purchase decision belongs to the site.

  • Length matters less than whether each paragraph keeps the reader interested. Long copy can beat short copy when the reader is already interested. He will read several pages to learn more.

  • A short promotional email that produced a single tracked result used this structure:

    1. The headline is used as the subject line.
    2. Two or three one-line payoff sentences follow.
    3. One big promise.
    4. One quote from an authority figure (an expert or well-known person).
    5. One link, as the only call to action.

    The email showed no product details, so readers had to click to learn more. In that tracked campaign, roughly 11 out of every 100 recipients clicked. This is one result to watch, not a guaranteed benchmark.

  • Place social proof right beside each unique value proposition (USP: what makes your offer different), such as a review, a bestseller or a customer story. Claims alone persuade less than claims shown with evidence.

  • Remove text that repeats the header, such as a brand name next to a logo. This keeps the email shorter and easier to scan.

  • Compose.ai is a tool for writing marketing emails. It helps when a store needs many email drafts without hiring a copywriter.

Modular content blocks and master template

  • Universal content blocks are reusable email building pieces. A set of them, with a master template (one shared base design), lets the same pieces power welcome flows, campaigns and one-off sends.
    • A welcome flow is an automatic email series for new subscribers (people on your email list).
    • A campaign is a one-time email to a list.
    • Templates built this way stay consistent across all sends.
  • Universal content blocks are made with the email template builder skill. It needs these inputs:
    • Two reference images: an anatomy image and a module library image.
    • brand-kit.json, your brand kit saved as a JSON file (a plain text file with a fixed structure).
    • The output is a set of blocks and a master template. These are pushed into Klaviyo.
  • Once universal content blocks exist in Klaviyo, new emails are fast to build. Just swap images and reorder a few modules. Every send looks consistent.
  • After the foundation stage (universal content blocks and master template), you can stop and use working templates. These cover sales postcards (short promotional emails), product showcases and editorial emails (emails that read like a magazine article).
    • Later stages only add flows (automatic email series), audits (checks) and segmentation (sorting subscribers into groups).

    • Raw HTML cannot be edited in Klaviyo.
    • To change it, you must export it again.
    • Push templates and universal content blocks as structures that stay editable in Klaviyo.
