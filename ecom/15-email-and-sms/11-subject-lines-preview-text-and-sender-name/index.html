Subject lines, preview text and sender name

Section 15.11 · 11 of 17 in this chapter · 10 min read

In short

  • Your subject line has one job: get the reader to open the email. The body does the selling.
  • Write the subject line first. It decides whether anyone reads the rest.
  • Rotate your tactics. Overusing one wears it out, and fake deadlines and spam words make people stop opening.
  • Keep it short, specific, and written to one person, like a message to a friend.

Competitor research

1 min
  • Competitor email research procedure:
    1. Sign up to many email lists (mailing lists businesses send to) from competing and non-competing businesses.
    2. Collect screenshots (saved pictures of the screen) of their inbox (the list of emails a person receives) showing subject lines, sender names, and send times.
    3. Note which subject lines catch your attention and why.
    4. Use those observations to shape your own subject lines.
  • Subscribe to the lists of the businesses in your matrix (your comparison table of competitors).
  • This shows what content they send and how they talk to their audience.
  • Use a separate Gmail account (a free email account from Google) for this.
  • Check it once a month for 20 to 30 minutes.

Purpose of the Subject Line

1 min
  • The subject line has one job: get the reader to notice the email and open it.
  • It does not need to sell or convince.
  • The body copy (the main text of the email) handles persuasion.
  • The call to action (the request that tells the reader what to do next) is also handled by the body.
  • The open rate is the share of readers who open your email.
  • Inbox providers (email companies such as Gmail and Hotmail) can flag your domain (your web address, such as yourshop.com) as spam when open rates are low.
  • Low engagement (few people opening or clicking) can hurt deliverability (whether your emails reach the inbox at all) for your entire list.
  • This harm is not limited to the people who did not open.
  • Subject lines work with two parts: attention and curiosity.
  • Attention makes the email stand out in crowded inboxes.
  • Curiosity makes the reader want to open it.
  • Both are essential.
  • A subject line must stand out in a crowded inbox.
  • It must be compelling (strongly interesting), urgent (needing action now), or curious.
  • It can combine more than one of these qualities.

Writing Order

1 min
  • Write the subject line first, before the body copy.
  • It is the first thing a reader sees.
  • So it decides whether the rest of the email gets read.

Subject Line Formulas

5 min

A formula is a fill-in pattern you can reuse.

  • Question:
    • Question lines trigger curiosity and fear of missing out (FOMO, the worry of missing something good).
    • Common formats: 'Did you know this?', 'Do you know how to [benefit]?', 'Have you ever made this mistake?', 'Can you afford to miss out on this?'
    • Keep them short and cryptic (mysterious).
    • The email body must deliver genuine value on what the subject line promises.
    • Otherwise, future open rates will fall.
  • How-to:
    • How-to lines appeal to the desire to gain skills and self-improve.
    • Examples: 'How to pick the perfect beginner surfboard', 'How to improve your complexion', 'How to bake a cake in five easy steps.'
    • They naturally boost engagement because everyone wants the proven method.
    • They are especially effective as top-of-funnel content (the first content that introduces people to your business).
    • This content introduces prospects (people who might buy) to your business.
  • Scarcity and urgency:
    • These lines pressure warm audiences (people who already want your products) by creating fear of missing out on limited deals.
    • Examples: 'Ends Midnight - 2 for 1 Wine Glasses', '50% Off Storewide till tomorrow 9pm', 'Free shipping on all orders (if you buy today).'
    • Always include a precise deadline.
    • Use scarcity only for genuine inventory limits (real stock limits), legitimately rare promotions, or real time constraints.
  • Offer and urgency formula:
    • Formula: benefit (free shipping, a percentage off, a bonus) plus a time limit (ends tomorrow, last day, this weekend).
    • Why it works: a benefit makes the email relevant to a buyer.
    • A deadline removes the option to postpone.
    • Example one: 'Ends tonight: free shipping on every order', with preview text (the short line shown after the subject line in the inbox) 'Your cart is waiting.'
    • Example two: 'Last chance: 15 percent off this weekend only', with preview text 'Use it before Sunday midnight.'
  • Declaration:
    • Declaration lines spotlight new products or breaking announcements to capitalize on novelty (make use of what is new).
    • Examples: 'Introducing Luxo premium dog kennels', 'NEW: primo chandeliers (just $549 today)', 'Available now: Thrustmaster T-LCM racing pedals (free shipping).'
    • Apply them to product launches, revived items with upgrades, refreshed versions, new content, videos, or downloadables (files people can download).
  • Number:
    • Numbers in subject lines significantly increase open rates compared with lines without them.
    • Numbers add clarity, precision, and intrigue (a sense of mystery).
    • Examples: '3 easy steps to choose a surfboard', 'The top 10 induction cooktops 2020', '5 ways that an e-bike will change you.'
    • Saying 'the top 10' outperforms just 'the top.'
    • Number formula: state a number and the keyword benefit (the keyword is the word people type when searching).
    • Examples: 'Five budget blenders for smoothies' or 'Three apps that schedule Instagram captions.'
    • It works because a number signals a finite (has an end), scannable (easy to skim) list.
    • It also works because the keyword matches what the reader searched for.
    • Keep the subject specific to the article it sends readers to.
  • Curiosity:
    • Curiosity lines withhold information (hold back the answer) to spark interest.
    • They are structured like questions but are sometimes statements.
    • Examples: 'The little known trick to paddling', 'The big mistake 99% of surfers make', 'You need to see this...'
    • They succeed because readers worry they are missing knowledge or repeating a common error.
    • Essential: deliver exactly what the line hints at.
    • Otherwise, trust and open rates are permanently damaged.
    • Curiosity formula: a short quoted claim or attributed statement (a quote or a statement credited to a person), followed by a secondary line.
    • The secondary line raises a question or promises an insider detail (a secret only insiders know).
    • Why it works: an unanswered question creates tension the reader wants to resolve.
    • Example one: 'The one thing our returns team never tells customers', with preview text 'Ask us this before you buy.'
    • Example two: 'Why we stopped stocking our bestseller', with preview text 'It wasn't what you think.'
  • Personalization:
    • Personalized lines insert the recipient's first name alongside a question or curiosity angle.
    • Examples: 'Jon, did you see this?', 'We've got a big surprise Jon', 'Jon, do you want to save 50%?'
    • Names stand out because personalization is rare in bulk email (sending the same email to many people at once).
    • Klaviyo, Drip, and Mailchimp (email tools) support this with shortcodes (short placeholder codes the tool fills in automatically) that auto-populate names.
    • Use it sparingly to maintain impact.
    • Personalization formula: open with a first-name token (a placeholder the tool swaps for the reader's first name).
    • Follow it with an offer that reads naturally with the name.
    • It works because a personal opening makes the message feel directed at the reader.
    • Check that the tool's fallback (the backup text used when no name is stored) works.
    • Subscribers (people on your list) without a stored name must not see an empty gap or a raw placeholder.
    • Example in the same pattern: 'Jordan, your spring picks are now on sale'.
  • Emoji (a small picture such as ⏰):
    • Formula: a clear message plus one emoji matched to its meaning, not a string of emojis.
    • Why it works: the emoji breaks up the visual pattern of plain text lines.
    • It also signals the type of message before the reader opens it.
    • Use an alarm clock for time pressure, a lightning bolt for speed, or a flash deal symbol.
    • Example one: 'Flash sale ends at midnight ⏰', with preview text 'Up to 30 percent off our full range.'
    • Example two: 'Your restock is here ⚡', with preview text 'Limited quantities, once they are gone they are gone.'
  • Welcome (source: Source C, not Source B):
    • Keep it short.
    • Name the gift or the welcome offer.
    • Avoid heavy sales words so the email reads like a personal message.
    • Examples: 'Your welcome discount is inside' and 'A thank-you gift for joining us.'
  • Follow-up after a full presentation:
    • Formula: [specific payoff with an amount] that could [pay or deliver the benefit] [specific next timing].
    • Use it for a follow-up email sent after a reader has watched the full presentation.
    • A concrete amount and timing signal a specific offer rather than a generic message.
    • Why it works: specific numbers and a near-term timeframe create curiosity.
    • They also make the benefit easy to picture.
    • Example one: 'The Refund Check That Could Return $480 by Friday'.
    • Example two: 'The Restock Alert That Could Save You $62 Before Tuesday'.

Length

1 min
  • Keep subject lines as short as possible.
  • They are cut off after a set number of characters (letters and spaces) on mobile devices (phones and tablets).
  • The exact cut-off depends on the device and email client (the app used to read email).
  • A cut-off subject line shows an incomplete message.
  • So take the time to craft short, punchy subject lines.

Tone

1 min
  • Write subject lines conversationally, as if writing to a friend rather than a crowd.
  • Use informal language.
  • Address a single person, not a group, even though many people receive the email.
  • At any moment, one person is reading the email.
  • Email is a personal communication tool, so keep that tone.

Spam Trigger Words

1 min
  • Avoid known spam trigger words (words that set off spam filters).
  • Inbox providers (Gmail, Hotmail, Yahoo) keep these words on spam lists as signs of promotional or spam emails.
  • Common triggers: money, make money, free, free inside.
  • Some autoresponders (tools that send automatic emails) check for these words and warn you.
  • Others do not.
  • If you use spam words, emails land in spam folders without you knowing.
  • This causes mysteriously low open rates.
  • Check your subject lines by hand against spam word lists before sending.
  • Trigger words can send emails to the spam folder or the Gmail promotions tab (the Gmail tab for marketing mail).
  • Keep a current published list of known spam trigger words in mind while writing each subject line.
  • Trigger words matter less for e-commerce (online shops) than for other business types.
  • They still need to be checked.

Capitalization and Emoji Tests

1 min
  • Test different capitalization styles: all lowercase, title case (first letter of each word capitalized), and variations.
  • Testing reveals what stands out most in your audience's inbox.
  • Test emojis in subject lines selectively.
  • Emojis appear in color in inbox lists.
  • This makes them stand out and increases the likelihood that recipients notice and read the line.
  • Do not use an emoji on every email.
  • Varying your approach preserves the tactic's effectiveness.
  • Use emojis only when they are genuinely relevant to the email content.

Over-Use and Fake Urgency Pitfalls

1 min
  • Overusing any single subject line tactic diminishes its effectiveness.
  • Tactics include questions, scarcity, personalization, or emojis.
  • Recipients grow accustomed to it, and open rates decline.
  • Rotate among different tactics to maintain impact.
  • Constant daily sends or fake scarcity claims train subscribers to ignore these emails entirely.
  • Faking scarcity destroys audience trust.
  • If you claim limited supply or deadlines that are not genuine, audiences quickly recognize the false pattern.
  • This is especially true if these claims recur constantly.
  • Audiences then permanently ignore these emails.
  • Overpromising in curiosity or question subject lines erodes trust.
  • If a line says 'The big mistake 99% of surfers make', the email must deliver a significant, widespread error.
  • It must not be a beginner-level or rare mistake.
  • Overhyping without delivery damages credibility for future campaigns (email sends).

Preview Text

1 min
  • Email inboxes display a short second line next to the subject line.
  • This line is often called preview text or preheader (another name for the same line).
  • Use it as a companion to the subject line.
  • Add a detail, a question, or a hint that supports the subject.
  • Do not repeat the subject.
  • The subject line and pre-header work as a pair.
  • The subject names the message (a gift, a reminder, a last chance).
  • The pre-header repeats the offer and the deadline in short form.
  • So the inbox preview shows both the reason to open and the value.
    • Example 1 for a 10% welcome offer: subject 'Your first order is waiting', pre-header '10% off your first order [valid 48 hours]'.
    • Example 2 for a saved cart: subject 'Your cart is almost gone', pre-header 'Your saved items plus 10% off [today only]'.
  • Reminder without a discount:
    • Use playful, self-aware humor (light joking about yourself) about the shopper hesitating.
    • Follow it with the saved item and one plain reason to return.
    • This lowers the pressure of a reminder without a discount.
    • Example 1: subject 'Your cart is still warm', pre-header 'Your picks are waiting, no pressure.'
    • Example 2: subject 'Hard to say goodbye to that jacket', pre-header 'The jacket is still in your cart.'
