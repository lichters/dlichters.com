Playbook › 15. Email and SMS

SMS marketing: compliance, opt-in and flows

Section 15.10 · 10 of 17 in this chapter · 26 min read

In short

  • Send few texts. Too many make people leave faster than too many emails do.
  • Get clear written permission before you send marketing texts. In the US, the TCPA law requires it.
  • Put a clear way to stop (opt out) in every text.
  • Check the timing rules for your country before you send the three cart-reminder texts.
  • SMS Pump only sends texts if your account balance is at least $20.
More key points
  • A win-back SMS brings back customers who have not bought for 30 days.
  • Each text needs a short brand name, one discount code, a link, and a way to stop messages ("Reply STOP").
  • Keep each text under 160 characters, or it splits into two messages.
  • Test SMS against email, and compare open rate and click rate before you do more with SMS.

When and How Much to Use SMS

2 min
  • Use SMS (text messages) sparingly. People get fewer marketing texts than emails, and frequent promotions quickly annoy them. Too many texts make people opt out (stop receiving messages) faster than too many emails do.
  • Use SMS mainly for valuable messages, such as order updates. Customers want these and can opt in (say yes to receive them) on Shopify (the online store software). Save promotional texts (texts that sell a deal) for rare, genuinely valuable offers.
  • Asking for SMS consent (permission to text) at checkout is fine if you might use text marketing later. Ask very sparingly. Frequent discount texts can damage the customer relationship more than they help.
  • Email is worth more in the long run than a phone number, because email lets you remarket (show messages again to people who visited your website). SMS works well when it is targeted (sent to the right person) at the right time. Still, use it sparingly.
  • Do not run ads that send visitors to an SMS sign-up page instead of your website. SMS sign-ups from paid traffic (visitors from paid ads) are not a recommended default.
  • Audience matters. Younger customers tend to accept text marketing more readily. Older customers may find frequent texts annoying. Test SMS with your audience before scaling it (making it bigger).
  • Collect a phone number from every customer, so SMS is available when it is valuable. Phone numbers are an asset for order updates and occasional offers.
  • Every SMS campaign should be an automated sequence (a series of messages sent automatically) set up early, with A/B testing (trying two versions to see which works better) built in. Never send one-off texts. Automation captures volume even before you have large subscriber lists (lists of people who opted in).
  • Two sources disagree on one-off sends. says never send one-off texts. describes a one-off SMS offer update as a single setting inside a Klaviyo (email and SMS software) flow step. Treat the one-off setting as a step inside a flow, not a standalone blast (one message sent to everyone).

Consent and Compliance

1 min

Compliance means following the law.

Legal Baseline by Market

1 min
  • In the US, marketing SMS sent to customers generally needs prior express written consent (clear written permission given before any message) under the TCPA (a US texting law). Every message must include a clear way to opt out. Confirm consent is captured at sign-up before running SMS flows in the US.
  • Before building an SMS opt-in (a sign-up for texts), verify the required consent wording for your jurisdiction (the country or region whose laws apply). In the US, TCPA compliance applies.
  • Check the SMS rules and GDPR (an EU privacy law) compliance of every target country before sending texts. Phone consent is stricter than email consent.
  • Use an SMS app with built-in compliance features, such as GDPR settings and quiet hours (times when messages must not be sent). Make sure messages are sent only at permitted times.
  • Every SMS campaign must include an unsubscribe option (a way to stop receiving messages).
  • Avoid SMS providers (the companies that send your texts) that use shared sender numbers, because your messages may come from a number shared with unrelated companies. Choose a provider with a dedicated number (one number only for your store).

Opt-Out in Every Message

1 min
  • Include opt-out instructions in every SMS. Sending without them exposes the store to legal and carrier (phone network) complaints.
  • Every marketing SMS sent through SMS Bump (an SMS app) should include opt-out instructions, such as a reply STOP line (a line telling people to reply STOP). Leaving them out creates compliance risk and hurts customer trust.
  • In SMS Pump (another SMS app), always include opt-out instructions and keep them turned on in the flow.
  • Opt-out and discount fields in SMS Pump: the discount code field must be filled in before a message is saved, and the opt-out text must have room.

Opt-In Disclosure

1 min

Place the disclosure (required fine print) directly under the phone number submit button (the button that sends the form). It must state:

  • that the visitor agrees to receive marketing texts,
  • The message frequency per month,
  • that message and data rates may apply (phone company charges may apply),
  • that consent is not a condition of purchase (the visitor does not have to say yes to buy).

Adapted from a public compliant SMS opt-in text:

By submitting this form, you agree to receive recurring automated marketing text messages (e.g. promotions and cart reminders) from [Brand Name] at the phone number provided. Consent is not a condition of purchase. Message frequency varies (about 3 to 4 messages per month). Message and data rates may apply. Reply HELP for help or STOP to cancel.

Prompt for drafting and checking opt-in language (a prompt is a ready-made instruction you paste into an AI tool):

You are an expert in marketing and SMS law. Help me write opt-in language for a form at the bottom of a submit button on a website. The form requires detailed opt-in language that includes marketing messages sent via SMS, about 3 to 4 messages per month, and automated messages. When crafting the opt-in, be transparent and compliant: ask for consent, state that consent is not a condition of purchase, state the number of messages per month, and state that message and data rates may apply. Then check my draft against the SMS disclosure language published by a major email/SMS platform and tell me where it differs.
  • Postscript (an SMS app) has list growth tools (Acquisitions > List growth tools > Convert your email list). They show ready-made mobile opt-in code with TCPA-style consent language (wording that follows the US texting law). Use it as a wording reference only. Check it against your own SMS provider's requirements instead of copying from a live client account.

Collecting Phone Numbers

1 min
  • Collect phone numbers from abandoned checkouts (customers who left before paying) and purchases, not through cold pop-ups (surprise boxes on a website), because phone numbers are personal. An incentive, such as an extra 5% off, can encourage the visitor to give the number.
  • Collect phone numbers at checkout, where buyers already enter them. Add a second pop-up step after email signup that offers an extra 5% VIP discount in exchange for a phone number.
  • The phone number field's placeholder (the grey hint text inside the box) should encourage the visitor to enter their real number, because the discount code is sent there. A fake number means the visitor never receives the code and the subscriber is lost.
  • Name every page in an Optimonk (a pop-up tool) campaign before setting up navigation rules, such as the form, the email thank-you page, the SMS upgrade page and the SMS thank-you page. Clear names keep the Next-page dropdowns (menus that pick the next page) unambiguous when a flow has several steps.
  • The Klaviyo SMS opt-in flow depends on the welcome flow already being live (switched on for visitors). It is complete once the SMS opt-in flow is live.

Email-to-SMS Escalation (Trojan Horse)

1 min

Trojan horse here means a two-step sign-up: first email, then SMS.

Structure

1 min
  • Trojan-horse structure: first collect the email with a discount reveal (a code shown after signing up), then escalate (step up) to an SMS upgrade with a higher discount, so each signup leads to a bigger offer.
  • Set the SMS upgrade offer to 15% (upgraded from the 10% email code) and make the upgrade the main action. Keep a 10% "No thanks" option as the alternative.

Abandoned Checkout Message Design

1 min

Shape and Length

1 min
  • Abandoned checkout SMS should be short, three to four lines. Use the customer's first name as a dynamic field (a spot the system fills in automatically). Include the offer (10-20% off), a sense of urgency (a reason to act now) and the deal end. Leave out reviews and extra content, since SMS must stay small and crisp.
  • Put the offer and the discount in the first line of every SMS, since readers skim and rarely read the full message. Keep messages short and direct.
  • Put the offer and event in the first one or two lines so they show in the notification (the preview on the phone screen).

Formula: Abandoned Checkout (SMS Bump)

1 min

A formula is a simple pattern to copy. The pattern: a short greeting with the first name, a reminder that items are still in the cart with a note of urgency, a single checkout button, and a reply line for help. Later messages add a discount code created in the SMS app and keep the same checkout link.

  • Example 1: "Hi Sam, your order is still waiting. Tap here to finish checkout before your size sells out. Reply here for help."
  • Example 2: "Hi Lee, your basket is saved. Here is 10% off with the code BACK10 at checkout: link. Reply STOP to opt out."

Formula: SMS Retargeting (SMS Pump)

1 min

One formula: a short reminder with the product name, a discount code that grows with each message, a link straight to checkout, and an opt-out line. The delay grows over time (15 minutes, 2 hours, 20 hours), and each message offers a bigger discount than the one before.

  • Example A: "Hi, your [product] is still in your cart. Use code SAVE10 for 10% off, link below. Reply STOP to opt out."
  • Example B: "Last chance: your [product] is waiting. Take 20% off with code SAVE20 before it sells out. Reply STOP to opt out."

Formula: First Abandoned-Cart SMS

1 min

Open with a non-blaming line that says checkout hit a snag and the order is saved. Then add three short proof or policy points, such as customer count, return window and free shipping threshold (the order amount needed for free shipping). It reassures without implying carelessness and gives concrete reasons to return.

  • Example A: "Hiccup at checkout, don't worry, your cart is saved. 12,000 happy customers, 30-day free returns, free shipping over $75."
  • Example B: "Looks like checkout didn't finish, but your order is still waiting. 5,000 five-star reviews, easy 60-day exchanges, free delivery from $100."

Win-Back

3 min

SMS Bump procedure

Trigger (the event that starts a message): Days without order, set to 30, meaning 30 days since the customer's last purchase. The source describes two routes to the same setting.

Route A:

  1. Click Edit on the customer win-back flow. A flow is an automatic series of messages. Win-back means bringing back customers who stopped buying.
  2. Remove any extra delay step (a waiting time before the next step), so the trigger alone controls timing.
  3. Set the trigger to Days without order and enter 30.
  4. Edit the SMS (text message): paste the template, create a discount code (a code the customer types at checkout to pay less) with the desired amount (example: 15% on the next purchase), add the homepage link and an opt-out instruction (a way to stop future messages).

Route B (adds a delay, then removes it):

  1. Click edit on the Customer win-back flow.
  2. Add a step with a delay in days set to 30.
  3. Edit the SMS with the win-back template and create a discount code with the chosen amount.
  4. Click save.
  5. Remove the extra delay step, set Customer win-back with days without order to 30, then save.

Formula

Use this checklist for every win-back message:

  • Mention the time since the last order.
  • Say you miss the customer.
  • Offer a discount for the next purchase.
  • Link to new products.
  • Add an opt-out line (a way to stop messages).
  • Keep the offer modest and specific.

Examples:

  • "Hi Jo, it has been a while since your last order. Here is 15% off your next order with code COMEBACK15: link. Reply STOP to opt out."

Message Formatting and Character Limits

1 min

A character is one letter, number, space or symbol. The character limit is the most characters one message can hold.

Sender and layout

  • Start each SMS with the brand name and a colon, so the reader knows who sent it.
  • Keep the brand name short, so there is room for the offer and the link inside the character limit.
  • In the Klaviyo SMS editor (Klaviyo is email and SMS software, and the editor is where you write the text), turn on "Automatically shorten links."
  • Add one blank line before the final sentence, so the message does not look cramped.

Character counting

  • Klaviyo's SMS editor counts characters against 160 for a single message.
  • Longer messages are sent as multiple parts. The counter then shows a 306-character limit for two parts.
  • Shorten the text where possible, so the message stays within one 160-character part.
  • A draft with a link read 190 of 306, which splits the message into two parts. Check the counter before saving.

Reply line and help

  • Include a reply line for questions in each message, such as "If you have any questions, just reply to this message and we will be happy to help."
  • For a personal tone, write as one person who is ready to answer, not as a broadcast (a message sent the same way to many people).
  • A phone number is personal.

Campaign Settings and Tool Checks

1 min
  • For a one-off SMS offer update in Klaviyo (a single change, not a repeated message), turn off Smart Sending and Quiet Hours in the SMS step.
  • Smart Sending limits how many messages one person gets. Quiet Hours stops sending at set times.
  • The message then sends at its scheduled point in the flow instead of being held.
  • Source conflict: requires quiet hours and permitted send times. turns Quiet Hours off for a one-off offer update.
  • Permitted send times are the hours your market's rules allow messages. Check them in your market before turning Quiet Hours off.
  • Check the Optimonk Next-page settings (Optimonk is a pop-up tool that shows boxes on your website): Conversion closed (the visitor took the wanted action), campaign goal reached, Smart sync on.
  • Name every Optimonk page before setting up navigation (the way visitors move between pop-up pages).

Testing SMS Against Email

1 min

Procedure

  1. Set up an SMS message on the abandoned-cart flow first. The abandoned-cart flow sends messages to customers who left items in the cart without paying.
  2. Compare the open rate (share of people who open the message), the click-through rate (share who click the link), and purchases from SMS against the same flow in email.
  3. Expand SMS only if it performs better or adds value your email cannot.

Practice

  • Use SMS and email together and compare their performance across campaigns (a campaign is one set of messages sent for one goal).
  • Build SMS and email flows as automated sequences, with A/B testing built in from the start (A/B testing means sending two versions to split groups to see which works better).
  • Test the Optimonk pop-up live before relying on it, enabling the app embed in the Shopify theme. Shopify is the online store platform. The theme is the store's design. The app embed is the switch that adds an app to that design.

Tools

  • SMS Bump and Postscript are Shopify SMS tools (apps that send text messages in a Shopify store). They are broadly similar, so how you use SMS matters more than which tool you pick. Drip (an email tool) and some email autoresponders (tools that send scheduled emails automatically) also offer SMS features.
  • TxtCart is an SMS cart recovery app (it messages customers who left items in their cart). It is easy to set up. The author reported a 50x ROAS during the free trial. ROAS (return on ad spend) is money earned per dollar spent on ads, so 50x means 50 dollars back for each dollar spent.
  • The author said that result was strong enough that other SMS recovery apps were no longer needed. This is a single reported trial result, not a benchmark (a standard to compare against).
