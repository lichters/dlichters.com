Deliverability foundations: authentication, reputation and warm-up

Section 15.3 · 3 of 17 in this chapter · 14 min read

In short

  • Send cold or bought lists from a second domain, so your main domain's trust score stays safe.
  • Before the first send, set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC, three checks that prove your emails are really from you.
  • A new domain or new email provider starts with zero trust, so warm it up slowly for about three to four weeks.
  • Before switching providers, clean your list and tag people who opened or clicked in the last 90 days.
  • If open rates fall, send less and check your wording for spam-like content.

Separate sending domains by list type

1 min
  • Send existing brand subscribers from the main domain. Send cold purchased lists (people who never asked for your emails) and re-engagement campaigns (emails that try to wake up inactive people) from a secondary sending domain or a separate email service, such as Send in Blue. Do this to protect the main domain's reputation.
  • If a cold list has low engagement (few people open or click), ISPs (the mail services that deliver to inboxes) flag the domain as spam.

Authenticate the sending domain before the first send

1 min
  • A campaign that warns that its emails are not authenticated using DNS records (the domain's settings list, stored at your domain host) must be fixed before sending. Authentication through DNS records is required for providers such as Gmail or Outlook to show a sender identity.
  • The three authentication methods are SPF, DKIM and DMARC. SPF (a list of approved sending servers) checks whether the sending server is on the domain's list of approved senders and flags the email if it is not. DKIM (a digital signature) checks that the email has not been altered in transit (on its way to the inbox). The sources name DMARC (a rule that tells providers what to do with failing email) but do not explain how to set it up.
  • An SPF TXT record and a DKIM TXT record (short text notes stored in DNS) let receiving servers verify that mail from your domain is legitimate. This raises deliverability (the share of emails that reach the inbox). **

Google Workspace SPF and DKIM setup

3 min

Before you start, check that the setup is ready:

  1. In the Google Admin console (Google's control panel for company accounts), go to Apps > G Suite > Settings for Gmail and open Authenticate email. G Suite is the older name for Google Workspace. Before this, check under Apps > G Suite that the Gmail service shows ON for everyone in the organisation.
  2. The Authenticate email section may not appear until 24 hours after a new G Suite sign-up. Do not panic if it is missing.

Then add the records:

  1. Open the DNS settings of your domain at your domain host (the company where you bought the domain). The layout differs by provider, but the method is the same.
  2. Copy the existing TXT record that starts with v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com. G Suite adds this record automatically when it is connected to the domain.
  3. Add a new TXT record with host SPF in uppercase (the host is the name field of the record) and paste the copied value as the TXT value.
  4. Click Save.
  5. In the Google Admin console, go to Apps > G Suite > Settings for Gmail and open Authenticate email.
  6. Select your sending domain and set the DKIM key bit length (the size of the signing key) to 1024.
  7. Click Generate to create the new DKIM TXT record.
  8. Back in the DNS settings, add a new TXT record with host google._domainkey and paste the whole generated value into the TXT value field. Click Save.
  9. Return to Authenticate email and click Start authentication. Leave it running while DNS updates.

Rules for the setup:

  • DKIM key length: The source sets the key bit length to 1024 and stresses changing it from the default. The screen showed 2048 as the default. Do not keep that default when following this setup.
  • DKIM record format: The DKIM record host is google._domainkey, and the value begins with v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=.
  • Do not regenerate during authentication: Generating a new DKIM TXT record stops email authentication until you click Start authentication again and DNS has updated. Do not regenerate the key while authentication is running unless you are prepared to restart it.
  • Propagation time (how long changes take to spread across the internet): After Start authentication, DNS changes can take up to 48 hours to propagate. The status shows Authenticating email while it runs.
  • One SPF record per domain: A domain should publish only one SPF TXT record. If a record starting with v=spf1 already exists, edit it instead of adding a second one. Two SPF records can make SPF checks fail. This check is an addition to the source's steps, not something the source teaches.
  • SPF qualifier (the ending rule that says what to do with failing mail): The Google SPF value is v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com followed by a qualifier. The screens show ~all (soft fail, accept but mark as suspicious) before the change and -all (hard fail, reject) after it. Choose the qualifier your policy needs and keep only one SPF record per domain.
  • Keep the verification record: Keep the existing google-site-verification TXT record (a proof-of-ownership note) in the DNS zone (the full list of your domain's records). It proves domain ownership to Google, and removing it can break the G Suite domain verification.
  • Keep the mail routing records: Leave the MX records in place (the addresses that route mail to your inbox) when adding the SPF and DKIM TXT records. They route incoming mail to Google.

Keep the domain's history clean

1 min
  • If a domain is blacklisted (listed as a known spam sender) before use, check its history with the Wayback Machine (an archive of old websites). Also search the domain name for reviews before buying a premium domain (a costly, already-registered name).

Understand sender reputation

2 min
  • Sender reputation (how much email providers trust you) has four levels: bad, low, medium and high. Aim for medium or high at all times, because low and bad scores send most mail to spam.
  • A new provider does not assign a starting grade such as bad. The score is empty at first and needs a few weeks of sending to fill in. The goal of the warm-up (sending small amounts first and growing slowly) is to reach a positive reputation that you can carry forward before opening larger segments (groups of subscribers) and volume.
  • Switching to a new email service provider (the company that sends your emails) resets your sender reputation to zero, even if you have mailed the same list for years. The reset applies at domain level, so the old reputation does not carry over to the new platform.
  • A newly registered sending domain also starts with no reputation, even when it uses the same IP addresses (numbered internet addresses of your mail server) and infrastructure (the technical setup) as before. A new domain needs its own warm-up.
  • The reset happens because the new provider sends through different servers and IP addresses. Mailbox providers have never seen this combination of domain, IP and infrastructure. They cannot use past performance, so the sender must build a new history through positive signals such as opens, clicks and replies.
  • Most email platforms with shared IPs (addresses that many senders share), such as Klaviyo or ActiveCampaign, already manage warmed IP addresses (addresses with a good sending history), so IP warming is normally not needed. Only a business that manages its own dedicated IP (its own private address) or server needs a separate IP warm-up strategy, which should follow the provider's advice closely.
  • Each email provider publishes its own onboarding guidance (setup instructions) for new domains, such as Klaviyo, Mailchimp or SendGrid. Follow that guidance, verify the domain and set up authentication protocols, because they differ by provider.
  • Google shows a domain reputation level, such as High or Medium, for each sending domain. During the warm-up, check this level alongside the email provider's own numbers, since the provider's metrics are not always fully accurate.
  • Continuously follow deliverability best practices (proven habits for reaching the inbox) after the warm-up. Keep cleaning the list, avoid spammy or misleading content, respect subscriber preferences, and make unsubscribing easy. These habits keep the sender reputation strong.

Prepare the list before a provider switch

2 min

Procedure before closing the old email provider:

  1. Record the deliverability baseline (your starting numbers to compare later) for 30, 60 and 90 days: open rate (share of people who opened), click rate (share who clicked), bounce rate (share of emails that failed to deliver), unsubscribe rate (share who unsubscribed) and spam complaints (people who marked you as spam).
  2. Run all lists through a third-party email validator (an outside tool that checks whether addresses are real). Do this if the list has not been cleaned in the last six months.
  3. Remove invalid addresses and addresses that cannot be reached, including hard bounces (emails that failed permanently), before importing (loading the list into the new provider).
  4. Export the engagement data from the old provider: segments, suppression list (the do-not-email list) and performance history.
  5. Tag every subscriber who opened or clicked in the last 90 days as engaged (active). A tag is a label attached to a person. Record behaviour (opens, clicks) and product interests where available.
  6. Import the list together with the tags so the new provider can separate engaged from unengaged subscribers.
  7. Set up email authentication (SPF, DKIM and DMARC) for the new provider, following its setup guide.

Reasons behind the steps:

  • Clean the list before import. Email addresses go invalid, mailboxes fill up, and Microsoft and Google delete inactive mailboxes. Bounces to such addresses count against your reputation, even if the list was double opt-in (people confirmed twice that they wanted mail) or verified at signup.
  • Old, unverified addresses cause bounces, which count negatively against your reputation. Mailboxes that Microsoft or Google have deleted for inactivity will bounce as well.
  • Migrating segments and engagement history matters because a new provider knows nothing about your subscribers beyond what you import. Without tags, the new list is one undifferentiated block (one mixed group) and you cannot target the engaged group first.
  • Failing to migrate engagement data forces a random first send. This lowers opens and clicks and weakens the new reputation. Without history, the provider cannot separate engaged from unengaged subscribers.
  • Engaged subscribers are those who opened or clicked within the last 90 days. They should receive the first campaign on the new platform. Targeting the most engaged group gives the highest open and click rates, which rebuilds trust fastest.

Warm up a new domain or provider

2 min

Procedure after switching providers or registering a new domain:

  1. Ask the new provider for its onboarding or warm-up guidance. For a very large list, request a custom warm-up plan that states the daily send volumes (how many emails go out per day).
  2. On day one, send only to the most engaged segment so the first send stays under the provider's limit. Klaviyo's first send is below 3,000 recipients. Provider limits are usually below 10,000.
  3. Increase the volume each day by the percentage the provider allows. This is typically 10 to 15 percent and sometimes up to 50 percent. Never increase faster than that.
  4. Optionally connect an automated warm-up tool such as Warmup Inbox to the domain and let it run for a couple of months.
  5. Monitor open rate, click rate and inbox placement (where your emails land: inbox, Promotions tab or spam) every day. If they drop or red flags (warning signs) appear, reduce the volume and review the content.
  6. After about three to four weeks of gradual increase, move to your usual email strategy and send to the full list.

Volume and timing rules:

  • Daily increase, two sources: The usual safe increase cited is 10 to 15 percent per day, occasionally up to 50 percent. Some providers allow the daily send volume to roughly double each day. Follow the exact criteria your provider gives you, because they reflect what is safe for that platform.
  • Full warm-up duration: The full warm-up period for a switch or a new domain takes about three to four weeks. Do not move to the full list before that period ends.
  • Gradual plan: A gradual volume plan over three to four weeks protects the new provider's reputation and avoids underperformance. A business that skips the plan and sends to a 100,000-subscriber list straight away is likely to run into trouble.
  • Full list on day one: Sending the full list on the first day after a switch, especially when open rates are already weak, leads to deliverability problems. Skipping the gradual ramp also makes the damage to reputation longer and harder to repair later.
  • Non-linear curve: The warm-up is not linear (not a straight, steady line), so every business may need to adjust its schedule. Monitor it week by week and expect changes depending on list and content.
  • Red flags: Watch for falling open rates or click rates. If they drop, reduce the volume, check content for spammy or misleading elements, and then increase again more slowly.
  • Recovery after a switch: After a provider switch, open rates should recover gradually toward the levels recorded in the old provider's 30-, 60- and 90-day baseline. This recovery depends on the domain building positive interactions, which lets more mail reach the inbox instead of spam.

Control send timing and batching

1 min
  • Campaign send mode: use Gradual send (sending in parts over time) over several hours, starting at 11 am Eastern, with 20 percent of recipients per hour, instead of sending the whole list at once.
  • Sending a campaign to the whole list at one moment can overload the mail server (the computer that sends your emails) and reduce deliverability. Spreading the send over several hours avoids this.
  • Build sends in engagement buckets (groups sorted by how recently people engaged, for example 0-14, 15-30, 31-60 and 31-90 days engaged) and send them in batches. Smaller batches make it easier to see where opens begin to fall.

Write content for the primary inbox

1 min
  • Email deliverability depends heavily on content quality. If emails are landing in spam, control content quality first.
  • Text-based emails with personalization generally land in the primary inbox (the main inbox tab) more often than image-heavy emails. Image-heavy emails can go to the Promotions tab and be left unopened. A brand logo, footer and a few supporting images can still be added on top of text-heavy designs.
  • Send more personalized emails that look less like ads, and include offers people want to open. Watch engagement, because opens signal to the inbox provider that subscribers want the mail.
  • A high click-through rate (share of people who clicked a link) signals relevance to inbox providers such as Gmail. This supports deliverability and keeps emails in the primary inbox instead of the Promotions tab or spam folder. Opens without clicks over time signal that the email delivers little value. **
  • Spam includes buying an email list, or emailing addresses that never opted in (never agreed to get mail), such as contacts from an old address book. **
  • Newsletters must include a working unsubscribe link. Missing or broken unsubscribe options and re-subscribing people by other means are the main causes of blacklisting. **

Troubleshoot spam placement

1 min

Procedure if confirmation or order emails land in spam **:

  1. Remove images from the affected emails and test again.
  2. Remove one trigger word at a time, such as "free" (a word spam filters watch for), and send a test to a new address that is not in Klaviyo.
  3. Ask customers in early welcome emails to add the sender to their primary inbox.
  4. Ask early emails to elicit a reply (get a response) with a specific question.
  5. Add a reply request to order confirmation emails to build engagement.

Related rules:

  • Sending to many bouncing addresses makes email providers such as Gmail treat the sender as a spammer. That lowers deliverability, so more emails end up in spam folders. **

Understand Gmail tabs

1 min
  • Emails often land in Gmail's Promotions tab or in spam. General tips are to send more personalized emails that look less like ads, include offers people want to open, and watch engagement.
  • Image-heavy emails can go to the Promotions tab and be left unopened.
  • A high click-through rate keeps emails in the primary inbox instead of the Promotions tab or spam folder.

Measure opens and clicks with Apple MPP

1 min
  • Apple Mail Privacy Protection (MPP) opens emails automatically through a proxy (a middleman server) to prevent user tracking. This breaks open tracking.
  • Track both opens and clicks instead. Opens measure non-MPP engagement (engagement from users who do not use MPP). Clicks prove real user action and bypass the proxy for MPP users.

Diagnose a provider whose deliverability is failing

1 min

Procedure:

  1. Open the campaign's Deliverability tab and check Performance by inbox provider (open rates grouped by the company that runs each mailbox, such as Gmail or Yahoo). This shows whether the weak engagement is limited to one provider.
  2. Open Performance by domain to pinpoint the provider or domain with the low open rate.
  3. Create an exclusion segment for that provider's unengaged profiles (no opens or clicks between 15 and 365 days ago).
  4. Add this segment to the Don't send to field of the next campaigns. Clone a campaign (make a copy of it) to reuse the recipient settings if needed.
  5. Keep the segment excluded for about 7 days. Then check the provider's open rate again in the Deliverability tab after each send.
  6. When the provider's open rate rises above 30%, widen the segment gradually by editing its definition (for example, changing the 15-day window to 25 days). Check again after a few days rather than changing it immediately.

Principles:

  • Excluding inactive profiles from a provider for a short period lets their sender reputation recover. This improves inbox placement for the active profiles. Excluding them is a temporary fix, so the exclusion has to be reviewed and loosened as metrics improve.

Recover from a blacklisting

1 min

Procedure if a domain is blacklisted for email **:

  1. Identify which blacklist is listing it.
  2. Search for how to remove it from that specific provider.
  3. Follow their free delisting process (the steps to be taken off the list).
