Playbook › 17. Customer service, returns and chargebacks

Designing the support operation: scope, channels and ownership

Section 17.1 · 1 of 22 in this chapter · 10 min read

In short

  • Answer every chat, email and message on time, especially in the first weeks.
  • Handle all support yourself at first, so you learn which questions customers ask.
  • As orders grow, split support into a pre-purchase team and a post-purchase team.
  • Products priced $2,000 or more need much more support, so plan for it before you sell them.
  • Publish your support hours and an email address so buyers know a real person answers.
This section has 9 parts. Tap one to open it.

Scope: which channels support covers

2 min
  • A store's support covers live chat (a chat box on the website), inbound phone and email, plus social messaging if the store uses it. Inbound means the customer starts the contact. At low volume, one person can handle all of these.
  • Cover three channels: email, live chat on the store, and social media messages and comments. Social media means Facebook page messages, Instagram DMs (private direct messages) and comments on TikTok organic videos (free posts, not paid ads).
  • Answer direct messages and comments on time. If you run organic TikTok videos, assign someone to answer the comments on them.
  • Staff live chat or another support channel, because new visitors ask questions before they buy. Publish your support hours and an email address so buyers know a real person answers.
  • At launch, let customers reach the store through live chat, email and phone. Their questions show how the business actually works and what needs to be clarified.
  • A customer service role covers live chat, phone calls, and order shipping and tracking (following where a parcel is). Social media and content writing go to a separate person, because combining them breaks down quickly.
  • Automating about 30% of tickets (single customer requests) lowers first response time (how long until the first reply) and cost. It frees the team for complex cases that can lead to sales. Self-service answers (customers find the answer alone, for example on a FAQ page) also beat making customers wait for an agent.
  • TikTok direct messages now transcribe voice memos (recorded voice messages turned into text) automatically as they are sent. This makes person-to-person conversations easier to read and follow.
  • A structured customer service setup (one with set steps and roles) answers questions quickly, prevents refunds caused by confusion, builds trust in the brand and supports sustainable (lasting) store growth.
  • Phone volume in a small dropshipping store (a store where the supplier ships each order directly to the customer) can be about one or two calls per day, and nearly all of them can be sales-related. At that volume, phone can be an extra task for a freelance assistant (an outside helper paid per job) who already works at a computer, rather than a full-time role.

Scope of post-sale support in a store sale

1 min
  • A store sale means selling your whole store to someone else. A typical post-sale scope (support after the purchase) is about one month of email support plus two calls of at least one hour each over that month. Agree the scope before the deal closes (before the sale is signed).
  • Pre-selling means the calls and emails a buyer needs before committing to a purchase. Products that need a lot of pre-selling add workload for the owner, so avoid them when building a low-effort store.

Who handles what at each stage

2 min
  • Early on, the owner answers every chat, email and call personally. Quick replies to stock (products you have available to sell) and order questions turn clicks into sales.
  • Handle all customer service yourself at first, so you learn which questions customers ask and how to solve their problems.
  • The store owner handles customer contact and makes sure customers are satisfied, while the supplier usually stores and ships the product. Returns (products sent back) reduce the total sales figure in the store report (your sales summary).
  • Move customer service off the owner's plate as early as possible, even if only part of it is delegated (handed to someone else) at first. This covers phone calls, emails, online chat and sometimes supplier contact.
  • Typical contacts that interrupt an owner are shipping time questions and order-status questions (where is my order), arriving by phone, email and online chat. These are low-value, unpredictable inquiries and should go to a support person.
  • A virtual assistant (VA, a remote helper) can handle emails and phone calls as order volume (how many orders you get) grows.
  • Hire a VA working about 4 to 8 hours a day only after you know the questions and answers yourself.
  • Owners who run the business from abroad need phone and email coverage, handled personally or outsourced (given to an outside person or company), to keep buyers who are ready to purchase.

  • As order volume grows, split customer service into two teams:

    • Pre-purchase (sales, inquiry response, incentive-based compensation, meaning pay that depends on results such as sales). This team works before the buyer pays.
    • Post-purchase (fulfilment, returns, support). Fulfilment means packing and sending the order. This team works after the buyer pays.

  • Pre-purchase staff must understand order profitability (whether an order makes money), discount authorisation limits (how much discount they are allowed to give) and real-time stock status (what is in stock right now) before speaking with customers. Without this knowledge, they promise excessive discounts or unrealistic delivery dates. This creates losses and operational chaos (daily disorder in the work) for the fulfilment team downstream (the team that works later in the process).

Workload by price point

1 min
  • Products priced at $2,000 or more typically require higher levels of pre-sale and post-sale customer support. Pre-sale means before the sale, post-sale means after it. (2024.)
  • High-ticket products (expensive items) raise the customer service workload, mainly in pre-sale questions and post-sale follow-up (contacting customers after the purchase). Plan for this before selling products above about $2,000.
  • Customer service is central in a business with expensive items, since more support is needed as prices rise. Plan staffing (how many people and hours you need) and support around the price and return rate (the share of orders sent back) of the niche (your narrow market).
  • Customer service volume rises with higher-priced items, so plan support for those products.
  • Lower-priced products attract more pre-sale calls, such as customers asking whether an item is in stock. Higher-priced products mean fewer calls, but you need to be prepared to answer or return calls quickly. Return a call means call the customer back. (2023.)
  • The sources disagree on higher-priced products. Some describe a higher overall level of support (,, ). Others describe fewer calls but a need for fast replies. Both apply: plan for more total support and faster response time per contact, even if the number of calls is lower.
  • Expect a slightly higher volume of inbound support requests for electronics and complex products. A high order value, such as an $8,000 sale, can justify handling that extra support.
  • Fewer orders mean fewer tracking numbers (codes that let customers follow a parcel), fewer shipping problems and less customer service work. This workload reduction is a main reason to sell higher-priced products.
  • A low-ticket store (cheap products) of $40 to $50 products sourced from China (bought from suppliers in China) generated high order volume but became a support nightmare (constant, exhausting problems). High order count with low-value products is not better than fewer higher-value orders.

Workload by order volume

1 min
  • Early on, support contacts in a new dropshipping store start slowly, often with only a few calls and sometimes none on a given day. Workload grows only once sales and momentum (speed of growth) build, so early volume is usually manageable.
  • At high order volume, a store can receive hundreds of customer emails per day. This is a reason to outsource support early.
  • Many customers buy without ever contacting support, so slow replies affect only some of your sales, mainly early on. Sales continue while you handle the backlog (the pile of messages waiting for answers).
  • The share of buyers who call support before purchasing varies by market (country or customer group). In markets where many customers ask questions first, plan more phone capacity. In others, expect most buyers to purchase without contacting you.
  • The early small-volume and later high-volume figures describe different stages of the same store. Both apply: plan for a quiet start and for a large inbox (your pile of incoming messages) once sales grow.

Hours, response windows and coverage

1 min

  • Buyers who are ready to purchase will leave for a competitor (another store selling similar things) if calls or emails go unanswered for more than a few hours. (2023.)

  • Some customers will not wait for a callback (a call back from you), so slow responses will cost some sales. There is no perfect fix, so treat it as an accepted trade-off (giving up one thing for another) while the business is small.

  • Pick up a call immediately only when you can take it from a quiet place. Otherwise let it go to voicemail (a recorded message the caller leaves) and answer emails or chats in set blocks, such as about half an hour during the day.

  • Email is easier to handle in batches (grouped together), because there is a natural gap between a customer's message and your reply. Answer emails after work or in set blocks during the day.

  • Daily coverage routine when working in a far time zone (when your clock is many hours away from the customers'):

    1. Check emails right before going to bed, around 21:00 to 22:00 local time.
    2. Fulfil (pack and send) any orders that came in during the day so they do not wait a full day.
    3. Send tracking numbers, either personally or through a VA.
    4. Check emails again first thing in the morning.

    About 20 minutes at night and a morning check cover the start and end of a normal business day, so suppliers and customers see replies during their working hours.

  • Check a competitor's support quality by searching its store name and URL (web address) on Google and reading the reviews. Complaints about unanswered calls or emails show a gap you can close by replying quickly.

  • Do not invite overseas customers outside your shipping area (the countries you ship to) to contact you. Shipping costs often exceed the product price, and such inquiries are frequently fraudulent (fake or scam).

Setting up support step by step

1 min
  1. Handle all customer service yourself at first, so you learn which questions customers ask and how to solve their problems.
  2. Add answers to frequent questions (shipping times, warranties (promises to fix or replace a faulty product), stock status) to your website, so fewer people ask.
  3. Set up automated post-purchase emails with tracking updates, for example with the AfterShip app (a tool that sends tracking updates).
  4. Hire a virtual assistant working about 4 to 8 hours a day only after you know the questions and answers yourself.

Keeping support separate for each store

1 min
  • Keep customer service and support separate for each store, because each store has its own customers and its own problems. Mixing support across stores creates confusion that is hard to undo.

Support and growth: what to expect

1 min
  • Good customer service protects reputation (how people see your business) and can bring referrals (new customers sent by happy ones) and positive reviews. It will not multiply store traffic (number of visitors) or customer numbers on its own, so owners should not expect support to be a major growth driver.
  • Customer service is a growth lever (something that pushes growth). Buyers with a good experience come back, while a bad experience stops repeat purchases. Treat service quality as a core part of building a business that lasts.
  • Fast answers to customer questions, especially before purchase, can raise the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy). Customer support therefore affects sales, not just service.
  • The sources disagree on how much support can grow the business. One view says support does not multiply traffic or customers. Another says it raises conversion and repeat purchases (,, ). Both apply: expect support to protect and convert the customers you already have, not to bring in new visitors.
  • Customer service is a core success factor (something the business needs in order to succeed). E-commerce (online selling) businesses that offer no service either fail or stay small. Expect to answer phone calls and emails, because the belief that you never have to do this is wrong.
  • Long shipping times combined with a poor customer experience caused the 2016 to 2018 approach to fail, even when cheap Facebook ads (paid posts) brought in orders. Consider shipping speed and customer experience alongside traffic.
  • For a newly launched dropshipping store, customer service and shipping are the operational areas (day-to-day work areas) where owners most often have questions. Sort out these two areas early, a common starting point for daily store management.
