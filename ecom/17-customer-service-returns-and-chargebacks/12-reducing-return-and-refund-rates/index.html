Playbook › 17. Customer service, returns and chargebacks

Reducing return and refund rates

Section 17.12 · 12 of 22 in this chapter · 12 min read

In short

  • A return rate is the share of orders sent back. A refund rate is the share of sales money given back. Check which one a number measures before you compare.
  • For premium products, aim for a 1 to 2 percent return rate. Plan with 2 percent as the highest acceptable number.
  • Avoid niches with high returns, such as sizing-dependent clothing, mattresses, bedding and pillows.
  • Describe products accurately and only promise what you can really deliver. That is the first step to fewer refunds.

Set the return target and measure it correctly

2 min
  • Two numbers are easy to mix up, and they are not the same.
  • A return rate (the share of orders sent back) is one number.
  • A refund rate (the share of sales money given back) is the other.
  • The targets below use different ones. Check which one a benchmark measures before you compare.
  • For premium products (higher-priced, high-quality items) sold at premium prices, a return rate of 1 to 2 percent is normal and a good target.
  • Some niches (small groups of related products) run higher.
  • Plan with 2 percent as a safe ceiling, meaning the highest number you plan for.
  • This advice is older, from 2019.
  • For refunds, aim for a refund rate under 1 percent over a long period.
  • Calculate it over the whole period, not per 10 items.
  • Leave out damaged items that need a replacement from this calculation.
  • Track the refund rate for each product as a quality check.
  • A low refund rate suggests strong product satisfaction (happy customers).
  • In one example store, about 410 orders were placed without a single refund.
  • Track returns, cancelled orders and sales discounts each as a separate percentage of gross sales (all sales before anything is taken away) every month.
  • Do this so that a rise in returns is not hidden inside the sales total.
  • In the example store, returns and cancelled orders of 237,813 equalled about 13 percent of gross sales of 1,826,410.
  • Sales discounts of 34,197 were about 1.9 percent.
  • This advice is older, from 2021 to 2022.
  • For fashion, check the net sales after returns (sales left once returns are taken away) before deciding whether a product is viable.

Choose niches and suppliers by return risk

2 min
  • Research the expected return rate for your niche (your chosen group of products) before committing.
  • Start with brand reviews on Google.
  • Look up supplier names on retailer sites for reviews.
  • This advice is older, from 2020 and 2022.
  • Look for patterns of complaints about damage, quality inconsistency (quality that varies from item to item) and defects (faults).
  • Before signing a supplier (the company that makes or ships your products), check its brand reviews.
  • Look for complaints about damage, wrong colours, or items that differ from the photos.
  • These complaints signal a high return rate.
  • A supplier with a high return rate is a product quality problem.
  • Stop selling its products.
  • Do not cover the losses with restocking fees (fees a customer pays to send an item back).
  • Skip any niche where high returns are expected.
  • Returns cost shipping, support time and revenue at the same time.
  • This advice is older, from 2019.
  • Avoid building a store around products where delivery and returns depend on slow or unreliable chains (long supply routes).
  • A poor customer experience leads to disputes (arguments with the customer) and loss of repeat customers.
  • This advice is older, from 2019.
  • Avoid niches where customers have strong emotional attachment and tight deadlines.
  • A late or failed delivery can ruin an event and cause severe complaints.
  • Products that must arrive by a fixed date carry much higher service risk (risk that the delivery service fails).
  • Avoid mattresses, bed sheets and pillows for dropshipping (the supplier ships each order straight to the customer, so you do not hold stock).
  • Do this unless the seller is a brand with very high margins (the profit left after costs).
  • Feel, softness and stiffness cannot be shown on a website.
  • These personalised items have very high return rates that cost time, labour and money.
  • Avoid products with high return rates or that customers often ask about, such as electronics and apparel (clothing).
  • These products need more support and return handling.
  • This advice is older, from 2021.
  • A store with a physical showroom (a shop where customers see products in person) can reduce returns.
  • It helps customers choose the right product in person.
  • It can sell online only the brands whose return policies allow it.
  • This advice is older, from 2021.
  • For fashion, returns are common.
  • They can be acceptable if sales exceed returns.
  • Sizing-dependent fashion (clothes where the fit must be right) creates high return rates.
  • Customers expect Amazon-level return policies (very easy, generous returns, like Amazon offers).
  • Dropshipping cannot sustainably support sizing-dependent products.

Product types with specific refund risks

2 min

Low-priced and impulse products

  • Impulse purchases (bought on a sudden urge) and low-priced markdown products (cheap items sold at a discount) drive very high reported return rates.
  • Premium products bought after more thought return far less often.
  • This advice is older, from 2019.
  • The two observations below do not conflict.
  • Very cheap novelty items (small fun products), around $13 or less, are less likely to be returned.
  • Customers often do not bother sending back a low-value product.
  • Check this against your own return rate before relying on it.
  • Items under about $13 are returned less often because return shipping is not worth the effort.
  • Low-priced markdown and impulse purchases are returned more often.
  • Apply the $13 rule only after checking your own rate.
  • Gimmicky products (products that rely on a clever trick or hook) carry high refund risk.
  • Buyers are often disappointed by the product's real quality.
  • Plan for refund handling and dispute management from the start.

Digital products

Digital products are items with no physical box, such as downloads or online content.

  • Refund requests for a digital product are a direct signal of low content quality.
  • They show where the product needs to improve.
  • Make sure the content is accurate and useful before launch (before you release it).
  • Poor content is what triggers refunds.

Assembly-heavy products

  • Include supplier videos showing assembly time (how long it takes to put the product together).
  • Include photos of what the packaged goods (products in their box) look like when delivered.
  • Set customer expectations upfront to reduce returns and complaints.
  • This advice is older, from 2022.

Fashion and footwear

  • Some buyers wear fashion items for a weekend and return them with the tags (price labels) still on.
  • This causes chargebacks (the customer's bank takes the money back after a dispute).
  • Plan return rules for clothing with this in mind.
  • Bracketing (buying several sizes to keep one) pushes return costs up.
  • Wardrobing (buying for a single use and returning) also pushes return costs up.
  • Strategic friction (small, deliberate hurdles) in the return policy or at purchase filters out customers who return for no reason.
  • This works without breaking loyal relationships.
  • Categories where the product may differ from the photos, such as jewellery or clothing with sizing or quality variance (differences in size or quality), cause hesitation.
  • Customers hesitate because of refund risk.
  • For shoes, state clearly on the product page that stock is limited.
  • Also state clearly that returns are not accepted.
  • This discourages buying one pair to send back for another size.
  • Expect that some wrong-size cases will still need a refund.
  • Sizing issues can cause returns, especially for shoes and footwear.
  • Plan refunds into the margin.

Electronics

  • Electronics carry a high risk of chargebacks and disputes when quality is poor.
  • Test a new electronic product with the first week of customers.
  • Collect reviews.
  • Ask about quality before increasing spend (the money you put into ads and stock).

Describe the product so the customer knows what arrives

1 min
  • Accurate product descriptions and photos reduce refund requests.
  • Staff notes that explain exactly what is in the product also reduce refund requests.
  • The reason is simple: customers know what they are buying.
  • Clarity before purchase is the first refund preventer.
  • Sell only what fits the promises you make.
  • Congruent purchases (purchases that match what you promised) reduce buyer's remorse (regret after buying).
  • Congruent purchases also reduce refunds and chargebacks.
  • A brand with a typical 20 to 25 percent return rate credited its low returns to accurate product advertising.
  • It also credited its low returns to strong product imagery.
  • It further credited them to a quality product that customers do not want to send back.

Set expectations on the sales page

1 min
  • State customer service speed and the returns policy on the sales page.
  • For example: "fast customer service" and "easy returns".
  • This way the buyer knows who will help if something goes wrong.
  • Visible support lowers the fear of being left alone with a bad purchase.
  • Place it next to the guarantee and secure checkout messages in the risk-removal section.
  • Reply to customer questions and complaints quickly to keep returns low.
  • One store had no refunds because support was fast and problems were fixed for the customer.
  • A money-back guarantee shown on the product page must match the refund process you actually operate.
  • Make sure customer support can process it without extra conditions the buyer was not told about.
  • A money-back guarantee needs fast, clear refund handling.
  • The guarantee is part of the offer and affects trust and conversion (the share of visitors who buy).

Remove the buyer's risk

1 min
  • Risk reversal (the seller takes on the risk so the buyer does not) removes the buyer's fear of losing money.
  • Common tools are a 30-day money-back guarantee with a free return.
  • Another common tool is a 100% refund if the buyer is not satisfied.
  • A stated free-returns policy is a third common tool.
  • A money-back guarantee works because buyers are loss averse (they fear losing money more than they value gaining a product).
  • Reducing the downside of a purchase increases conversion more than merely highlighting the upside (the benefits).
  • A returns policy that says no returns are needed and the product is replaced for free reads as a strong guarantee.
  • This kind of policy can lift conversion.
  • Display the 14-day money-back guarantee prominently during Q4 (the last three months of the year) to build confidence.
  • Do this only if the terms are clearly stated.

Build the return allowance into the margin

1 min
  • Build a return allowance (money set aside for expected refunds) into the margin.
  • This keeps refunds from pushing individual sales into loss.
  • Set the allowance from your expected return rate.
  • Plan refunds into the margin.
  • Sizing issues can cause returns, especially for shoes and footwear.
  • Include the cost of processing returns in the cost per customer.
  • Include the share of trials that are never paid for in the cost per customer.
  • Returns are unavoidable in e-commerce (selling online), but they can be reduced.
  • Reducing returns is a core task.
  • Plan for returns as a normal part of the business and work to minimise them.
  • This advice is older, from 2021 to 2022.
  • Plan for refund handling and dispute management from the start, particularly for gimmicky products.

Keep the customers who return less

1 min
  • Long-term customers are less likely to return items.
  • Attract customers who want to wear the product for a long time and keep coming back.
  • Prefer them over customers who buy for a one-time occasion.
  • The brand with a typical 20 to 25 percent return rate named its main reasons for lower returns.
  • The first reason is accurate advertising.
  • The second reason is strong product imagery.
  • The third reason is a quality product that customers do not want to send back.
