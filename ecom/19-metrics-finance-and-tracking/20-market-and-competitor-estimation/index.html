Playbook › 19. Metrics, finance and tracking

Market and competitor estimation

Section 19.20 · 20 of 23 in this chapter · 19 min read

In short

  • Tools are only about 50 to 70 percent accurate for small stores, so check two tools before you spend money.
  • Look for demand first: a steady Google Trends interest, and competitors who keep their ads running.
  • Use the high-range CPC to plan content, never as your ad budget.
  • Judge a new store only after 24 hours of data.
  • High views do not prove sales. Check real sales with a tracking tool before you copy a product.
More key points
  • An AI answer is a starting point. Confirm any number with a second source before you use it.
  • For big decisions, compare two or three tools first.
  • Estimate profit as revenue x profit margin. Use the lower end of 15 to 30 percent unless free traffic supports more.

Start with market size and expectations

2 min
  • Market size predictions are usually wrong.
  • Some products grow far past expectations, going from 5k to 10k daily and beyond.
  • Other products stop growing (plateau) even with large potential.
  • So focus on actual inputs and outputs (what you spend and what comes back), not on guessing a ceiling (the highest level a product can reach).
  • Before you commit a large budget, know three numbers:
  • Your total spend per customer per year.
  • Total market consumption (how much the whole market buys).
  • The share of the market the product appeals to.
  • Without these figures, you cannot judge the cost of acquiring a customer.
  • Roughly 3 million people in the US have hallux valgus (a bunion, a bent big toe joint).
  • Use this as a rough upper limit (the most it could be) for the market of a bunion-related product.
  • Compare it with the sales a product claims before you accept the claim.
  • If an ad's response (how many people reply or buy) is measured in one town or group, check how many readers are real prospects (people who might buy).
  • Waste circulation means copies that reach people who will never buy. Its cost depends on the share of real prospects.
  • An offer that appeals to 1 in 5 readers can have a far lower cost per prospect than an offer that appeals to 1 in 100.
  • Requests for samples grow with how broad a product's appeal is.
  • One newspaper run of a widely used canned food drew 1,460,000 sample requests.
  • A narrower product drew only a fraction of that.
  • Before printing, forecast sample demand from the product's breadth of appeal.
  • Product research and ad numbers are guidelines, not fixed rules.
  • A benchmark (a reference number to compare against), such as 300 orders, is a starting filter, not a final verdict.
  • Products or ads just below it can still have real potential.
  • Use viewer and analytics data (numbers on who watches and buys) to decide what to add to the store.
  • When one group is large, such as around 30 percent men, add products for that group.

Validate demand with Google Trends and seasonality

1 min
  • Google Trends (a free Google tool that shows search interest over time, from 0 to 100) is useful for checking demand.
  • Interest between 25 and 100 is a good range for demand validation (checking that real demand exists) for a product or niche (a small market segment).
  • A broad keyword (a general search term) with zero interest for one to two months is a red flag: demand is low.
  • Google Trends can show a niche's search interest over several years.
  • Look for a dip that repeats in the same period each year, not one-off swings.
  • **
  • Check seasonality (sales that rise and fall with the seasons) before you choose products.
  • Sales can drop when the weather changes.
  • Slides sell poorly in cold weather, while sneakers sell all year.
  • A cold-weather season can be framed as clearing summer stock (selling off leftover summer goods).
  • Brand and product name searches show organic demand (people wanting the product without paid ads).
  • Rising brand searches show that customers look for the brand directly.
  • The case had about 8,000 monthly brand searches and about 14,000 for the product name.

Keyword demand and CPC

1 min

Keyword Planner metrics

1 min
  • Keyword Planner (Google's free keyword tool) shows average monthly searches: how many people search the term on Google per month.
  • It also shows a competition rank set by Google.
  • It shows a top-of-page bid range (the price range for an ad at the top of results) with a low and a high value.
  • CPC (cost per click: what an advertiser pays for each click) in your tracking sheet is the high-range top-of-page bid.
  • Track keywords using the high-range CPC.
  • Never treat it as a budget.
  • Keywords are researched for content (articles and pages), not bought as ads.
  • The low range can be far below the high range: 99 cents against $6.08 for "standing desk".
  • A high CPC is a sign that the keyword probably converts (leads to buying).
  • That makes it worth creating content for.
  • The lower bid range is not used for decisions because it varies more strongly.
  • CPC changes with match type (how closely a search must match your keyword) and with ad quality.
  • A long-tail search (a longer, more specific phrase), such as a product name plus a city, costs less than a broad term.
  • Better ads, which get more clicks and keep visitors longer, lower the cost per click.
  • These differences explain why two similar keywords can have very different bids.
  • A keyword with much lower search volume can have a higher CPC.
  • That suggests advertisers earn more from it, so it likely converts better.
  • Example: "stand up desk" had 33,100 monthly searches at $7.48 CPC, while "standing desk" had 201,000 at $6.08.

Estimate competitor sales from order numbers

1 min

Test-order method

2 min

Use a test purchase to read a store's order number and estimate its order volume.

  1. Place a test order (buying one item yourself) on the competitor's store. Cost: for example 20-30 USD.
  2. Note the order number. Shopify (a platform for building online stores) order numbers start at 1,000. Orders placed = order number minus 1,000.
  3. Revenue in that window = (order number minus 1,000) multiplied by the product price.
  4. Example: order 1,500 for a 20 USD product gives 500 orders, about 10,000 USD.
  5. Place a second test order 24 hours later.
  6. Subtract the first order number from the second. The result is the orders in the last 24 hours.
  7. Multiply that by the average product price to get daily revenue.
  8. Compare the total with the account's views. Factors such as older viral videos (posts that spread widely earlier) are not visible in this method.
  • Estimate a competitor's order count by placing a test purchase and reading the order number.
  • Subtract the store's starting number, about 1,000 in the example, to get an approximate order count.
  • Multiply that by the average order value (the average amount spent per order) to estimate revenue.
  • To estimate total orders from a store's order numbers, take the latest order number you see.
  • Subtract the lowest number seen earlier, as a baseline (a starting point).
  • Then subtract one for your own test order.
  • Example: 3,493 minus 1,000 minus 1 gave about 2,492 orders in roughly one month.
  • When average order value is unknown, use the highest likely unit price (for example $13).
  • This gives a careful upper limit (the most the revenue could be).
  • Some stores show only a confirmation number at checkout.
  • They send the real order number by email later.
  • Wait for that email before you decide a test purchase failed.
  • If it does not arrive, check the spam folder (junk mail).
  • Revenue from a viral product (one that spread widely) can only be estimated.
  • Use public signals such as follower counts, view counts and posting frequency (how often an account posts).
  • Exact sales cannot be read from the outside.

Worked cases

1 min
  • Infinity Pencil case: a corn-themed video using a trending sound (a popular audio clip) reached about 6.8 million views in two days.
  • The store sold at $13. Its product cost $2 to $3 from AliExpress (an online supplier marketplace).
  • It had about 10 reviews.
  • It recorded 2,492 orders in about one month, roughly $32,000 in revenue. This was estimated with the maximum price of $13 per order.
  • Key point: the trending sound drove reach.
  • Key point: the cheap price lowered the purchase barrier (the obstacle to buying).
  • Key point: the broken product link was a missed-sales problem that likely reduced results.
  • Competitor check on a viral toy product (monkey product): the account had about 200-300 million views over a year of uploading.
  • Its order numbers showed about 600 orders at about 40 USD, roughly 24,000 USD in total revenue.
  • That is about 120 USD per million views, below the author's average of about 180 USD per million.
  • This confirmed the product was low-converting (few viewers turned into buyers).
  • Another product tested with a dummy order (a fake test purchase) had only two orders in 24 hours, so it was dropped.

Check tracker accuracy and limits

1 min

Accuracy

1 min
  • Cross-check store sales with at least two tools, because no sales tool is 100 percent accurate.
  • For stores around $1,000 per day, estimates are roughly 50 to 70 percent accurate.
  • Accuracy drops as stores scale up.
  • Tracker estimates from Peekster are about 50 to 70 percent accurate.
  • Use them as a directional signal (a hint of the direction, not exact numbers), not as exact revenue.
  • Peak Star tends to underestimate sales.
  • A store showing $800 there is likely doing at least that much.
  • Shop Hunter and AfterLive gave the most consistent numbers in the comparisons.
  • Do not treat tracker revenue, spend or traffic figures as exact.
  • Use them to rank stores and products.
  • Then cross-check them before you commit your budget (the money you plan to spend).
  • Revenue from tracker estimates is only a hypothesis (an unproven guess) until it is confirmed.
  • Sales tools typically track a store for one to two hours and extrapolate (project up) to a full day.
  • So any single estimate is only a hypothesis.
  • For important decisions, compare two or three tools before you act.

New stores and large stores

1 min
  • Sales trackers need about 24 hours before they show reliable figures for a newly opened store.
  • Judge a new store's sales only after that window, not from the first hours of data.
  • Stores reporting $1 million or more per day in sales tools are usually established brands.
  • These brands have big launches, multi-channel promotion (marketing on several platforms at once) or celebrity support. They are usually not new stores.
  • Use such figures only as a sign of what is possible, not as a benchmark for a beginner.
  • Winning Hunter's Sales Tracker (a tool by Winning Hunter) only estimates revenue for stores doing $10k to $100m per month.
  • The reason: the old tracker capped at $1m per month, meaning it stopped counting above that.
  • Each estimate falls between two revenue brackets (ranges), such as $9,750,000 to $13,000,000 per month.
  • So read it as a range.
  • In Winning Hunter's store list, the Est. Revenue (estimated revenue) column can switch between 1d (1 day) and 30d (30 days) views.
  • It shows MIN (lowest) and MAX (highest) estimates.
  • Compare the 30d figure with its MIN and MAX range, rather than a single number.
  • The revenue is a bracket estimate (a range), not a tracked figure.

Trend tracking

1 min
  • Track the same store's daily sales across several days to see the direction of the trend.
  • A sharp rise or fall is a reason to recheck the numbers in a second tool before you act on them.
  • A store went from 2.6k to 5.2k in sales over two days.
  • Monitor it for trend changes.
  • Comparing those numbers with a second tool gives a more reliable figure.

Ad counts

1 min

  • So any ad count it gives is unverified.
  • Count the paid Meta ads for each competitor yourself in the Meta Ad Library.
  • This takes about five minutes per niche.
  • Use the 'Active' filter to see which ads are still running.
  • Recheck it regularly to see whether competitors keep running ads for a product.
  • Continued spending signals demand.
  • Competitor active ads for one product show its stage.
  • 20 or more active ads: the competitor has moved from testing into scaling (spending more to grow).
  • 30 or more active ads: solid proof of concept (proof the idea works).
  • 50 or more active ads: suggests roughly $1,000 to $2,000 or more in revenue from the product (time period not specified).
  • High engagement (likes, comments and shares) on competitor ads is a positive signal for a test product.

  • The Ads Launched figure on a brand overview counts the ads a brand started within the selected period.
  • It is separate from total and active ad counts.

Proof of offer

1 min
  • A store with consistent sales for a long time is proof that the offer works.
  • Its ads and landing page (the page a visitor arrives on) can be used as a baseline (a starting point) for testing your own.
  • Your version can change the offer and branding (name, look and style).

Check traffic sources

1 min
  • Traffic sources can be estimated with Similarweb (a website traffic tool). The mix shows where the brand's growth is coming from.
  • In the case, roughly 53% of traffic was direct (visitors who type the address), 29% social (visitors from social media), and 66% organic (free visitors from search) in total.
  • (As written, these shares add up to more than 100%, so check the source numbers in Similarweb before you rely on them.)
  • Traffic estimates from third-party tools (tools made by other companies) can differ widely between country sites. They are not exact.
  • Example: one brand showed about 20,000 monthly visits on its UK site, but 183,000 to 193,000 on its US site.
  • So check each market separately.
  • When SimilarWeb hides a competitor's traffic, Ubersuggest gives an estimated monthly visit count for each country site.
  • Pull several months of data and check the trend.
  • A steady rise points to a growing store.
  • A competitor site without a country rank has too little traffic in that country to be ranked.
  • This does not mean the site has a problem.
  • Competitors visiting your site can usually only be identified at the IP address (a number that marks a device's internet connection) and city level through standard tracking.
  • Few businesses analyze individual visitors this deeply.
  • So repeat visits from rivals are not a practical concern.

1 min

  • A competitor's account with 10 million views made just 12 sales.

  • Always check sales with a tracking tool (software that counts real orders) before you copy a product that looks like a winner.

  • A bunion corrector (a device for a bent big toe joint) seemed to go viral on many accounts.

  • One store was estimated at about $800,000 in sales within one to two months of its first upload.

  • Use an AI assistant with web browsing (an AI chat that can search the internet) to find a quick market figure (a number about how a market behaves).

  • Example: how much of a product people use per year.

Turn estimates into profit

1 min
  • Estimated minimum profit = revenue x profit margin.
  • Revenue is the total money from sales, before costs.
  • Profit margin is the share of revenue you keep as profit.
  • Example: $134,000 x 30% is about $40,000.
  • Typical margins range from 15 to 30 percent.
  • So use the lower end unless free traffic (visitors who find the store without paid ads, called organic traffic) supports a higher figure.
