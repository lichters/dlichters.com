Break-even and target ROAS, CPA and allowable ad spend

Section 19.3 · 3 of 23 in this chapter · 49 min read

In short

  • ROAS (return on ad spend: sales earned per ad dollar) is revenue ÷ ad spend.
  • Break-even ROAS is the lowest ROAS with no profit and no loss. Calculate it with Revenue ÷ (Revenue − COGS), where COGS is what the product costs you.
  • Before running paid ads (ads you pay for), know three numbers: COGS, break-even ROAS and break-even cost per purchase.
  • Keep your actual cost per purchase (what one sale costs in ads) below the break-even cost per purchase.
  • Spending more on ads than your profit before ads means a loss.
More key points
  • Break-even ROAS is the minimum return your ads must earn. Below it you lose money. Above it you make money.
  • ROAS means sales from ads divided by ad spend. Work out your break-even before you spend on ads.
  • Each product has its own costs, so work out break-even per product, not once for the whole store.
  • Use a profit target above break-even to decide when to spend more. Use break-even to decide when to stop.
  • Other people's ROAS targets only help if they match your own margins and costs.
  • Break-even means the point where you neither win nor lose money. Know it for each product before you run ads.
  • Your ad cost per sale must stay below your profit per sale, or you lose money.
  • Set your target ROAS above break-even, with a small safety buffer.
  • Sources give different formulas. Use the margin-based formula once you know your real costs.
  • Judge ads by cost per customer or cost per dollar of sale, not by the number of replies.

Core definitions: ROAS, CPA and cost per purchase

2 min
  • ROAS (return on ad spend: sales earned for each ad dollar) is revenue ÷ ad spend. Examples: $100 ÷ $25 = 4x; $1,000 ÷ $100 = 10x; $1 spent that returns $1.60 gives 1.6; $10 spent that returns $80 gives 8; $1 that returns $2 gives 2.
  • Source B defines ROAS as revenue attributed to the ads (sales the ads brought in) divided by ad spend. So 15x means 15 units of revenue for every unit spent on ads. **
  • Source B also defines ROAS as sale price ÷ cost per acquisition (CPA, the cost to win one customer). A $2,000 product at a $500 CPA gives 4x (400% in Google Analytics, Google's website statistics tool). **
  • Google Analytics shows ROAS as a percentage: 4x appears as 400% and 10x as 1,000%. Compare analytics ROAS with calculated targets in the same format. **
  • Source A gives the same ROAS definition with a 10x example: $100,000 revenue from $10,000 spend equals 10x.
  • Cost per acquisition (CPA), cost per purchase (CPP) and cost per sale describe the same measure: the cost to win one customer or one order. Ad platforms (the tools where you run ads) and reports use them interchangeably.
  • CPA = total ad spend ÷ number of purchases. Read it in Meta Ads Manager (Facebook's ad tool) or calculate it from Shopify data (your store's sales records).
  • Cost per sale = total ad spend ÷ orders from that spend in the same period. Example: $14,000 over 366 orders is about $38.25 per sale. This figure should fall when paid traffic (visitors who come from ads) converts better (a higher share of visitors buy). **
  • The total campaign spend for cost per sale must include clicks on every product in the campaign, including products that never sold. Otherwise the figure is understated (too low). Example: $152 of spend with 2 sales gives $76 per sale.
  • Compare ROAS against CPA: a $20 CPA acquisition that generates $100 in revenue is more profitable than a $10 CPA that generates $50. Profit per sale matters more than cost per acquisition.
  • Source A: minimum product price = cost per sale × target ROAS (the ROAS you aim for). At a 10x target, a $74 sale needs a product priced at least $740, and a $52 sale needs at least $520. Any product priced above this threshold (minimum line) gives a ROAS above 10x.

Break-even ROAS and break-even CPA

1 min

Definition and calculation

4 min
  • Break-even ROAS (the lowest ROAS where you neither make nor lose money) is the point where revenue exactly covers COGS (cost of goods sold, what the product costs you) and ad spend. It leaves zero profit and zero loss. Above it, the campaign is profitable. Below it, the campaign is a loss. Knowing it shows which campaigns are viable (worth running).
  • Formula: Break-even ROAS = Revenue ÷ (Revenue − COGS) = Revenue ÷ Profit. Here profit means revenue minus COGS. Example: $1,000 revenue with $400 COGS gives $600 profit, so $1,000 ÷ $600 = 1.67x.
  • Simple example: $1,000 revenue with 50% COGS ($500) leaves $500 profit. If all profit is spent on ads, break-even ROAS = $1,000 ÷ $500 = 2.0x. Above 2.0x is profitable; below is a loss.
  • Conflict within the Source A source: one formula line reads "(Revenue − Cost of Goods) ÷ Revenue", which as written gives 0.6, not 1.67. The worked result of 1.67x matches Revenue ÷ Profit. Use Revenue ÷ (Revenue − COGS).
  • Sauna example: $400 sourcing cost (what you pay the supplier), $1,000 revenue, $600 gross profit (revenue minus product cost). A $300 ad budget gives a target ROAS of 3.3x (1,000 ÷ 300) and a break-even ROAS of 1.67x (1,000 ÷ 600).
  • Google Ads version: Breakeven ROAS = Revenue ÷ (Revenue − COGS & Fees). Google Ads is Google's ad tool. Fees are payment and platform charges. Example: revenue $10,000, COGS plus fees $6,000, profit before ads $4,000, so break-even ROAS = 2.5x.
  • Your margin before ads (the share of each sale you keep before ads, as a percentage of revenue) equals the maximum ad spend percentage at break-even. A 40% margin lets you spend up to 40% of sales on ads and still break even. Reducing COGS and fees raises the profit available for scaling ads (growing ad spend).
  • Source C: Break-even ROAS = Selling Price ÷ (Selling Price − Expenses). At the start, Expenses = product cost + shipping. This is an early estimate that does not yet cover other costs. Calculate it before launch and judge every ad set (a group of ads with one budget and audience) against it.
  • Break-even ROAS = Selling price ÷ (Selling price − COGS). For $60 with $20 COGS this gives 1.5. Dividing by the $20 cost alone would give 3 and is wrong.
  • Conflict within the Source C source: and write the formula as Selling price ÷ COGS, which gives 3 for $60 and $20, yet both state the result as 1.5. The 1.5 matches Selling price ÷ (Selling price − COGS), as says. Use the corrected form.
  • Source C tool example: COGS $10, selling price $40, break-even ROAS 1.3x. This matches Selling price ÷ (Selling price − COGS).
  • Gross-margin shortcut: Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ gross margin % (the share of each sale left after COGS). At that ROAS, the margin covers ad spend.
  • Break-even ROAS before fees = selling price ÷ (selling price − product − shipping). Payment and app fees raise the number. Include them for the real break-even.
  • Dropship Toolkit (a calculator tool) example: a product costing $18 sold at $39.99 has a break-even ROAS of 1.85 in the tool. A plain price-minus-cost calculation gives 1.82 (39.99 ÷ 21.99), so the tool likely deducts some extra cost.
  • Example with a $39.90 price and $17 fulfilment cost (the cost to pack and ship an order): a $22.90 margin gives 1.74. A stated profit of $22 gives 1.81. Check which profit figure a calculator uses before relying on it.
  • Source C KPI sheet formula (KPI means key performance indicator, a number you track to judge progress): Break-even ROAS = AOV ÷ (AOV × (1 − fee %) − COGS). AOV means average order value, the average money per order. Example: 60 ÷ (60 × 0.96 − 20) = 1.60. The daily business cost is not part of this ROAS formula.
  • Source E: Break-even ROAS = new-customer AOV (tax-inclusive, as shown in ad platforms) ÷ cost per order that makes contribution margin zero (gross profit per order). Contribution margin is what one order leaves after its direct costs. Example: $238.49 ÷ $130.27 = 1.83. At 1.83, the business neither makes nor loses money on first orders.
  • Break-even ROAS of 1.3-1.5 is the stated ideal range for any product. A break-even near 1.9 means only a thin profit even at a ROAS of 2.
  • Aim for a break-even ROAS of 1.6 or lower. That gives roughly three times the margin on the product. This leaves room for ad spend, app and staff costs (people you pay to help run the store) and payment fees.
  • For a product with break-even ROAS 1.5, a ROAS of about 1.6-1.8 or higher is profitable. A ROAS of about 1.3-1.4 or lower is a loss. Small ROAS changes decide profit or loss.

Break-even cost per purchase (margin per order)

1 min
  • Break-even cost per purchase = selling price − (product cost + shipping cost). Example: $6 product + $4 shipping, $40 price: $30 break-even margin per purchase. This $30 is the minimum the ad set must spend before you decide to continue, pause or kill (stop for good) it.
  • Break-even cost per purchase = selling price − COGS. Example: $60 − $20 = $40. CPA below $40 yields profit; above $40 yields a loss.
  • Break-even cost per purchase is the profit margin per product. Spend at least this amount on an ad set or ad before judging it. Judge at ad level later for more precision.
  • For first tests, fees and other expenses can be ignored. Subtract them later as they become known, for example transaction fees (the charge for each card payment). Example: $30 price − $10 product − $10 shipping = $20.
  • Full break-even CPP with the KPI sheet: AOV × (1 − average fee %) − average COGS including shipping − daily cost of running the business. Example: 60 × (1 − 0.04) − 20 − 0.9524 = $36.65. This is lower than the simple $40 margin because fees and daily costs are included.
  • Break-even CPP must be checked against actual cost per purchase. If CPA is $40 and break-even CPA is $36.65, the seller loses about $3.35 per sale (the source text says $3.50).
  • Procedure: calculate break-even cost per acquisition, write it down, and keep the actual cost per purchase below it. Any cost above it means the product is losing money.
  • Per product: know COGS, break-even ROAS and break-even cost per purchase before running paid ads. These are the baseline KPIs (starting numbers you track) that show whether a product can make money at all.
  • Source C gives a formula for break-even CPP as the margin, and a note on kill rules (rules that stop a losing campaign). Use it to set kill rules for campaigns.

Break-even CPA and max cost

2 min
  • The maximum you can pay through paid traffic is the break-even CPA. It equals net profit before marketing (profit left after product and per-sale costs, before ads) when you count one purchase per customer. If you use customer lifetime value (the total a customer spends over time), the maximum can be set against it. That may mean a loss on the first order. **
  • Break-even is not a good target for every sale, because a business that only breaks even earns nothing. Set the ideal target CPA below break-even (target CPA is the cost per customer you aim for). This keeps the chosen margin in the business. **
  • Formula: Break-even CPA = net profit before traffic per sale. Target CPA = net profit before traffic − profit to keep per sale. Example: $500 net profit before traffic and $300 wanted profit gives a target CPA of $200. **
  • Worked example: a product sold for $200 and bought for $100 leaves $100 gross profit. Free shipping and other per-sale costs take $20, so net profit before marketing is $80. Keeping $40 as profit and spending up to $40 per sale on ads gives a target CPA of $40. **
  • The unit cost is the total cost of one sale before marketing. It includes product cost, shipping, staff costs such as customer service, and all other overheads (fixed business costs) divided down to a single sale. Net profit before traffic = selling price − this full unit cost. **
  • Break-even max cost per sale is normally calculated on a single first purchase. A store with repeat buyers (customers who buy again) can instead use customer lifetime value. That may allow a maximum above break-even and means accepting a loss on the first order. **
  • Before scaling paid traffic (growing ad spend), know the maximum cost per sale and the ideal cost per sale for each product, or at least for product groups on average. Without both numbers, decisions to scale or pull back are guesswork. **
  • The ideal cost per sale sits below the maximum so that each sale keeps a chosen margin. Running every sale at break-even means the business never earns money. **

Break-even by units and volume

1 min
  • Break-even units for a one-time sponsorship fee (a single payment to be featured) = fee ÷ revenue per unit. Example: a $10,000 fee at $100 per unit needs 100 units sold to recover the fee in revenue, not profit.
  • Break-even sales per month = total monthly fixed costs (costs that stay the same each month, such as software) ÷ margin left per sale after product, fees and acquisition costs. This shows how many sales cover overhead.
  • Paid-ad dropshipping (dropshipping means a supplier ships each order for you; paid ads bring the buyers) customer acquisition cost (CAC, what it costs to win one new customer) is often about $5-15 per new customer, depending on niche (a narrow product market) and market. Use your own measured figure once ads run.
  • Paid ads cost roughly $7-12 in ad spend per conversion (one completed sale) for the products discussed. Include this when judging whether a product can sell at a profit with ads.
  • Include more costs than COGS and ads once orders are numerous. These are payment processing (around 4% for Shopify Payments, Stripe or PayPal, which are payment services), app subscriptions, Shopify transaction fees, and virtual assistant or staff costs. Add them to the break-even calculation after the first few profitable weeks.
  • For a store with several products, calculate one break-even ROAS from the average selling price and average fulfilment cost. Keep prices and fulfilment costs close across products so this one number stays accurate.
  • Calculate for each acquisition channel (each place you get customers, such as Meta or Google) the cost to acquire one customer, the time to break even, and the point at which that customer becomes profitable. These three numbers set how much you can safely spend on traffic.

Target and desired ROAS from profit allocation

1 min

Target ROAS with a profit share

1 min
  • Target ROAS = Revenue ÷ (Profit × Allocation %). Allocation % is the share of profit you choose to spend on ads. Example: $1,000 revenue, $600 profit, 50% allocated to ads gives 1,000 ÷ 300 = 3.3x.
  • Procedure:
    1. Calculate break-even ROAS first (for example, 1.67x).
    2. Decide the percentage of profit to spend on ads (for example, 50%).
    3. Calculate target ad spend = Profit × Percentage (for example, $600 × 50% = $300).
    4. Calculate target ROAS = Revenue ÷ Ad spend (for example, $1,000 ÷ $300 = 3.3x).
    5. A higher target ROAS means a lower ad spend allocation and more profit remaining.
  • Example: $1,000 revenue with $600 profit before ads. Allocating 50% of profit to ads gives $300 ad spend and a target ROAS of 3.3x. $300 of profit remains after paying for ads.
  • Spreadsheet columns for break-even and target ROAS, in calculation order, with the example values:

  • Break-even is the point where all profit before ads is spent on advertising. With $1,000 revenue and $600 profit before ads, spending $601 on ads loses $1, while spending $599 keeps $1.
  • Every dollar of ad spend above profit before ads is a dollar of loss.

Desired ROAS for a profit target

1 min
  • Desired ROAS is the ROAS a campaign must reach to hit a chosen profit target, such as 15% or 20%. Calculate it from COGS and AOV, not only from break-even ROAS.
  • Desired ROAS = AOV ÷ Profit Target CPP (the cost per purchase that leaves your target profit). Example: 60 ÷ 27.65 = 2.17.
  • Desired ROAS for a target margin uses COGS, AOV and the chosen profit percentage (for example 10%, 15% or 20%). It is read from a KPI calculation sheet.
  • Required ROAS for a profit target = 1 ÷ (margin after COGS and all non-ad costs, as a share of revenue, minus target profit share). Non-ad costs include payment fees, app fees and staff costs. Example: 66.7% margin, 4% fees, 15% target gives 1 ÷ 0.477 ≈ 2.1. A sheet with extra costs can land slightly higher, such as 2.17.
  • For a 15% profit target with COGS $20 and a given AOV, the target ROAS is 2.17. If Facebook ads alone run at 2.17 ROAS, the 15% margin is reached. Higher ROAS means a larger margin and possibly a reason to scale (spend more on ads).
  • A cost per purchase near $27.65 corresponds to roughly 15% profit under the same COGS and AOV. It is a good target when using the calculation sheet.
  • Source C benchmark ROAS formula: Benchmark ROAS = BE-ROAS + 20% margin. BE-ROAS means break-even ROAS. Your own benchmark (the target you measure against) can use a different margin, because not everyone aims for 20%.
  • Scaling target: aim for a ROAS above break-even by at least one point (break-even ROAS + 1).

Desired ROAS from first-order contribution

1 min
  • Target ROAS for a desired first-order contribution margin: amount to keep = AOV × target margin %. Required ROAS = AOV ÷ (gross profit per order − amount to keep).
  • Example with $238.49 AOV and $130.27 gross profit: for 15% margin, keep $35.77, and required ROAS = 238.49 ÷ (130.27 − 35.77) = 2.52. For 25% margin, required ROAS = 3.38.
  • Break-even ROAS shows the minimum ad return to avoid losses. Target ROAS sets the margin you want.
  • Set targets from the profit you want to keep, and compare them with ROAS in the ad account.
  • If ad ROAS meets the target but profit is still low, the problem is unit economics (what you earn and spend on one sale), not advertising.

Maximum acquisition cost and the 10% rule

1 min

Maximum acquisition cost

1 min
  • Maximum acquisition cost = profit per sale × share of profit you are willing to give to the ad platform. Example: $50 product with $15 profit gives a maximum of $7 if you keep 50% of profit (the source says $7; 50% of $15 is $7.50), or up to $14.95 with a much thinner margin.
  • Lifetime value changes the ceiling. A $50 product with a historical customer lifetime value of $500 can afford $450 to acquire that customer.
  • Without lifetime value data, the same business can only justify spending up to its first-order profit.
  • Know two dollar figures immediately: the maximum you can afford to spend to acquire one customer, and the target cost per acquisition for your ideal profit position. **
  • Both figures depend on your specific business. There is no universal correct amount. **
  • Use the maximum affordable acquisition cost and the target CPA to decide where to advertise, where not to, and where to spend more. **
  • Check these numbers across the full customer journey (every step from first click to purchase) and against customer lifetime value. **
  • Keep a financial system that shows at any moment whether actual CPA is at or below target, and why or why not. **
  • Hitting the target CPA is the marketing-side indicator of profitability (a sign that marketing is making money). **
  • To find the maximum you can spend per sale in the Profitable Traffic Calculator (a break-even tool), enter 0 as target profit per sale. **
  • Enter the profit you want to keep as a currency amount (for example $300), not a percentage. The break-even results then show the highest CPA, ROAS and max CPC (the most you can pay per click) you can accept. **
  • Ad cost per sale should be compared against average order value. A 10x ROAS target allows ad cost of one tenth of the order value. (2025)

The 10% rule

1 min

  • Set ad spend at up to 10% of product price per sale. A $200 product supports up to $20 of ad spend per customer; a $1,000 product supports up to $100. **

  • Keep customer acquisition cost (CAC) at a maximum of 10% of product price. For a $1,000 product, up to $100 of ad spend per sale.

  • The 10% CAC limit is a rough average rule. Real acquisition costs fluctuate between campaigns, so judge it over many sales rather than one.

  • Maximum customer acquisition cost is about 10% of the sale price, which is the 10x ROAS target. On a $1,000 product, spend up to $100 per customer. (2023)

  • Source A 2022 budget per sale:

    1. Set the minimum advertised price (MAP) (the lowest price you may show in ads) at least 2X wholesale cost (what you pay the supplier). For example: $500 wholesale means a $1,000 MAP.
    2. Aim for a target ROAS of 10X, so every $1 on ads returns at least $10 in sales. For example: a $1,000 product allows up to $100 ad spend per sale.
    3. Typical cost structure per sale: COGS about 50% of selling price, a 2.9% transaction fee (the card payment charge), shipping covered, leaving about 10% of the price for traffic. The source does not list the remainder in detail, so check it against your own costs.

    **

  • Use a minimum ROAS of 10x when budgeting ads for a product. Leave margin for shipping and the merchant fee (the fee the payment provider takes). **

  • Low ROAS on lower-priced products has three possible causes: ads not targeted tightly enough, keywords (the search words that trigger your ad) too broad, and low website conversion rate. **

  • Review all three causes before deciding a niche is wrong. Focus first on costs you control, such as shipping rates and ad spend. **

Revenue per click and maximum CPC

1 min

Core formulas

1 min
  • Revenue per click (RPC, the average money each click brings in) = total conversion value (revenue) ÷ total clicks. It is the basis for all bid calculations. A bid is the price you offer for each click.
  • Predicted revenue per click comes from historical performance data (past results) for each product. Combined with target ROAS, it determines the maximum CPC you can profitably bid.
  • Target CPC (maximum bid) = Revenue per click ÷ Target ROAS. Divide revenue per click by target ROAS to get the highest CPC (cost per click, what one click costs) that still keeps you profitable.
  • Example: 100 clicks generating $1,000 revenue gives $10 RPC. At a 10x target ROAS the target CPC is $1.
  • Maximum CPC for a target ROAS = RPC ÷ target ROAS. Example: a $1,000 budget earning $2,000 has ROAS 2. With RPC $2, the maximum CPC for ROAS 2 is $1.
  • Source B: Maximum CPC = target CPA × conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy). At a $500 CPA and 1% conversion, break-even max CPC is $5. At a $200 target CPA and 1% conversion, max CPC is $2. **
  • Source B: a higher bid is only profitable if conversion rate rises in step. **
  • Paying above target max CPC pushes profit below your target per sale. A falling conversion rate makes existing bids unprofitable. **
  • Bid, conversion rate and ROAS are linked. A high bid stops being profitable when conversion falls too low. Check every bid level against the conversion rate it actually produces. **

Worked case: competitor and optimised advertiser

1 min
  • Competitor (a rival store) baseline: $1,000 budget at $1 CPC gives 1,000 clicks. At a 1% conversion rate that is 10 conversions. At $200 customer value, that is $2,000 revenue, ROAS 2 and RPC $2. The highest CPC that still holds ROAS 2 is $1.
  • Optimised advertiser with the same $1,000 budget: shopping title (the product name shown in Google Shopping ads) and quality score (Google's rating of how relevant an ad is) lowered the cost to $0.80 CPC, giving 1,250 clicks.
  • Facebook retargeting (showing ads again to past visitors) raised conversion to 1.26%, giving about 16 conversions.
  • The email lift (sales from emails to customers) raised customer value to $220. Revenue is about $3,520, and the maximum CPC for ROAS 2 becomes $1.41.
  • The source quotes $3,500 ÷ $1,000 as 4. The correct result is 3.5. Use exact figures and recalculate with your own revenue before setting a bid target.
  • The competitor and optimised advertiser both had a $1,000 budget. The optimised one reached about $3,500 revenue, with a much higher ROAS. The source gives a stated 76% increase from better titles, dynamic retargeting (showing each visitor the products they viewed) and email opt-ins (people who agree to get your emails). On the given figures, the increase is 75%.
  • The gap made it hard for the competitor to match the bid.

KPI baseline sheets

3 min

Baseline procedure per product

  • Before running paid ads, know three numbers for each product:

    • COGS
    • break-even ROAS
    • break-even cost per purchase

  • Procedure to calculate the KPI baseline for a product:

    1. Add COGS (product price plus shipping).
    2. Add average order value.
    3. Read the break-even KPIs. These are the minimum ROAS and maximum CPA that avoid a loss.
    4. Set a profit target (e.g. 10% or 15%) and read the profit-target KPIs.
    5. Compare live campaign ROAS and CPA against these numbers before you decide to scale (spend more), hold or kill (stop) the product.

  • Build the KPI sheet in this order:

    1. Enter average order value, average fee % and average COGS including shipping per order.
    2. Enter the daily cost of running the business. The sheet's value is total monthly cost ÷ monthly orders (÷ means divided by): 400 ÷ 420.
    3. Enter the profit target %.
    4. Calculate break-even CPP (cost per purchase). Then calculate the profit-target CPP by subtracting profit target % × AOV.
    5. Enter add-to-cart %, reached-checkout % and purchased %. These are the funnel rates: the share of visitors who reach each step.
    6. Work backwards from CPP to CPIC, then CPATC, then CPVC, using the funnel rates.
    7. Calculate break-even ROAS and profit-target ROAS.
    8. If the ad account is not in USD, enter how many units of your currency equal 1 USD.
    9. Compare the goal CPC with actual CPC and funnel rates.

  • Break-even KPIs cover the costs of the sale and leave no profit.

  • Profit-target KPIs are the stricter limits to aim for.

  • Each funnel-stage cost comes from the next stage's cost multiplied by the conversion rate between them. The funnel is the path from seeing a product to buying it.

  • Higher conversion at any stage allows a higher cost at the stage above.

Funnel formulas (KPI sheet)

  • CPP means cost per purchase. Profit Target CPP = Breakeven CPP − (profit target % × AOV). Example: 36.65 − (0.15 × 60) = 36.65 − 9.00 = 27.65.
  • CPIC means cost per initiate checkout (someone starts checkout). Cost Per Initiate Checkout (CPIC) = CPP × (purchased % ÷ reached checkout %). Example: 36.65 × (2 ÷ 4) = 18.32 at breakeven; 27.65 × (2 ÷ 4) = 13.82 at the profit target.
  • CPATC means cost per add to cart (someone adds the product to their cart). Cost Per Add to Cart (CPATC) = CPIC × (reached checkout % ÷ add to cart %). Example: 18.32 × (4 ÷ 6) = 12.22 at breakeven; 13.82 × (4 ÷ 6) = 9.22 at the profit target.
  • CPVC means cost per view content (someone looks at the product page). Cost Per View Content (CPVC) = CPATC × add to cart %. Example: 12.22 × 0.06 = 0.73 at breakeven; 9.22 × 0.06 = 0.55 at the profit target.

Reading results and acting on them

2 min

  • Compare actual ROAS with break-even ROAS and target ROAS.

    • Above target: performing well.
    • Between target and break-even: profitable, but short of goals.
    • Below break-even: losing money.

  • Calculate break-even ROAS before launching any ads. It is the reference number for profit or loss. Compare every campaign, keyword and product against it. A keyword is a word that triggers your search ad.

  • Break-even ROAS depends on each product's COGS and fees. Calculate it per product or per product category, not one number for the whole store. You can use one spreadsheet for all products, or group products by category.

  • Campaigns below break-even lose money on every order. Fix the funnel or remove the product.

  • Campaigns above break-even but below the profit target need optimisation before you scale. Optimisation means improving settings to work better.

  • Judge an ad set against break-even cost per purchase. An ad set is a group of ads with shared settings. Use ad-level data later for more precise decisions.

  • Use the break-even cost per purchase to set kill rules: rules for when to switch a campaign off.

  • Know four numbers before you review ad performance:

    • target cost per sale
    • target ROAS
    • break-even ROAS
    • break-even cost per sale Work them out at least roughly. Without a target, report data means nothing. **

  • Track cost per product and cost per creative alongside ad spend. A creative is the ad's picture, video or text. This shows which products and creative types cost the most to support.

  • True ROAS = attributable revenue ÷ (Meta ad spend + cost to produce the creatives). Attributable revenue means the sales the ads can be credited with. Adding creative cost gives a more accurate return than ad spend alone.

  • If ad ROAS is at or above target but profit is still low, the problem is unit economics. Unit economics means profit on one single order. The fix is not in the ads.

  • A ROAS of 2.6-3 on the same margins was reported to give about 35% profit. A ROAS of 2-2.5 with break-even of 1.3-1.5 gave about 15-20% profit.

  • Example: a ROAS of 3.5-4.5 on CAD 1,000 daily spend and CAD 3,500 sales gives a stated net daily profit of CAD 1,100-1,200. Net means after all costs.

  • Winter ad spend of about $5,000 produced about $18,000 in sales in November. The brand roughly broke even overall. This was accepted because the strategy was long term.

  • Source C example: $1 spent that returns $1.60 gives a ROAS of 1.6.

  • Read ad cost per conversion against the margin per sale. Only then can you decide whether a product sells profitably with ads.

    • your average cost per click
    • your site conversion rate
    • The value of one sale These decide whether the campaign can be profitable. Without them, you cannot calculate break-even or the ROAS you need.

Break-even ROAS: definition and rules

3 min
  • Break-even ROAS is the minimum return on ad spend an offer needs to avoid a loss. An offer is what you sell at a given price. Any ROAS above it makes money. Any ROAS below it loses money.
  • Break-even ROAS is the point where a campaign is neither profitable nor loss-making. Above it means profit. Below it means a loss.
  • Break-even ROAS is the point where revenue equals COGS plus ad spend. Profit turns from negative to positive above it.
  • Break-even ROAS is the return at which a campaign covers its costs. The mentor gave no fixed target above it. They described a ROAS of about 1.7 as acceptable and about 3 as great.
  • Before running ads for an offer, compare its break-even ROAS with the ROAS you expect or measure. Keep ads running only while measured ROAS stays above break-even. Do this for every offer, including bundles and buy-X-get-Y offers.
  • Calculate the minimum ROAS for each campaign, keyword and other segment before launch. Let that minimum guide every bidding and budget decision.
  • Compare ROAS with break-even. Above it, the campaign is profitable. Below it, the campaign is at a loss.
  • Know the break-even ROAS for every candidate product before testing. It sets the minimum return a product's ads must generate.
  • Break-even ROAS depends on margin. Always compare ROAS with break-even before you decide whether to keep or cut an ad set.
  • Break-even ROAS changes with the product's margin and costs. Calculate your own floor for each product, not another store's figure.

Break-even CPA and cost per purchase

2 min
  • Margin = what you keep from a sale after paying costs. Break-even CPA (CPA = cost per acquisition, what one customer costs you in ads) = the most you can pay in ads for one customer without losing money. Formula: selling price − product cost − other per-order costs. Example: $10 product cost and a selling price of at least $30 gives a margin of about $20, so break-even CPA was about $20 to $21.
  • Break-even CPA = selling price − (product cost + shipping cost). Example: $30 price with $10 cost gives $20. $40 price with $10 product cost gives $30.
  • Break-even cost per purchase = the most you can spend to get one customer. Formula: selling price − product cost − shipping cost. Example: $30 price, $10 product plus shipping gives $20. A $40 price with a $10 product cost gave $30 before shipping.
  • Target cost per purchase = the most you can spend on ads to get one sale and still profit. Example: $150 price, $35 COGS (cost of goods sold, what the product costs you) gives $115 (price − COGS − other per-unit costs as the sheet defines them). Any cost per purchase above it is unprofitable.
  • Cost per purchase = listing price (the price shown on the product page) − COGS. Set the cost-per-purchase and ROAS targets inside the ad account (the account where your ads run) from your actual margin.
  • Target CPA in the Ecom Margin Calculator (a calculator tool) = dollar margin per order (listing price − total cost). Cost per purchase in the ad account must stay below it to be profitable. Example: $200 listing, $60 total cost gives a target CPA of $140.
  • Profitable target CPA: $70 or less when the listing price is $100 and total cost is $30.
  • Break-even CPA check for a product: compare CPA with profit margin per sale. With a $30 margin, target a CPA of roughly $16 to $20 at most. For VAT-adjusted markets (VAT = sales tax added to the price in some countries), add the extra VAT, for example 20%, into the break-even calculation before running ads to that market.
  • Calculate break-even CPA before judging whether an ad set (a group of ads inside a campaign) is working.
  • Plan customer acquisition costs (what one new customer costs) of roughly $15 to $20 per customer on paid platforms, with some estimates as low as $10. Product margin must be high enough to cover these costs.

1 min

    2. Subtract supplier cost and other costs per sale.
    3. Decide your profit target. The remaining amount is what you can pay Google per sale, which is your target CPA.
    4. Repeat for each product, since margins differ.

Measuring what pays: cost per reply, cost per customer, cost per dollar of sale

1 min

  • An ad producing replies at an average of $0.41 for two years is the benchmark that new ads must beat.
  • Final conclusions about an ad should rest on cost per customer or cost per dollar of sale, not on the number of responses. An ad can bring many worthless replies, while another brings fewer valuable ones.
  • Use sample requests and coupon counts to measure interest. But judge each lead's (a person who may buy) final value by cost per customer. Cheap inquiries that never convert are not savings.

Break-even sales and targets for a first campaign

1 min
  • Source A's break-even example: $100 revenue ÷ $25 net profit per order = 4. Every $1 of ad spend needs $4 in sales to break even. So $25 of spend needs $100 in sales (merged: true duplicates, two identical entries combined).
  • Give this break-even ROAS to your paid advertising agency as the target. Any campaign running below it is losing money at that margin.
  • The same break-even comparison applies to every campaign and offer. Compare ROAS with break-even before deciding to keep or cut.

Further details

1 min
  • To calculate break-even ROAS:
    1. Determine revenue target (e.g., $1,000).
    2. Calculate COGS in dollars (e.g., $400) and percentage (e.g., 40%).
    3. Calculate profit before ads = Revenue − COGS (e.g., $1,000 − $400 = $600) and as percentage (e.g., 60%).
    4. Calculate break-even ROAS = Revenue ÷ Profit (e.g., $1,000 ÷ $600 = 1.67x).
    5. ROAS above this number is profitable; below is a loss.
  • Calculating and using the target cost per sale:
    1. Calculate the target cost per sale using the traffic calculator.
    2. Write down the resulting numbers.
    3. Compare these targets with the actual figures in the financial tracking system.
    4. Check profit on a day-to-day basis against the targets.
