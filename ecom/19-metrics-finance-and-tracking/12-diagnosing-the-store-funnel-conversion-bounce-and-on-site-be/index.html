Diagnosing the store funnel: conversion, bounce and on-site behavior

Section 19.12 · 12 of 23 in this chapter · 32 min read

In short

  • Check your numbers before you change anything: how many visitors come, how many add to cart, and how many buy.
  • Find the one step where the most people leave, and fix that step first.
  • Good ad clicks with no sales usually means the store or offer is weak, not the ad.
  • Judge a store only with enough visitors, about 100 to 200 per day. Fewer visitors give unreliable results.
  • Check each traffic source (where visitors come from) on its own. Never judge the store by one mixed total.
More key points
  • A paid channel should convert at 2.5% or more. Conversion rate is the share of visitors who buy.
  • Bounce rate is the share of visitors who leave after one page. Judge it only on free search visitors, and do not change a new store before paid traffic starts.
  • Wait for a statistically significant result (a difference that is unlikely to be luck) before you pick a winner in an A/B test (two versions shown to different visitors).
  • For upsells and bundles, track the average order value (average money per order), not only how often people accept them.

Verify tracking and data before you judge

3 min

Tracking means the tools that count visits and sales. Check that they work before you trust any number.

  • Record how many visitors arrive each day, how many are organic (free traffic, not paid ads), and where they come from. Do this before you judge conversion.
  • Conversion rate means the share of visitors who buy. If traffic is high but sales are low, check the numbers first. Example: 2,000 visits (1,500 from Google Ads, Google's paid ads, and 500 direct, meaning visitors who typed the address in) should produce dozens of sales at a healthy conversion rate.
  • Blended conversion rates from Shopify (the online store software) or Google Analytics (a free visitor-tracking tool) are misleading. They include bot traffic, which is fake visits from automated programs.
  • A blended store conversion rate also mixes bot traffic, organic searches and repeat visits. So measure each paid channel (a source you pay for) separately. Examples: Google Shopping (Google's product ads), Facebook Dynamic Remarketing (ads that show past visitors the products they viewed) and Google Remarketing (ads that reach past visitors).
  • The conversion rate in the default Shopify dashboard blends all sources. It understates the performance of sources you control, especially Google Ads.
  • Track add-to-cart, checkout and purchase rates in Shopify to find the exact step where shoppers leave. Dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships directly to the customer) is mostly about reading these numbers after each change.
  • Shopify shows average order value (AOV, the average amount per order), add-to-cart percentage, initiate-checkout percentage and session conversion rate in the Analytics section of the dashboard. A session is one visit.
  • Add-to-cart percentage shows how well the landing page (the page an ad sends people to) and the offer work. It measures how many visitors take the first buying step. A weak value points to a page or offer problem before the ads are blamed.
  • Store analytics show which products customers view, add to cart, buy and ask about. Google Ads and Google Analytics data are the most valuable sources for understanding real customer behavior.
  • Shopify analytics show website visitors and the pages they open. You need no extra tool at the start.
  • Review Google Analytics data about every two weeks to track performance trends and make data-based changes instead of guesses.
  • When a store has few sales, compare Google Analytics traffic week over week or month over month since launch. This shows whether the problem is traffic volume or conversion.
  • Analyze traffic by source over 30 days. Sources include paid Shopping, organic social, organic search, referrals and unassigned (visits with no known source). Do this to find where sales come from before you change anything.
  • Review two numbers in analytics: where traffic goes (the most visited pages) and where sales come from (source attribution, meaning which source gets credit for the sale). Together they show which pages to improve and which channels to scale (spend more on).
  • When judging a store, focus first on high-intent actions. These are strong signs a buyer is close: add to cart, initiate checkout (start the checkout) and completed purchase. Traffic source matters mainly when you find a tracking problem or unwanted traffic.
  • Conversion rate was the top priority for merchants in the audience poll. Track AOV and customer lifetime value (how much one customer spends over time) alongside it when you improve the cart.
  • Track revenue per visitor and per sale. A store with about 8,711 visitors and only 8 sales is a warning sign that ad spend is not reaching buyers.
  • Unverified industry figures are often quoted. Examples: average cart abandonment (shoppers who leave with items in the cart) around 70%, and roughly 70% of website traffic eventually returns to the homepage. Use them only as rough benchmarks (reference numbers). Compare them with your own checkout and analytics data.

Sample size and traffic bursts

1 min

Sample size means how many visitors you have. Too few visitors make results unreliable.

  • Judge a store funnel (the path from visit to purchase) only with at least 150 to 200 visitors. 50 to 100 visitors is too little data to conclude anything.
  • Judge a store's conversion rate only after about 100 to 200 or more visitors per day. 30 to 50 visitors are too few for a reliable conclusion.
  • If traffic is below that level, run ads for two more days before you change the landing page.
  • Check the store after 100 to 150 visitors. If add-to-cart is still low, change the offer. If it stays low after several offers, switch to the next product.
  • Ad platforms send an initial traffic burst (a sudden spike of visits) when a campaign starts. Remove it from the visitor count before you calculate add-to-cart rates.
  • If you cannot separate the burst exactly, an estimate based on the timing of the burst is acceptable.
  • Launch ads six to seven hours before the planned running start time. The initial burst then happens at a known moment, so you can exclude it. Once the burst is identified, all other visits count as real visitors.

Core store rates and benchmarks

2 min

A benchmark is a reference number to compare your store against.

Formulas

  • Add-to-cart rate = add-to-cart events / landing page visitors (sessions that reach the product page).
  • Example: 100 visitors and 4 add-to-carts gives 4%, which is the minimum benchmark.
  • Per Session Value (PSV) = revenue / sessions.
  • Average Order Value (AOV) = revenue / transactions.
  • Conversion rate = transactions / sessions.
  • Check: $57,286 / 50,970 sessions = $1.12 PSV, and $57,286 / 1,019 transactions = $56.22 AOV. These match the control figures in the case. A control is the original version that a test compares against.
  • Revenue per session is the metric that matters most in conversion rate optimization (CRO, improving the share of visitors who buy). It matters more than conversion rate alone.
  • A test that raises conversion rate can still lose money if it attracts lower-spending customers or lowers AOV. Measure every A/B test (a test comparing two versions of a page) against revenue-per-session improvement.

Benchmarks for add-to-cart and conversion

ATC means add to cart. LPV means landing page view (the page loaded for the visitor). CTR means click-through rate (the share of people who see an ad and click it).

  • In dropshipping, the add-to-cart rate should be roughly double the purchase conversion rate, because shipping times are long.
  • Use benchmarks as a guide for where to look, not as a verdict on their own. Each benchmark is valid only with enough traffic behind it.
  • A slow traffic week with a healthy conversion rate points to a traffic problem, not a product or checkout problem.
  • A store with 20,000 visits and no sales has a conversion issue on the site, not an ad issue.
  • Strong top-of-funnel traffic (the first stage, where visitors arrive) with no add-to-carts usually means the bottleneck (the slowest step) is the website.

Reading the funnel stage by stage

4 min

A funnel is the set of steps from ad click to sale. Each step loses some visitors.

  • Diagnose a weak store in this order: ads shown, clicks, visits, add-to-cart, checkout starts and completed purchases.
  • The largest drop between two stages shows where the problem sits. Fix that stage before you change anything else.
  • Example: many checkout starts with no purchases points to checkout or trust problems. It does not point to ad targeting (choosing who sees the ad).
  • Analyze the funnel in this order: pre-click ad metrics, then website and landing page metrics, then checkout, then customer support and supply chain (how products travel from supplier to customer). If the ads perform well, the problem is usually further down the funnel.
  • In the Shopify report, check sessions per landing page, add-to-cart rate and conversion rate. This finds the leak between ad click and purchase before you change creatives (the ad images and text).
  • A typical funnel drops about half at each stage, from ad click to add-to-cart to final checkout. Shipping costs are the most common reason people leave at checkout.
  • Check funnel ratios per product and per country: CTR, CPC (cost per click), CPM (cost per 1,000 views), conversion rate, add-to-cart ratio and initiate-checkout ratio.
  • The add-to-cart rate depends mostly on what happens on the store after the click: site quality, product and offer. It does not depend on the traffic source alone. Judge it per product and per store, not per ad platform.
  • Read the add-to-cart rate used to judge a product page in the Shopify backend (the admin area only the store owner sees). Check that the page structure is good enough before you copy it.
  • In Business Manager (Meta's tool for managing ads), check add-to-cart and initiate-checkout rates per ad asset (each ad image or video). Compare them with the Shopify dashboard. A drop at one step shows where the funnel breaks.
  • A profitable funnel needs two healthy parts. The first is strong Meta metrics (Meta means Facebook and Instagram ads; CPM, CTR, CPC and cost per purchase). The second is a website that converts visitors into buyers. A good website cannot make expensive traffic profitable. Cheap traffic cannot save a website that does not convert.

Procedure: funnel check with a 3-step order

  1. Check the number of visitors arriving at the store first.
  2. Check whether those visitors add products to the cart.
  3. Check whether people are buying.
  • Use these results to decide whether the price, the product and the website are converting. Look at all three together.

Procedure: diagnosing a failed campaign

  1. Check whether tracking is correct. Compare the ad platform numbers with the store analytics, for example Shopify.
  2. Check CTR and CPC. A very high CPC points to a creative or audience problem.
  3. Check the add-to-cart rate against the 4% benchmark. If it is low, the creative, offer or landing page is the suspect.
  4. If add-to-carts are fine, check the step from add-to-cart to checkout: shipping costs, trust signals (proof the store is safe, such as reviews) and whether the checkout works at all.
  5. If people reach checkout but do not buy, test the checkout yourself and review the purchase step.

Four-scenario diagnosis from the tracker

The tracker is a sheet where you log your daily numbers. ROAS means return on ad spend (revenue divided by ad cost). Break-even ROAS is the point where ads pay for themselves.

  1. Ads and website both healthy: all metrics in green, ROAS well above break-even, stable for 5 to 7 days. Action: scale (spend more).
  2. Ads and website both weak: high CPM, expensive clicks, few or no sales, everything in red for 5 to 7 days. Action: improve both parts of the funnel.
  3. Ads strong (good CPM, CTR and CPC) but website weak (few or no add-to-carts, low conversion rate such as 0.5%). The bottleneck is the website.
  4. Website strong but ads weak (high CPM, CPC near $6). The funnel cannot scale until traffic cost is fixed.

Examples from the same tracker

  • Optimal funnel: $100 daily Meta spend, ROAS of 4.5 (versus break-even 1.30), 10 add-to-carts, 3 purchases, and a cost per purchase below the $115 target.
  • Weak funnel: $100 daily spend with high CPM, traffic that costs too much, some add-to-carts but few or no sales, and all values in red. Action: fix both the ads and the website.
  • Website bottleneck: $100 spend with good CPM, CTR and CPC, but zero or very few sales and a conversion rate around 0.5%. Action: focus on the website. Examples: trust elements (such as customer testimonials), the chosen price point, and whatever stops people from buying.
  • Ads bottleneck: a website with many add-to-carts, purchases, a positive ROAS and a good conversion rate, but terrible Meta metrics such as high CPM and a cost per click near $6. Action: the funnel cannot scale until traffic cost goes down, even though the website is good.

Add-to-cart and checkout drop-offs

2 min
  • Check the funnel when add-to-carts are low, or when there is a large drop between add-to-carts and initiate-checkouts. These drop-offs point to causes in the store or offer.
  • If add-to-carts are at or above 4% but there are zero sales, the problem lies after add-to-cart: checkout, shipping costs, trust or checkout setup. A problem in the checkout area is far more likely than a problem in the ad or product page.
  • Track add-to-carts alongside sales. A store can show cart activity without enough purchases. In one example, four carts led to two sales. That pointed to the checkout experience and trust factors as the next place to look.
  • Add-to-cart and checkout activity can be high while sales are zero. The speaker called this unusual and worth investigating. Add-to-cart to checkout conversion of around 71% was considered strong.
  • Use the 71% figure as the strong end of add-to-cart to checkout conversion. The leak table treats under 30% as cart friction. So below 30% is a clear problem, and 71% is a clear strength.
  • When add-to-cart, checkout and click-through are all healthy but sales are still missing, the problem is more likely the product or the ad angle (the main idea of the ad).
  • Healthy add-to-cart and checkout rates suggest the landing page works. That moves the problem toward the offer, the creative or the product.
  • When CTR is high but add-to-cart and checkout ratios are low, the product page fails to hold the visitor. Typical causes: an unattractive offer, a weak product description, or any reason that makes visitors leave before adding to cart.
  • If ads drive add-to-cart activity but few visitors complete checkout, the problem may be the offer rather than the ad. Test a clearer or stronger offer first.
  • When an ad gets clicks but no add-to-carts or purchases, the page or checkout is a likely cause. Test the same ad again after a fix. This helps separate an ad problem from a checkout or page problem.
  • A product page that gets clicks but does not convert needs work on the product page: price, bundles or copy (the written text).
  • If content views are far below link clicks, the landing page may load too slowly. Visitors leave before the product page registers. Content views (times the product page loaded and was seen) should be somewhat lower than link clicks. A big gap is a warning sign for site speed.
  • A good add-to-cart rate with zero sales points to a problem after the cart, not in the ad or the product.
  • Check each step against its benchmark before you decide. The stage with the largest gap to its benchmark is where you start.

Site versus ad: where the problem sits

4 min

Core split between ad and store

  • Check CTR and CPM first in Facebook ads. High CTR with no sales points to the offer, not the ad.
  • Track CTR for ads and conversion rate for the website separately. A good CTR with a low conversion rate points to the website or offer. A poor CTR points to the ad.
  • A strong CTR with no sales means the ad is working. The store or offer is the problem.
  • If CTR is at least 1.5% and CPC is below 1 USD but there are still no sales, the problem is the store. If CTR or CPC are off target, change the ads.
  • If ads reach a CTR above 1.5% but produce no add-to-carts or sales, the problem is likely the offer. Test different offers before you change anything else.
  • A good CTR of around 3% with many add-to-carts but no sales points to a problem after the add-to-cart step, such as currency or checkout.
  • After you have a good CTR and a good offer, check the store conversion rate. Then decide whether to scale or move on, in that order.
  • Apart from the creative, the only other lever you control is the post-click part: landing page and funnel. Turning more visitors into buyers helps you win the same audience as competitors.
  • When ad CTR and CPC are acceptable but sales are missing, check the add-to-cart and initial checkout rates on the store.

Hook, desire and interest

A hook is the opening of an ad that grabs attention. Desire means the wish to buy.

  • Diagnose a funnel problem in this order. First check that attention (hook rate, how many people keep watching the start of the ad) is strong. If clicks are good but sales are poor, the problem is in desire.
  • Possible causes of weak desire: an ad that does not sell the idea, unexpected fees, a price that is too high, a product page that does not sell well, a functional problem on the page, or a lack of trust.
  • A high click-through rate with poor sales shows real interest. The next step is to fix the product page, checkout fees or trust signals, not the ad.

Case: a launch with clicks and no sales

  • A product launched with a strong hook and a strong landing page. It got €190 in ad spend over four days without a single sale. Sessions were about 50 per day, then rose to 100 to 120 per day after new ad sets (groups of ads with their own audience) were added. Sales fell to zero.
  • Before the drop, the account had ROAS of about 2.2 and 4.4x return on ad spend, with a click-through rate of about 2 to 3%.
  • The first step was to check that the page and checkout worked correctly. Lucky Orange, a tool that records screen recordings of visitors, was used to watch how people moved on the site.

Low conversion despite traffic

  1. Check which keywords drive impressions (times an ad is shown) and clicks in your Google Shopping campaign keyword report.
  2. Search Google for the product yourself. Analyze what competitors show: messaging, offers and product photos.
  3. Compare your product page with competitor pages: pricing, descriptions, images, shipping and returns.
  4. Fix product page conversion elements, not just the ads.
  • A low conversion rate on a product page often means the traffic is at an awareness stage (the visitor does not yet know they want the product), and the page does not address that stage. Check the traffic source and stage before you change the page itself.
  • A high bounce rate (share of visitors who leave after one page) on a high-traffic page can point to a gap between what visitors expected and what the page shows.

Unexplained sales drop

  1. Check whether the drop is seasonal or market-wide. Compare with previous years and with competitors.
  2. Look at what competitors are doing, including new products, better offers and stronger traffic.
  3. Check your own traffic, conversion and offer for changes. Fix what has shifted.
  • When a profitable store turns unprofitable after months of work, find the root cause with data before you change things. Persistent problems usually come from specific parts of the funnel.
  • Before you change the store, check whether a poor result is a traffic problem or a conversion problem. Compare it with the previous period.
  • When you ask a mentor or coach to diagnose weak ad performance, send screenshots of the ad results and screenshots of the Shopify analytics showing how visitors behave on the store. This lets the reviewer see whether the problem is in the ads or in the store.

Advertorial route

An advertorial is an ad that looks like an article. It sends visitors to an offer page.

  • Check how many visitors move from the advertorial to the offer page.
  • If that number is low, rewrite the advertorial copy to strengthen the argument.
  • Alternatively, run a split test (send half the traffic to each version) of sending ad traffic directly to the offer page against the advertorial route. Keep the stronger one.

Traffic source, country and product breakdowns

1 min

Traffic source

1 min
  • Measure conversion rate (the share of visits that end in a purchase) separately for each traffic source (where the visitor came from). Use Google Shopping, Facebook Dynamic Remarketing and organic (free search visits). Do not use blended figures (one mixed total for all sources).
  • Each paid channel should reach 2.5% or more conversion. These channels are Google Shopping, Facebook Dynamic Remarketing (ads that show past viewers the products they already looked at) and Google Remarketing (ads shown again to past visitors).
  • If one traffic source converts well and another does not, leave the working source alone. Focus the investigation on the weak one.
  • Compare conversion rate by traffic source in Google Analytics (a free Google tool that tracks visits) to see which source is which.
  • Measure conversion rate and ROAS (return on ad spend: money earned back for each unit spent on ads) per traffic source, especially Google Ads, to see what is working.
  • Track ROAS, conversion rate, bounce rate, cost per sale and spend per product group together. (2023)
  • A high bounce rate can be normal for product-page traffic that already knows what it wants. (2023)
  • Judge the bounce rate by paid traffic segments when a new store launches. Do not change the store before paid traffic starts.

Country

1 min
  • Google Analytics shows the country distribution of visitors. This tells you which currency and pricing defaults to use.
  • Procedure: check conversion rate per country in Shopify (the online store platform used).
    1. Go to Analytics.
    2. Open Reports and then Online store sessions (sessions are single visits).
    3. Click View report.
    4. Set the columns to Sessions, Conversions (converted sessions) and Conversion rate.
    5. Set the time grouping to None so the data is not split by hour or day.
    6. Choose the Country grouping and compare the rows.
    7. Filter or drill down to Region and City to see which area converts best.
  • For market analysis, the usable columns in the Online store sessions report are Sessions, Conversions, Conversion rate, Country, Region and City.
  • Filter these columns to see which country, region or city performs best.
  • After you find a strong country, drill down to region and city.
  • Put more ad budget into the best-performing city, for example Melbourne in Australia, rather than spreading spend evenly across the country.
  • The Markets overview (Shopify's view of each country market) shows each market's share of total sales, sales and conversion rate for the last 30 days.
  • The Markets overview also counts how many countries are not in any market (209 in the example).
  • Check funnel ratios (the share of buyers who pass each step from visit to purchase) per product and per country, not only in total.

Product

1 min
  • When two or more products run on one store, give each product its own campaign and UTM tag (a label on a link that shows where the click came from).
  • Compare add-to-cart and initiate-checkout counts per product alongside purchases.
  • This shows at which funnel step each product loses buyers. A shared campaign cannot show this, because it can credit the same purchase to several products.
  • When running several product pages or stores from the same TikTok account, track each page separately to see which product converts.
  • Focus price and page optimization on the top 20% of product pages by traffic, since they drive most of the results.
  • Google Analytics > Engagement > Pages and screens shows which pages already get the most traffic.
  • Use Pages and screens to decide which product descriptions to rewrite first.

Bounce rate and time on page

1 min

Definitions

1 min
  • Bounce rate is the percentage of visitors who leave after visiting only that single page, without further interaction (clicks, scrolls or visits to other pages).
  • Average Time on Page shows per URL (web address) how long visitors stay on each product page in your store.
  • Bounce rate is the share of visitors who leave without further interaction.
  • An average time on site well below 10 seconds together with a very high bounce rate means the visitors are the wrong audience for the offer.
  • Google Analytics is used to check the bounce rate of the store. This is the share of visitors who leave the site without any action.

Thresholds (note the different sources and years)

1 min
Context Threshold Meaning Source ids
General store 60% or lower Healthy
General store 80% to 90% Most visitors leave right away; the landing page needs a fix
Organic landing pages 50% or below Good
Organic landing pages 80% to 90% Page problem or mismatch between page and search terms it ranks for
New store with paid traffic not yet started 78% to 82% with average session of 53 seconds Not a reason to change the store before paid traffic starts
  • Use the 2021-2022 organic thresholds when you evaluate landing pages (the first page a visitor sees) from organic search.
  • Use the 2018 general thresholds only as an older reference.
  • Judge user interaction only on organic traffic.
  • Paid traffic, especially from Google Shopping, usually has a higher bounce rate and different time on page.
  • Filter paid traffic out by Source/Medium (the tag that shows where a visit came from and how) before you evaluate a page.
  • Time on page under 30 seconds is not good for organic landing pages.
  • Time on page above 30 seconds is acceptable, and longer is better.
  • A very high bounce rate on a high-traffic page, with many visitors leaving within about 10 seconds, points to a problem with the offer.
  • It can also point to a gap between expectations and what the page shows.
  • Behavior data does not always reveal a problem. Use it to guide the investigation, not as proof.
  • Before judging a high bounce rate on a new store, check which search terms and countries Google Search Console (Google's tool showing which searches bring visits) shows for the store.
  • Traffic from unrelated search terms or distant countries often explains a poor bounce rate better than the bounce figure alone.

Procedure: diagnosing a high bounce rate in Google Analytics

1 min
  1. Open Behavior.
  2. Open Content.
  3. Find the pages with the highest bounce rates.
  4. Check which traffic sources send visitors to those pages.

Procedure: checking user interaction on landing pages

1 min
  1. Open Google Analytics in the default browser.
  2. Load the custom report and open it under Custom reports in the left menu.
  3. Filter by secondary dimension Source/Medium and keep only organic traffic.
  4. Compare bounce rate and time on page per landing page against the thresholds of 50% bounce rate and 30 seconds time on page.
  5. For pages above 80% bounce rate or under 30 seconds, check search intent match, above-the-fold content (what is visible without scrolling), overlays, readability and interaction points.
  6. Adjust the search terms if the keywords themselves are mismatched.
  7. Re-measure after changes.
  • The custom report supplied with this guide shows bounce rate and time on page for all landing pages.
  • Filter the custom report by the secondary dimension Source/Medium to isolate organic traffic.

Device: mobile versus desktop

1 min
  • Track conversion and return on ad spend by device.
  • Desktop often converts best.
  • Mobile carries most traffic and sales, and many mobile visitors buy later on desktop, so do not switch mobile off.
  • Check Google Analytics bounce rate and session duration, separated by mobile and desktop, before changing the store or ads.
  • Device differences often explain most of the problem.
  • Relative mobile conversion rate = mobile conversion rate / desktop conversion rate x 100.
  • This shows how well the mobile site converts compared with desktop. It can be read without being distorted by differences in campaign sources.

Page speed and reliability

1 min
  • One second of speed improvement can add about 7,000 USD in daily revenue for a store with 100,000 USD daily revenue.
  • The target First Contentful Paint (FCP) (the time until the first content appears on screen) is under 3 seconds.
  • FCP is the main speed metric to watch, because it reflects the first view a customer sees.
  • Page speed scores in the Shopify admin are less accurate than GTmetrics (an outside speed-test tool).
  • Use an outside tool to check page speed before making design decisions.
  • PageSpeed Insights (Google's speed tool) shows field data (speed data from real visitors) only when the Chrome User Experience Report (Google's dataset of real visitor speeds) has enough data for that exact URL.
  • Otherwise it shows an origin summary (an average for the whole domain). Check individual page URLs to see page-specific results.
  • Run WebPageTest (a speed-test tool) at least three times and compare the first view runs.
  • Treat a slow metric as a real problem only if it appears consistently across the runs, not only in a single outlier run.
  • Treat uptime monitoring (automatic checks that the store is online) as business insurance, not an expense.
  • A store that is down for days loses sales unnoticed. Shopify outages can happen even on a cloud platform.

Google Analytics and Shopify page reports

1 min
  • Google Analytics > left sidebar > Behavior > Site Content shows which URLs visitors view.
  • Use Site Content to decide which product pages to optimize first.
  • In Google Analytics, go to Sidebar > Behavior > Site Content > All Pages to view detailed page-level performance metrics displayed in red columns.
  • Menu names differ between versions of Google Analytics, so use the path your account shows.
  • Google Analytics shows which pages get views.
  • Google Analytics also shows a basic conversion rate per page.
  • Google Analytics is used to compare page versions at the end of a test.
  • Relevant page metrics are bounce rate, time on page, exit rate (share of visits that end on that page), conversion rate and page value (revenue a page helps generate).

Procedure: compare results after a test

1 min
  1. Open the two page versions in Google Analytics.
  2. Compare bounce rate, time on page, exit rate, conversion rate and page value for each version over the test period.
  3. Check whether the difference is statistically significant before making a decision.

On-page behavior: heatmaps and recordings

1 min
  • A heatmap (a colored map of where visitors scroll and click) shows how far visitors scroll.
  • A heatmap also shows where visitors click.
  • It tells you whether key content, such as reviews or the add-to-cart button, is actually being seen.
  • In Lucky Orange heatmaps (a tool that makes heatmaps and recordings), deep orange areas show where most visitors click.
  • If a non-clickable section receives heavy clicks (deep orange), visitors probably expect it to be interactive. Or the design confuses their intentions.
  • Use Lucky Orange screen recordings to see how shoppers move through the page and checkout when sales are missing.

Advertorials and landing-page routes

1 min
  • An advertorial is an ad written like an article. Use this decision logic when it underperforms.
  • Step 1: check how many visitors move from the advertorial to the offer page.
  • Step 2: if that number is low, rewrite the advertorial copy to strengthen the argument.
  • Step 3 (alternative): split test (A/B test) sending ad traffic directly to the offer page against the advertorial route. Keep the stronger one.
  • Google Optimize could run headline tests on advertorial and sales pages, to find the headline that grabs the most attention.
  • Google Optimize was installed as a snippet (a small piece of code added to the site) placed after the PageFly loader (the page builder app) in Shopify.
  • Google Optimize was discontinued in 2023. A/B testing described above needs another current testing tool, such as a Shopify app or a native testing platform.

Upsell metrics

1 min
  • Track the dollar value each upsell adds, not just its conversion rate. An upsell is an offer for a higher or extra product.
  • An offer that converts less often can still produce more revenue if its price is much higher.
  • AOV (average order value) is the key metric for upsells and cross-sells (related product offers).
  • Upsells and cross-sells raise revenue per existing customer rather than acquiring new ones.
  • Track AOV to judge whether an offer works.
  • Track AOV to measure the effect of bundles (several items sold together), cross-sells and upsells.
  • In the example, AOV rose from $40 to $70 with a two-unit bundle.
  • In the same example, AOV rose from $39 to $49 with a three-tier bundle ladder (bundle prices at several levels).
  • Example with a single-product store: average order value of 40 USD.
  • After switching to a bundle offer of buy two and get an extra 20% off, two items cost about 70 USD.
  • AOV rises from 40 USD to about 70 USD.
  • Shipping per unit also falls with two units, which raises average profit further.
  • The estimate says that about 80% of buyers do not uncheck pre-selected cart extras.
  • About 60% of orders then add 3 to 5 USD.
  • So each order can gain 5 to 10 USD in total from extras.
  • The estimate says that a cart upsell app such as Monster Cart Upsell can lift AOV by about 5 to 10 USD.
  • With extensive customization, the lift can reach 20 to 30 USD.
  • These are the estimate's own figures, not verified results.
  • Conversion rate is not the only measure for upsells.
  • Track AOV and profit per order together.

A/B test metrics and significance

1 min

Core metrics and reading the report

1 min
  • Measure all A/B tests against revenue-per-session improvement to judge profitability accurately.
  • Visually (an A/B testing app) shows the test report with status, start date, last edit, pages, audience, device and allocation at the top.
  • The report lists sessions, transactions, conversion rate, AOV, PSV (per-session value: revenue per visit), revenue, profit and odds to win (the tool's estimated chance a version is better) for the control (original version) and each variation (changed version).
  • The primary goal is marked in the report.
  • Metrics not selected at setup can be added later to the report.
  • For product recommendations and product upsells, Visually splits revenue into assisted revenue (a shopper clicked an upsell item and bought something later).
  • It also splits out direct revenue (the upsell item was bought directly).
  • Best-selling items for control and each variation are listed further down the report.
  • When setting up a Visually A/B test, the goal menu offers many options, including revenue, conversion rate, profit, AOV, subscriptions and returns.
  • You can select several goals, but one must be marked as the primary goal.
  • Events can also be sent to Google Analytics with a checkbox.

Significance

1 min
  • Wait for a statistically significant result before choosing a winner in Visually.
  • Keep the test running while the report shows no statistically significant results yet.
  • A variation with a high chance to win, such as 80% odds to win, is not enough on its own.
  • Do not claim double-digit conversion gains after only a few days of test data.
  • In the matching snapshot, conversion rate was around 2.0% vs 2.05% and not statistically significant.
  • So the reported gain was not visible there.

Case: mobile cart upsell test on a watch-band Shopify store

1 min
  • The test ran from 1 Nov 2024 (last edit 10 Nov 2024, version 1-14 Nov 2024).
  • It used an even 50% split, All Pages, All Visitors and Mobile targeting.
  • Control: 50,970 sessions, 1,019 transactions, AOV $56.22, PSV $1.12, revenue $57,286 and profit $42,105.
  • Variation: 50,753 sessions, 1,039 transactions, AOV $57.15 (+1.7%), PSV $1.17, revenue $59,375 (+3.6%) and profit $43,804 (+4.0%).
  • Visually still showed no statistically significant result and 80% odds to win for the variation.
  • So the test needed more days before a decision.

Lift calculation

1 min
  • Relative conversion lift = (new conversion rate − old conversion rate) ÷ old conversion rate × 100.
  • Example: a rise from 1.85% to 4.25% equals (4.25 − 1.85) ÷ 1.85 × 100 ≈ 129.7%.
  • That is an increase of about 130%.
