Valuing and selling a store: numbers buyers check

Section 19.22 · 22 of 23 in this chapter · 13 min read

In short

  • A store's price is its monthly profit (money left after all costs) times a number called a multiple, plus the value of qualifying assets.
  • Buyers use profit, not sales money coming in, and they look at the last 12 months.
  • Current sources say dropshipping stores sell for about 21 to 35 times monthly profit.
  • Keep clean money records and proof of real visitors, because buyers check everything.
  • Spread your visitors and products across several sources. One source lowers the price.

Valuation basis and formula

1 min

Store valuation (the price a buyer would pay for your store) is monthly net profit (money left after all costs) times a multiple (a number that turns profit into a price), plus the value of qualifying assets (things you own that meet the rules below). Revenue means all money from sales. COGS means cost of goods sold, which is what your products cost you. Expenses means money spent to run the business.

Store valuation    = Monthly Net Profit x Multiple + Assets
Monthly Net Profit = Revenue - (COGS + Expenses)
COGS               = product cost + shipping + extra costs
Expenses           = marketing + software + other business costs
Monthly Net Profit is averaged over the last 12 months.
  • Without an asset line, the formula is Valuation = (Income - Expenses) × Multiple. Income means revenue. The multiple is based on monthly net profit.
  • Assets are added only when they qualify (see Assets in the sale).
  • Net profit per month decides a business's value. A business making no net profit is worth nothing in a sale.
  • Buyers price net profit. A valuation built on revenue is not used.

Multiples: ranges and what moves them

3 min

Sources give different ranges. Use the one that matches your store's profile (what kind of store yours is) and the date of the source.

A broker is a middleman who sells online stores for owners. Dropshipping means you sell products that a supplier ships to the customer, so you do not hold the stock yourself. A traffic source is where your visitors come from.

  • The 2.5x to 3.5x figure uses annual profit (a full year of profit). It is not directly comparable with the monthly multiples. For reference, 30x monthly equals 2.5x annual.
  • Diversified traffic (visitors from many different sources) from Google, email sequences (automatic series of emails sent over time), SEO (free ranking in search results) and a stable TikTok or Snapchat flywheel (a steady loop of traffic that keeps itself going) pushes the broker multiple higher than the single-source range.
  • The multiple rises with track record length (how long the store has run), store size and diversification (spread across sources and products). It falls for short history, a single product, a single traffic source, or a six-month-only average.
  • A store bought and resold every 12 to 18 months lands at the lower end of the range.
  • Buyers treat the multiple as a discount for risk. Relying too much on one product, one traffic source (for example only Facebook or only Google Ads), one supplier, or a declining profit trend lowers it. Twelve months of stable or growing profit raises it.

  • Sites are sometimes valued on the average net profit of the previous three months, so changes need time to show in the figures before a sale.
  • Post-sale support (help you give the buyer after the sale) can change the price. A 90-day post-sale period with two calls per month and unlimited email support can add roughly $50,000 to the sale price, because new buyers overvalue the support.

Worked examples at different multiples:

  • $5,000 monthly net profit at 21x is $105,000. At 35x, the same store is $175,000.
  • $5,000 monthly net profit at 30x is about $150,000.
  • $10,000 monthly net profit at 22x to 30x gives about $220,000 to $300,000.
  • $25,000 monthly net profit sold for about $750,000 through a broker (about 30x).

Broker estimate from margin:

  • EBITDA (roughly, profit before interest, taxes and some accounting costs) equals profit margin times revenue. Profit margin is the share of sales you keep as profit.
  • A 30 percent margin on $1,000,000 in sales gives EBITDA of about $200,000.
  • That implies roughly $450,000 to $700,000 at broker multiples.
  • Higher margins raise the valuation sharply.

Market snapshot, 2018:

  • In 2018, 15 dropshipping stores sold, versus 12 in 2017.
  • The average sale price was $138,000 in 2018, compared with $71,000 in 2017.
  • Sales closed 14% faster, at 62 days versus 73 days.

Net profit averaging

1 min
  • Use a 12-month average of net profit as the default valuation basis. It covers seasonality (sales that rise and fall with the seasons).
  • Shift to a 3- or 6-month average only when a major change makes the 12-month figure unrepresentative of current performance. An example is losing organic traffic to a Google update (a change in how Google ranks sites) or a sudden viral rise.
  • Conflict: says sites are usually valued on the previous three months' average.
  • Conflict: uses the trailing 12 months (the last 12 months) together with the average of the last three months.
  • Apply the 12-month default unless a major change applies.
  • Where a source uses the three-month figure, changes made now need time to appear in it.
  • Track revenue, costs and owner time separately for each store from the start. Owner time means the hours you put in. Many sellers below $100,000 in revenue cannot say how many hours a specific store needs.
  • Buyers use a 12-month average. A store that sold badly in the period before listing (putting the store up for sale) is valued low.

Records and bookkeeping

1 min
  • A buyer will not trust a P&L (profit and loss report: money in, money out, and profit) built from manually typed figures in a spreadsheet.
  • To sell a business, you must prove your P&L is correct with records.
  • When a business sells for six or seven figures or more (that is $100,000 or more), buyers scrutinize (check very closely) the numbers strictly.
  • A small sale may pass with little checking, but larger deals will not.
  • Keep accurate financial records with all expenses documented.
  • Accounting errors destroy buyer trust and reduce the final sale price.
  • Keep accurate financial records from the start. Rebuilding 4- or 5-year-old records is a large amount of work.
  • Accurate records also support decisions such as return on ad spend (how much you earn back for each dollar spent on ads) and profit per product.
  • Track return on ad spend and profit per product. These are needed for smart business decisions, and buyers look for them in financial records.
  • Mistakes in the numbers quickly erode (wear away) buyer trust. Buyers may suspect that information is hidden.
  • Keep records as you go, including historical data for suppliers and VAs (virtual assistants, remote helpers), even when changing work accounts.
  • Use QuickBooks, Xero or FreshBooks (accounting software), or a spreadsheet, to track expenses.
  • For multiple stores, keep each store's books separate (its own money records) so the buyer can see each one's profit.
  • Keep supplier invoices and bills in one Google Drive (online file storage) organized by month, year, or supplier.
  • This archive makes it easier to pull numbers into the P&L when selling.
  • A broker's vetting (checking) includes checking that the Shopify (online store software) sales numbers are genuine. Buyers of a listed store do not need to verify them on their own.
  • Buyers still run their own due diligence (their own checking before buying) on the overall deal.
  • Keep separate, clear financials for each network (group) of related stores.

Metrics to gather before listing

1 min

Gather these before selling:

  1. Hours worked per week
  2. Company start date
  3. Average monthly revenue
  4. Average monthly expenses
  5. Number of products
  6. Unique monthly visitors (different people who visit each month)
  7. Email subscriber count (people on your email list)
  8. Number of social media channels with follower counts (each account and how many followers it has)
  • A business sells best with 18 to 24 months of history.
  • Buyers look at the trailing 12 months of net profit (the last 12 months).

Add-backs

1 min
  • An add-back is an expense that is valid for tax purposes (allowed under tax rules) but not for valuing the business.
  • Examples are a conference trip or a one-off redesign (a redesign that happens only once).
  • List it as an expense on the P&L, and add it back to net profit to be transparent (open) with buyers.
  • Buyers often negotiate add-backs (argue over them). A conference trip is an easy add-back.
  • A site redesign can be argued as a one-off.
  • Buyers may still treat it as a recurring cost (a cost that keeps coming back) if they expect redesigns every couple of years.

Assets in the sale

1 min
  • Assets that can be added to a store sale are inventory (goods you hold in stock, valued at wholesale, the price you pay the maker), equipment such as laptops or photography gear, and software.
  • An asset counts only if it is included in the sale and necessary to run the business.
  • Discontinued stock (products you stopped selling) and personal costs such as travel are not recoverable (you cannot get them back into the price).

Google Analytics and due diligence

1 min
  • Google Analytics (a free Google tool that counts visitors and where they come from) is essential for verifying traffic sources and visitor trends.
  • Buyers require access to confirm these metrics (measures) during due diligence.
  • Install Google Analytics about six months before the sale.
  • Buyers rely on it to detect bot or fake traffic (visits from software, not people) and to see traffic sources. It is the de facto standard (what everyone uses in practice).
  • Preparing Google Analytics access for buyer due diligence takes active time.
  • You must grant the necessary permissions, provide documentation of historical traffic sources, and remain available to verify visitor data accuracy and trends.
  • In Google Analytics, be an admin (the account owner with full control) or know the admin username.
  • Be ready to add buyers as users so they can run their own reports. Screenshots of Analytics can be enough for early review.
  • If a seller refuses Google products, Clicky (a traffic tool) is an accepted alternative to Google Analytics for buyers' due diligence.
  • Server log statistics (numbers from the server's own records), such as AWStats (a log reporting tool), are not accepted for due diligence.
  • Stats you simply post without a real analytics account are also not accepted. Buyers will not trust them.
  • Screenshots of earnings and traffic are easy to fake. Video is harder to fake and more trusted.
  • Record a screencast (a video of your screen) of earnings and traffic, especially for public auction listings (stores offered in open auctions).
  • Every financial figure in a listing must match the others.
  • State the exact date range for earnings and traffic. Say whether traffic is sessions (visits) or page views (pages loaded).
  • Brokers verify earnings by logging into the account and checking screenshots themselves, or by having the seller show the data live.
  • Expect this step before a listing goes live.

Seasonality evidence

1 min
  • Buyers of seasonal niches (topics that sell more at certain times of year) want at least two years of sales data before trusting a spike (a sudden rise). One year cannot show whether the spike repeats in the same period.
  • If the second year shows the spike in the same place as the first, the store is much easier to value.
  • The 12-month average covers seasonality, which is why it is the default basis.
  • Do not sell the store while sales are down, because the price will be lower than what it is worth.

Profit-growth levers before sale

2 min

Three levers (things you can pull) raise store value:

  • Raise revenue with profitable products, suppliers and traffic streams (flows of visitors).
  • Hold or improve margins (the share of each sale you keep as profit).
  • Lower buyer risk to raise the multiple.

Ways to grow profit:

  • To grow profit, improve ad ROI (ROI means return on investment, how much you earn back for each dollar spent). Also cut unprofitable ads.
  • To grow profit, you can also renegotiate product costs or move to a new supplier.
  • To grow profit, build relationships that turn customers into repeat buyers.
  • Email list growth (more people signed up to receive your emails) is a revenue and margin lever.
  • Higher average order value (the average amount per order), higher customer lifetime value (the total a customer spends over time) and better return on ad spend are also levers.
  • Adding email marketing to a store can lift revenue by roughly 10 to 20 percent, according to one estimate.
  • A long-running database of outreach contacts (people you have contacted for links or partnerships) and earned backlinks (links from other sites to yours) can add resale value.
  • A buyer may value that database much like an email list.
  • A sustainable cost per acquisition (what you pay to win one new customer, at a level you can keep up) raises valuation.
  • Upsells (extra add-on sales), pre-sells (selling before the main sale page) and reaching customers before the purchase help build that sustainable cost.
  • The same steps also raise defensibility (how hard it is for others to copy you), brand awareness and profit margin.

Improve margins before selling in four steps:

  1. Stop exploratory ad spend (testing ads) that does not return a positive ROI, especially in the 12 months before a sale.
  2. Renegotiate product costs by asking the supplier for lower prices on best-selling products, or source an alternative supplier with the same quality.
  3. Build customer relationships and retention (keeping customers coming back), since keeping existing customers is cheaper than acquiring new ones.
  4. Improve ad return on ad spend.
  • Eliminate ad spend that is exploratory and does not currently return ROI during the 12 months leading up to a sale.
  • Buyers value proven profit rather than tests.

Sale timing and format

1 min
  • There are three ways to increase store value: grow revenue while keeping margins, grow profit by cutting spending, or improve the multiple the store can sell for.
  • Sell a store as an asset sale (the buyer takes the store's parts, not the company itself) instead of the business entity (the legal company).
  • Stores profitable for at least 12 months can be listed on Empire Flippers (a marketplace for online stores).
  • Newer stores can be posted on the Fast Business Forum (a forum where stores are posted for sale).
  • A store bought and resold every 12 to 18 months lands at the lower end of the multiple range.

Further details

1 min
  • Annual return on buying a website (yearly profit as a share of the price) equals monthly net profit multiplied by 12, divided by the purchase price.
  • For example, a site priced at $100,000 that earns $5,000 net profit per month returns 60% per year if profit stays flat.
  • The result is a best-case figure (the most optimistic result) because it assumes no growth and no decline.
