Section 19.23 · 23 of 23 in this chapter · 21 min read

In short

  • A Shopify store plan (online store software) cost about 29 USD per month in 2020. These are old prices, so check today's.
  • Judge a store by its setup goals first. Then keep improving them, because a store is never finished.
  • Build a second store only after the first is fully set up and its ad budgets are tuned.
  • Write down a starting number before every change. Without one, you cannot tell if the change helped.
  • Odd or angry customer messages are normal once your shop is public. They usually do not mean your business is fake.
More key points
  • Keep one log of copied brand items. Check it on a regular schedule.
  • Raising prices did not always cut orders when demand was high and stock was low.
  • Judge your store by the goals you set first.

Startup, store setup and expansion

2 min

  • Startup costs in 2020 were small. A Shopify plan (software for running an online store) cost about 29 USD per month. A domain (your web address) cost about 14 USD per year. Google Ads and Facebook each gave a starting credit of 100 USD. Google Ads is Google's paid ad system. After that, put your profit back into ads.

  • Judge a store's performance by its setup goals first. The goals are:

    • Suppliers (companies that ship your products) approved.
    • A site built to convert (turn visitors into buyers).
    • Google Ads optimized (improved for better results).
    • Bing Ads set up. Bing Ads is Microsoft's paid ad system.
    • SEO (search engine optimization, making your site rank in free search results) foundations in place.
    • Email campaigns (planned emails to customers) running.
    • Remarketing (showing ads again to people who already visited) running.

    Then keep improving those goals. A store is never finished.

  • Use this checklist before starting another store:

    1. Get approved with every supplier in the niche (your product market). Aim for 20 suppliers.
    2. Optimize the site for conversions.
    3. Optimize Google Ads.
    4. Set up Bing Ads.
    5. Optimize the site for search engines.
    6. Set up email campaigns.
    7. Run remarketing.
    8. Keep auditing and improving the first store before building a second one.

  • Start a second store only when the first is set up and its budgets are tuned. Keep improving the first store while you build the second.

  • Use this setup sequence for a new store:

    1. Follow the Compliance Checklist (a list of legal rules for online sellers).
    2. Set up Google Analytics (a free tool that counts visitors).
    3. Set up Google Search Console (a free Google tool showing how people find your site in search).
    4. Submit your products to Google Merchant Center (Google's product list for shopping results).
    5. Create your Google remarketing campaign.
    6. Set up your Bing Webmaster Tools account (Bing's free site tool).
    7. Create your Microsoft Merchant Center store account.
    8. Submit your products to Microsoft Merchant Center.
    9. Create your Bing Shopping campaigns.
    10. Create your Facebook Ads account.
    11. Connect your store to Facebook Ads.
    12. Create your audience for Facebook Ads (the group of people your ads will reach).
    13. Create your Facebook remarketing campaign.
    14. Find and contact the top blogs in your niche.
    15. Create your promotions.

Shopify and platform settings

1 min

  • In Shopify, go to Online Store > Preferences. The Customer privacy section has two options:

    • Limit tracking for customers in Europe. This follows GDPR, an EU privacy law that requires permission (consent) before tracking.
    • Limit the sale of California customers' data to third parties. This follows CCPA, a California privacy law.

    Turn these on if your store targets those regions.

  • For each product, turn on Track quantity in its inventory settings. (Inventory means your stock.) Then enter the exact number in stock. Without this, the product page cannot show any stock count.

  • Turn on Shopify order notifications on your phone. You see new sales as they happen. The seller used this alert during the viral phase (a sudden spike in popularity).

  • Custom dashboards (your own summary screens) in Google Analytics and custom reporting in Google Ads are optional. Google's support pages have current guides with screenshots for both.

  • Source A's tracking sends store data to its provider, so the theme's performance can be monitored. To opt out, untick this option and click save.

  • If the reviewer must examine paid traffic data (visitors from ads), grant access to your Google Analytics and Google Ads accounts before the call. If you have not granted access, tell the reviewer clearly that they need it.

  • Use Zapier (a tool that connects apps automatically) at about 20 USD per month for abandoned cart recovery and order tracking. An abandoned cart is when a shopper leaves items in the cart without paying. Zapier recovers at least one extra sale from existing traffic. That makes it worth the cost for shops with a steady abandon rate.

Supplier, order and lead tracking

2 min

  • Track supplier outreach (your contact with suppliers) in a supplier tracking sheet. Note who you contacted, when to follow up, and reminders.

  • Keep a tracking sheet for supplier talks. Record the date, contact, what was said and the next follow-up step. This turns early contact into a reliable record.

  • Keep detailed notes for every supplier contact. With many suppliers, you will forget who said what and the next step.

  • Fill in these Master Supplier List tracking columns for every contact:

    Column Field Content
    E Date of contact Date you reached out to the supplier
    F Contact person Name of the person you spoke with at the supplier
    G Notes What happened on the call or email, plus next steps, such as a callback date, that the form will be emailed, or that a website dealer application must be submitted

    Always record the next step and any follow-up date, for example "contact us again in two months", so it is not forgotten.

  • With products from several suppliers in one order, each supplier usually ships separately. Each has its own tracking number and packaging. Customers expect this. They know they may get 2-3 packages from 2-3 suppliers on different days.

  • Use the paid orders list to find fulfillment bottlenecks (places where orders get stuck before shipping).

    • A card with a date older than your usual supplier response time (usually 1-3 days) signals a delay.
    • The delay may be in sending orders to suppliers, or in waiting for suppliers to respond.
    • The visual board shows exactly which stage orders pile up in.
    • Act early, before delays slow shipping for customers.

  • Above about 100 orders a month, orders are easy to lose track of. Use status labels so every order's stage is visible at a glance.

  • A glance at the prospect sheet (your list of possible customers) shows the day's activity. For example: 15 calls received, three closed sales and 30 prospects still open. This is enough to forecast the near-term workload.

  • Record all custom order requests (orders made to a customer's own specifications) in a database. This shows which follow-up timing works, which approaches convert, and why deals succeed or fail.

  • Add outcome columns to your custom order tracking sheet:

    • Record whether each lead became a sale: won, lost or pending.
    • Record the reason: budget, timeline, feature fit, competitor choice or follow-up timing.

    This shows the patterns behind lost sales and which fixes matter most.

  • About 80% of sales come from about 20% of products. Within that group, a further 80/20 split applies. Track live inventory only for your best sellers. Do not try real-time stock updates for the whole catalog.

  • The store received fewer than one refund per month, and sometimes about one refund every three months, across thousands of orders. These numbers depend on clear descriptions and clear policies.

  • The store received about two customer inquiries per week against five or more sales per week. This shows the effect of communication before and after the sale. These figures come from this store's own experience. They are not a benchmark.

Changelog, baselines and test records

1 min

  • Record a baseline (a starting number, such as the 90-day average of a metric) in your notes before each change. Then you can compare the result against it. Without a baseline, a result like a 50% open rate (share of emails opened) cannot be judged as an improvement.

  • Use the changelog (a log of every change) to explain sales movements. When sales rise or fall, first check which logged tests were running then.

    • Credit a rise to a test, and double down on it (put more into it).
    • Stop a test that seems to cause a drop, as soon as possible.

  • Fill in the Expected Results cell before a change goes live. A written prediction shows later whether the change met the target or only looked like it did. Without a pre-recorded number, a flat result gets explained after the fact instead of judged against the goal.

  • Track every test in one consistent system. A documented library of past tests supports future decisions and adds value to the business when it is sold.

  • Each priority needs measurables, which are hard, trackable numbers with a target value at the end of six months. Measurables can be:

    • Traffic (visits)
    • Number of keywords ranked (search terms your site appears for)
    • Number of backlinks (other sites linking to yours)
    • Sales count
    • Revenue
    • Profit
    • Profit margin
    • Conversion rate
    • Number of approved suppliers
    • Number of uploaded products

  • Set achievable targets, not extreme dreams. A target far out of reach for the period tends to discourage progress.

  • Typical business weaknesses to test for:

    • Paid ads that were not profitable.
    • No organic traffic (free visits from search), so every visit depends on paid traffic.
    • Unreliable suppliers, or too few supplier options.
    • Too few working hours put in by the owner or the team.

  • Do a time-tracking exercise before the annual review. Then you can check how you actually spent your hours. The review relies on recorded time, not memory.

  • Find your best-selling product from data, not memory:

    1. Open your Shopify sales reports and view sales by product.
    2. Find the product with the highest sales.
    3. Copy that product's URL for your records.

    If the store is new and has no sales yet, skip this step.

  • A founder who works 6 hours a day for weeks and sees no progress can give AI the product, the price and the ad results. AI can then find which element holds the results back and what to change. The next step is then based on data.

  • A monthly creative output report (a count of ad images and videos made) can be a stacked column chart. That means one column per month, with static images and videos stacked inside each column. Pair the chart with a table of the same monthly figures. Then growth or drop in total output is visible at a glance.

  • Store reviews are published every other week. Expect the next review about two weeks after each one.

Ad performance and direct response

2 min
  • Direct response ads aim to make people act now. When an ad taps existing mass desire (wants many people already have), each dollar of ad spend can produce roughly 50 to 100 dollars in sales. When an ad must create the desire first, it works like education. Then it returns about 1 dollar in sales per dollar spent.
  • A mentor can interpret account-specific metrics, such as CTR (click-through rate, the share of viewers who click) and CPM (cost per 1,000 views). A mentor can also tell you the time window to check and when to analyze ad sets (groups of ads with shared settings). General guides cannot give these thresholds for your own product.
  • Winning products often cannot be predicted. Test them in the market to see if they sell. Ad creative should create a problem or desire the buyer feels. The product itself is usually simple. The demand is created through the ad.
  • Historical Facebook ad example: a seasonal product launched with about 2,000 to 3,000 dollars in budget. About 50,000 dollars was reinvested in ads from 211,000 dollars in revenue within 30 days. This was in an unsaturated market (one not yet crowded with competitors), with cheap cost per click (the price of each click) and image ads. It is not a current benchmark.
  • Early internet scam-style ads, such as "win free iPhone" or "get free trip", reached 10 to 30 percent conversion rates. Users clicked readily and could not spot untrustworthy websites. This is a historical example, not a guide for current ads.
  • The 2021 Bing figure is 37.5% of U.S. desktop searches, the most recent figure cited at the time. This is a 2021 desktop-only share.
  • Do not use the 37.5% Bing share as the share of total search traffic. It covers only U.S. desktop searches. It does not cover all U.S. searches, including mobile.
  • To check whether readers finish an ad, offer a small cash reward, such as 5 dollars, to anyone who writes in saying they read it through. If very few reply, most readers skip the copy. Then the headline or opening needs to be stronger.
  • When you focus on B2B (business to business), remove low-priced products from paid ads. Keep the same ad approach for the rest. Advertise in trade publications (magazines for an industry), or set up affiliate deals with them (partners paid by commission) to reach business readers.
  • A friend's drop-shipping store (a store where the seller never holds stock, and the supplier ships) made about 476,900 USD in revenue in 30 days. Nearly all its traffic came from Facebook. Referrals came from mobile.facebook.com, facebook.com and l.facebook.com. The result came from running multiple stores with Facebook as the main traffic source.
  • One client grew from zero to 1.1 million USD in sales in 10 months. Another grew from 12,000 to 60,000 USD in monthly sales. Both are examples of Facebook ads working with careful targeting. The method was not fully described.

Copy and response measurement

3 min

  • Track the hit ratio, the share of sales letters or promotions that bring in sales. One copywriter reported a hit ratio of 20 to 40 percent before using a structured copy method. After applying it to every project, the hit ratio was 60 to 80 percent. Measure it per promotion to compare methods.

  • Breakthrough Advertising Marketing Plan: a Google Sheets template (a ready-made spreadsheet) to map your market positioning (how your product stands out) and messaging strategy. Complete it after reading the Breakthrough Advertising copywriting framework (a set of ad-writing rules). This aligns your positioning with your market's awareness level. https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1ocIg8b57icetsD1lAyLUpYlbZCXc3dHLzFHuUs9p4xE/edit#gid=1874141477

  • Flesch Reading Ease is a score for how easy text is to read. Calculate it by hand in this order:

    1. Count the words. Treat contractions, hyphenated words, abbreviations, figures, symbols and their combinations as single words.
    2. Count the syllables in each word as normally pronounced. Count abbreviations, figures and symbols as one syllable. If a word has two accepted pronunciations, use the one with fewer syllables.
    3. Count the sentences. A sentence ends with a period, colon, semicolon, question mark, exclamation point or dash. Ignore paragraph breaks and internal punctuation.
    4. Divide total syllables by total words to get average syllables per word.
    5. Divide total words by total sentences to get average words per sentence.
    6. Combine both averages in the formula to get the score.

  • Readability comparison of two ad paragraphs about the same offer. The Flesch-Kincaid grade shows the US school grade needed to read the text:

    Paragraph Sentences per paragraph Words per sentence Characters per word Flesch Reading Ease Flesch-Kincaid grade
    1 (plain, short sentences) 7.0 13.1 4.1 72.1 6.4
    2 (dense, long sentences) 3.0 25.3 5.3 34.1 14.7

  • Test a new mailing with a small list before committing a large budget. For example, use 5,000 names instead of 50,000. A small test shows whether the offer, the copy and the list work before the full cost is at risk.

  • Measure readership by how many people read the full copy. Not by circulation (copies sent) or impressions (times shown). Only readers who go through the copy can buy. A large audience that reads little makes a weak ad.

  • An illustrated four-page letter with a stamped post card cost about 4 cents per piece in 1913 US mail, including postage. It went first class with no enclosure. (Historical example.)

  • Follow-up response for a direct mail sequence (letters sent by post) to the same list of old leads. This is a historical example:

    Mailing Content Orders per mailed piece
    1 First letter to old leads 1 to 2 years old 4.5%
    2 Free examination letter with enclosed circular 2.5%
    3 Later letter with circular enclosure 1.5%
    4 Large folder with only a post card 1%
    5 Long four-page mop-up letter (a final letter to catch remaining buyers) 2%

  • Record orders per mailing on a record sheet for every letter, headline, envelope and variant. Let that record decide which versions stay. A letter's worth is measured only by the orders it brings back.

  • Test one element at a time, such as the headline, envelope, letterhead, stamp or opening. Compare each against a plain control letter (the old standard version used for comparison). Keep only what increases orders. Drop what does not.

  • Judging a letter by polish, or by how well it follows traditional rules, is misleading. A plain letter can outsell a polished one. Only the response measured in orders should decide.

  • In the first six months of one mail-order campaign, the profit on sales reached 34.5 percent.

  • Track response by test cell (each separate version tested) and count it over time. Most sales letter response comes in quickly. A small trickle follows weeks later. Compare the immediate response with the expected profit, since the trickle is usually small in profit terms.

  • Use no-cost pretests before paying for a mailing test. A small direct-mail test of 5,000 to 10,000 pieces can cost a lot. A pretest can reveal that a letter is so weak it must be rewritten entirely.

  • Use split tests (two versions compared side by side) to compare variations. For example, compare a neat, organized layout with a messy, busy one. The result is often the opposite of what designers expect. Test pricing formats, guarantees and premiums (free gifts) the same way.

Customer contact and reputation

1 min
  • Once your business is open to the public, expect odd or hostile contacts.
  • Hostile means angry or unfriendly.
  • Examples: calls at odd hours, or doubts about free shipping on heavy items.
  • Shipping means sending the item to the buyer.
  • These usually do not reflect the business's legitimacy, meaning whether it is real and lawful.

Brand protection and infringement tracking

2 min
  • Brand protection means guarding your brand from copies and misuse.
  • An infringement is when someone copies or misuses your brand without permission. Infringers are those people.
  • Tracking log means one spreadsheet file where you write down every case.
  • Use one tab per platform. A tab is one sheet inside the file. A platform is a website or app where items are sold or posted.
  • Every tab uses the same columns. Columns are the headings across the top.
  • A Complaint ID is the code a platform gives you after you file a report.
  • Copyright protects creative work, such as photos and text. Trademark protects a brand name or logo.
  • Status shows where each case stands.
Columns: Item (the infringer or page name) | Complaint ID (received after filing) | Person (who handled the report) | Detection Date | Reporting Date | Removal Date | Location (if detectable) | Link (to the infringing content) | Infringement Type (Copyright or Trademark) | Status (Detected, Reported, Resubmitted, Escalated, Removed).

Also keep a brand information tab with the details needed for reports: brand name, rights owner, official online presence, registration numbers, mailing address and reporting email.

Use the status 'Resubmitted' when the platform asks for more details, and 'Escalated' when outside help is needed.

  • Review the infringement log monthly, weekly or yearly. This is called analysis: checking the data for patterns.

    1. Count the infringements by platform.
    2. Count the infringements by type (copyright or trademark).
    3. Identify the most often infringed products.
    4. Identify where the infringers are located.
    5. Use the results to refine the detection and reporting process. Refine means improve. Detection means finding the copies.

  • Tabs in the infringement tracking board. The board is the whole spreadsheet file. Each tab holds one group of records.

  • Rights owner means the person or company who legally owns the brand.

  • Registration numbers are the official numbers a brand gets when it is registered.

  • A marketplace is a big online shop where many sellers sell.

  • Stand-alone websites are shops on their own site, not on a marketplace.

  • An archive is stored old records. (CLOSED) means the year is finished.

Tab Purpose
Brand Information Brand name, rights owner, official online presence, registration numbers, mailing address, reporting email
Amazon, eBay, Bol.com, TikTok, Etsy, Shopify One tab per marketplace or channel searched
Facebook, Instagram One tab per social platform, sorted by year groups such as 2024
Other Marketplaces Other channels not listed above
Stand Alone Websites (current year) Standalone shops found in the current year
(CLOSED) previous year tabs Archive of finished years, for example (CLOSED) 2023 Stand Alone Websites
  • Every platform tab uses the same column setup.

Case results and examples

2 min
  • Case study means one real example from one business.
  • Case study: Resistance Bands (stretchy exercise bands), Day 18.
  • Revenue means all money from sales before costs. Day 18 revenue: $507 on 17 orders.
  • Average order value means the average money per order: $29.85.
  • That price was full, with no discounts or giveaways. Giveaways are free items.
  • This shows that lasting growth does not need fake tricks like free giveaways or deep discounting, meaning big price cuts.
  • Case study: Resistance Bands, Week 5-6. A week here is 7 days.
  • 96 units sold. A unit is one item.
  • $2,900 revenue.
  • 10-18 units sold per day.
  • Average daily orders stayed above 10 on 6 of 7 days.
  • The price rose from a start of $24.95 to a $30.17 average, then to $35.95.
  • Orders did not drop much after the price rises.
  • Week 6, one day: 12 orders at the raised price of $35.95.
  • Net profit after Amazon fees: $22 per unit. Net profit means money left after all costs. Amazon fees are what Amazon takes from each sale.
  • Within 6 weeks, the initial investment (the first money put in) was fully paid back, and a reorder was funded. A reorder means buying more stock.
  • Weeks 7-8 brought profit payouts from the two-week Amazon payout cycle. A payout is money paid to you. A cycle is a repeating timing.
  • Key lesson: price increases did not always reduce orders. This happened when supply was low and demand was high. Supply means how much stock you have. Demand means how many people want the item.
  • Success case: 108 orders in one month, equal to $325,000 in sales.
  • Average order value: $3,000 per order.
  • Profitability timeline: the first sale usually comes within 30 days of launch, if the 21-day launch plan is followed. Launch means opening the store to customers.
  • Time to steady profit depends on the reinvestment rate (the share of profit you put back into the business) and ad optimization (improving ads so they work better).
  • Example: a competitor (another seller of the same kind of product) added extra features to their weighted blanket (a heavy blanket). It became Queen-size by default. Queen is a bed size.
  • This doubled the product cost.
  • You kept it simple, with a single size.
  • You spent the margin savings on ads. Margin means profit per sale.
  • You won the market.
  • Creative means the content you make to sell, such as text. Product description means the text on a product page.
  • Aim for a product description of about 600 words.
  • This length supports SEO (how well search engines find your page) and avoids thin content (pages with too few useful words).
  • Count the words in the product text and aim for that range.
  • Snapchat's SPECS AR glasses (augmented reality glasses, which show digital images over the real view) were reported to cost around $2,000.
  • This is an estimate from commentary on an update, not an official figure.
  • At this stage, the brand had about 185 products on sale.
  • Its annual revenue (sales money in one year) was around 1 million pounds.
  • The founder reported that the business reached a multi-seven-figure level. That means millions.
