Unit economics: margin, COGS and net profit per sale

Section 19.2 · 2 of 23 in this chapter · 48 min read

In short

  • COGS (cost of goods sold, what the product costs you) is the supplier's price plus shipping to the customer.
  • Treat 50% of the selling price as the most COGS you should accept on a first check. Aim lower where you can.
  • Add shipping, card fees and ad costs before you decide a product makes money.
  • Check net profit (money left after all costs) before you spend on ads.
  • Profit is what you keep after every cost. Start with a target of about 25% net margin.
More key points
  • A product should sell for at least 3 times what it costs you, or ads will eat the profit.
  • Check every product's numbers before you spend on ads. If the margin is too thin, do not run ads for it.
  • Returns, refunds and late payment claims cut profit later. Count them before you call a store profitable.
  • Confirm that each product makes money after cost, shipping, fees and ads before you spend more on it.
  • Count shipping and every fee as a cost. Leaving one out makes profit look higher than it is.
  • Use real supplier prices and real shipping quotes before you decide. Rough guesses are only for a first look.
  • Judge the business by net margin, the share of each sale you actually keep.

Column letters (A, B, C ...) refer to the research worksheet from section 2.3.

Cost of goods sold: what goes into it

2 min
  • Cost of goods sold (COGS, what the product costs you) is the supplier's purchase price, paid only after a sale. In the example, a product sold for 1,000 USD cost 400 USD in COGS.
  • Track COGS as a percentage of revenue (money from sales) as well as a dollar figure. Margin is the share of sales money you keep after product cost. One source calls COGS of 25% of revenue high and says to reduce it where possible.
  • Benchmark conflict: Other sources treat about 50% of selling price as the typical COGS ratio and the upper limit for a healthy product. The sources do not say which products or channels each figure fits, so use the 25% figure as a direction for reducing cost and the 50% figure as the ceiling for the first check.
  • Dropshipping (the supplier ships each order straight to the customer, so you hold no stock): COGS is the product price plus the shipping price to the customer's country, both taken from the supplier's price list. Always include shipping, otherwise margin calculations are overstated.
  • PPC definition (the name this source gives its definition): COGS includes all costs to produce and deliver: manufacturer or wholesale cost (the supplier's price to you), taxes, payment processing fees, fulfilment (packing and sending the order), and shipping. Express COGS as a dollar amount and as a percentage of revenue.
  • Blended definition (all costs added into one figure): Blended COGS includes every cost of fulfilling an order: product, packaging, freight (shipping of bulk or heavy loads), fees and 3PL charges (an outside company that stores and ships orders). It excludes ad spend, which is handled after the gross profit line as customer acquisition cost (the money spent on ads to win one customer).
  • Full cost per sale: The unit cost for a per-sale calculation is the full cost of a sale before marketing: product cost, shipping, customer service wages and all other overheads (fixed monthly costs of running the business) allocated to each sale. Leaving any of these out inflates apparent profit and affordable ad spend. **
  • Own shipping: When you ship on your own account, COGS comes from the supplier and shipping comes from a carrier (the shipping company). Match both to each order to calculate profit per order. **
  • Fees in COGS: The sources differ on whether payment fees sit inside COGS. The PPC and blended definitions include them. The full-cost-per-order formula lists processor fees as a separate line. For per-sale decisions use the full figure. For the first screen, use product cost alone, then add ads, shipping and other costs.

Keeping cost data accurate

1 min
  • Enter accurate COGS for every SKU (stock keeping unit, one product code or variant) in Shopify's product cost fields (Shopify is online store software) before building any calculator. Triple Whale (a tracking tool) and spreadsheet calculators take these values as the baseline, so wrong values distort every later metric.
  • In Shopify's product editor, the Pricing section's Cost per item field (the box for the product cost) is hidden from customers and used internally. Once entered, Shopify shows Margin and Profit for the product. Demo products that are never sold can skip it. **
  • Track COGS for every offer (every product you sell) continuously. If COGS rises, raise the price so margin stays intact. Example: a 13 USD cost on a 29.90 price that rises to 20 USD could justify a price of 39.90.
  • Do not treat a guessed cost as fact. A cost of about 20 cents per bar was an unverified estimate. Use the real landed cost (the full price delivered to your door) from the supplier.
  • Freight quotes (price offers for shipping) show the product price per unit and the shipping cost for each country (for example US, Canada, Australia, UK). Shipping prices differ by destination.
  • Enter COGS and selling price for every product. The gap between them sets margin and break-even ROAS (the lowest return on ad spend where you do not lose money; ROAS is sales made per dollar of ads). Compare your selling price with the competitor price to see whether you can price higher or lower.

Overhead and full cost per order

2 min
  • Full cost per order = product cost + shipping costs + shipping/handling fees + payment processor fees (companies that handle card payments) + paid advertising spend on Google, Bing, Facebook and Pinterest. Use this figure, not product cost alone, to judge profitability.
  • Overhead per order = average monthly overhead ÷ average monthly orders. Overhead includes Shopify, software, virtual assistants (remote helpers) and similar fixed costs. Example: $1,500 monthly overhead ÷ 100 orders = $15 overhead per order.
  • Net profit per order (what is left after all costs) = product list price − (product cost + shipping/handling + payment processor fee) − overhead per order. Example: $100 list price − $60 for product, shipping/handling and payment fees − $15 overhead = $25 net profit.
  • Hidden loss: A brand can look profitable without paid advertising and still lose money once total paid ad spend is added. Test profitability with aggregate ad spend (all ad money added together) included.
  • Fixed costs: Include the yearly domain fee (the yearly cost of your web address), monthly billed Shopify apps, virtual assistant wages and any other recurring overhead. Spread them over expected sales so each sale carries its share.
  • Cost list: Use a spreadsheet to list all supplier, fee, acquisition (the cost of winning customers), fixed and return costs. This shows the complete picture per month and per sale.
  • Own time: Cover the monthly Shopify subscription and your own time too, because a per-order check alone tends to overstate profit. **
  • Sale overheads: Every sale carries costs beyond the product and the ad: payment gateway fees (the charge for the link that sends card payments), a share of app, virtual assistant and platform subscriptions, shipping and similar overheads. All are paid from the gross profit (money left after product cost) of that sale. Know each one and how much gross profit each sale can carry for ads. **
  • Storage and customer shipping: Customer shipping and storage costs (keeping stock in a warehouse) must both be built into pricing and margin before launch. A product that looks profitable on paper can lose its margin once logistics (moving and storing goods) are paid. **

Gross margin and the cost-to-price check

3 min
  • Gross margin = revenue − COGS. Example: a MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price the brand lets you advertise) of 800 with wholesale of 400 leaves 400 before shipping, ads and other costs.
  • Gross margin on MAP: (MAP − wholesale) ÷ MAP. With MAP 1,000, wholesale 500 gives 50%; wholesale 700 gives 30%. That 30% is a gross figure, not the same as 30% net profit after shipping and ad costs.
  • Gross profit before ads: Selling price − sourcing cost (what you pay to get the product) − any shipping you absorb (pay yourself). Example: a 9.99 USD sale price − a 6 USD sourcing cost leaves 3.99 USD per sale before ads and fees.
  • Gross margin per sale (2019): Retail price − wholesale cost − shipping cost. Example: $699 − $530 = $169 before shipping. $200 shipping turns that into a loss of $31. **
  • First cost check: The supplier's cost should be about 50% of the selling price or less. At a $1,000 selling price, product cost should be $500 or lower. This is the first margin check, before counting ads, shipping and other costs.
  • Wholesale rule (2021): Wholesale cost should be at most half the retail price. Then subtract shipping, card processing at about 2.9% and advertising. Example: on a 1,000 dollar product, wholesale should be 500 dollars or less. **
  • Multiplier check: Check the wholesale-to-MAP multiplier (one number that compares wholesale to MAP) first when reviewing a product's margin. **
  • Average COGS: COGS averages about 50% of the selling price.
    • Gross margin (GM) = selling price − COGS
    • Net profit = gross margin − shipping cost
    • Net margin (%) = net profit ÷ selling price × 100

  • Ad budget per product: Decide the ad budget (money for ads on one product) from the amount left after wholesale and shipping are subtracted from MAP. Calculate this before setting any ad spend.
  • Non-MAP products: For non-MAP suppliers (suppliers who do not set a minimum price), a target of at least 50% margin based on wholesale is the benchmark. On average this means MAP is about double the wholesale price.
  • Spread and remaining money: Spread (the gap between MAP and wholesale) = MAP − wholesale. Spread − estimated shipping = money left before ad spend. Then check whether what remains can cover an ad budget of about 10% of the product price while keeping a good margin.
  • Discounts: Net profit after COGS and fees = sale price − COGS − credit card fees − shipping − ad cost. Discounts are not subtracted again separately, because they already reduce the sale price. **
  • Discount and card fee example: The source's example: a 10% discount on an item with a 20% margin, plus a 3% card fee, leaves 7%. Check margins after all fees. **
  • Markup vs margin: Markup is profit added on top of cost. Wholesale 500 × 1.3 = 650 is a 30% markup on cost. A 30% margin (the share of selling price kept) on a 1,000 selling price (2× wholesale) leaves about 300 after COGS, fees, shipping and ads. Do not confuse the two. **

Net profit per sale: formulas by source

4 min

Each formula is kept with its example. Where two sources state the same formula, they are merged and both ids are kept.

  • Dropshipping average (undated): Net profit per sale = sale price × average net profit margin. For drop shipping, an average net margin of about 30% is assumed. A $10 product keeps about $3 per sale, and a $1,000 product keeps about $300 per sale.
  • Order formula (2020): Net profit per order = selling price − wholesale cost − shipping cost − order processing fee − advertising cost per acquired customer. Example: 2,500 − 1,250 − 400 − about 50 (processing) − 100 (ads) leaves about 700 dollars. **
  • All-in cost (2020): All-in cost per product = wholesale cost + free shipping (you cover the shipping cost) + about 2.9% credit card merchant fee + app and other fixed monthly costs + ad spend per sale. Target a net margin of about 30% after all of these. Example: on a $1,000 MAP, total all-in cost should not exceed $700, leaving about $300 net. **
  • Cheap vs premium (2020): On a $1,000 MAP product with a $500 wholesale cost, $500 remains to cover shipping, merchant fees, fixed costs such as apps and Shopify, and ad costs. On a $200 to $500 product the amount left shrinks, so shipping and ad spend must be tightly controlled. Shipping can be cut by comparing rates from different freight carriers. **
  • Revenue-based formula (undated): Net profit = revenue − ad spend − (product + shipping cost) − payment processor fees (e.g. Stripe, PayPal) − app fees − other expenses − returns and refunds. Profit percentage = net profit ÷ revenue. Example: revenue $1,000, ad spend $500, product plus shipping $300, with fees and refunds subtracted, shows the real margin.
  • Tracker formula (undated): Profit = revenue − all expenses. Profit percentage = profit ÷ revenue. Expenses in the tracker (your tracking spreadsheet): refunds, COGS (product plus shipping), ad spend, payment processing fees, and Shopify fees. Add about 1% if Shopify Payments (Shopify's own card payment service) is not used.
  • Profit per order (2021): Selling price − supplier wholesale cost − card processing fees (about 2.9%) − advertising cost per sale − shipping cost, if you offer free shipping. **
    • Example: a $500 item costing $250 leaves $250. A $3,000 item costing $1,500 leaves $1,500.
    • If free shipping costs about $300 on a large item, the cheap item drops to about $50 and the expensive one keeps about $1,200. **
  • MAP formula (2023): Net profit per order = MAP price − wholesale cost − shipping cost − average ad cost − Shopify and app fees − other overhead. Target about 25% net operating profit after all expenses, and accept no less than about 20%.
    • Example: MAP $1,000, wholesale $500 (half of MAP), free shipping $150 and average ad cost about $80 leave $270 before Shopify and other costs.
    • Correction: The intermediate step of $300 after shipping is a subtraction error, since 500 − 150 = 350.
  • Net profit per sale (2019): Selling price − product cost − shipping − payment processing − ad cost per sale. Example with a $1,000 MAP sale: 1,000 − 500 − 100 − 30 − 70 = $300 net profit. **
  • Card-fee formula (2019): Net profit per order = sale price − wholesale cost − shipping cost − ad cost per sale − credit card fee, where the card fee is about 2.9% of the sale price. Example: at a MAP of 2,000 USD and wholesale of 1,000 USD, the card fee is about 58 USD before shipping and ads. The exact margin is only known after real sales, so calculate actual net profit for each order as orders come in. **
  • Net profit per order after COGS and shipping (undated): Net profit per order = selling price − COGS − shipping cost. Customer acquisition cost, payment processing fees and the seller's own time also reduce the result and must be subtracted where they apply.
    • Example: a $5,000 sale with $2,000 COGS and $1,000 shipping leaves $2,000 profit, before any customer acquisition or fees.
    • Even a very expensive, heavy item can be profitable if the price covers these costs.
  • Pricing-sheet formulas (2021-22): These are the formulas in a spreadsheet for working out prices.
    • Gross profit = (list price + shipping paid by customer) − supplier cost − supplier shipping cost
    • Net profit = gross profit − payment processor fee − insurance fee
    • Payment processor fee = (fee % × total received) + static fee per transaction (a fixed amount charged on every payment)
    • Insurance fee = insurance % × total received
    • Gross margin = gross profit ÷ total received
    • Net margin = net profit ÷ total received
    • Example check: fee on $1,000 at 2.9% + $0.30 = $29.30. **
  • Worked case: the sheet's examples (2021-22):
    • Product A: list $1,000, supplier cost $600, supplier shipping $150, free shipping to customer. Gross profit $250 (25% gross margin). After the $29.30 processor fee, net profit is $220.70, a 22.07% net margin.
    • Product B: list $2,500, supplier cost $1,750, supplier shipping $250, free shipping. Gross profit $500 (20% gross margin). After the $72.80 processor fee, net profit is $427.20, a 17.09% net margin.
    • Adding a 1% insurance fee: Product A drops from $220.70 to $210.70 (21.07%), and Product B from $427.20 to $402.20 (16.09%).

Card processing, payment and platform fees

3 min

  • Card fee rule: Include the card processing fee (the charge the card company takes on each payment) in every margin calculation: about 2.9% plus $0.30 per transaction. ( is older advice, 2021)

  • Single sale: On a 1,000 USD sale, the card fee is 29.30 USD (2.9% + 0.30 USD). This lowers the 370 USD profit example to about 340.70 USD. Card fees are easy to forget on a single sale.

  • Batch: 50 orders worth 50,000 USD carry 1,465 USD in fees: 2.9% of 50,000 is 1,450 USD, plus 0.30 USD per transaction for 15 USD. Net profit after fees and before ad spend falls from 19,970 USD to 18,505 USD. Note that 2.9% of 50,000 is 1,450, not 1,400.

  • Other processor rates:

    • PayPal and Stripe (card payment companies) charge roughly 1.5% to 3% per transaction, so this fee must be built into every price.
    • Break-even figures need a blended figure of about 4% on average, covering Shopify Payments, Stripe or PayPal.
    • Source D' calculator uses a gateway of 5.5% + $0.30 per order. Use the figure your own processor charges. The 2.9% + $0.30 standard above is the typical new-seller rate. [L0148?]

  • Source D gateway formula (code, verbatim). "Gateway" is the link that sends card payments. "Pick N Pack" is a warehouse fee for picking and packing orders.

    Gateway = Price On Site x 5.5% + 0.30
Total Cost For Us = COGS + Pick N Pack + Shipping + Gateway

  • Shopify plan costs: Shopify costs include a monthly plan fee and, depending on the chosen plan, an additional charge per sale. Both belong in the cost calculation.

  • Shopify transaction fee: One seller reported a Shopify transaction fee of 2.3% on the plan they were on, against a normal rate of 2.5%, and expected to pay the higher rate the following month.

  • Third-party processors: Payment processors outside Shopify Payments (Stripe, Square and others) charge 1% of transaction value on top. Shopify fees add about 1% if Shopify Payments is not used.

  • Plan switch break-even (2021-22): Break-even monthly sales for a higher Shopify plan = monthly plan price difference ÷ difference in transaction fee rate. Example: a USD 50 price gap with a 1 percentage point fee gap (one percentage point means 1 more % on each sale) breaks even at USD 5,000 in monthly sales. The source's rough estimate of USD 30,000 to 40,000 monthly revenue for switching implies a fee gap of only about 0.13 to 0.17 percentage points. Check the real fee gap before deciding. **

  • Hidden costs in break-even: Break-even figures must include payment processing fees, app fees, Shopify transaction fees and VAT (value-added tax, a sales tax used in many countries). Ignoring these makes the break-even ROAS too low.

  • Commissions (2021-22): Commission cost per sale = sale price × commission rate. Example: a $6,000 sale at 10% costs $600 in fees. Percentage commissions make high-ticket (expensive) products costly to sell through this channel. **

  • Tax-inclusive revenue: Ad platforms report revenue including tax. Start from the tax-inclusive average order value (the average sale amount, tax included) and then remove sales tax. Otherwise tax inflates profit: a $200 product with $10 tax shows as $210 in Facebook.

  • Shipping fee to customer: A shipping fee of $4.99 does not significantly reduce conversion (the share of visitors who buy). Customers are less price-sensitive to shipping than sellers assume, so charging shipping on lower tiers (cheaper order levels) protects margins without hurting conversions.

  • Chargebacks and refunds: A chargeback is when a customer reverses a card payment through their bank. If chargebacks and refunds become too high, the payment processor may close the account. Track the chargeback and refund rate and act early to keep it low.

  • Optional insurance: The pricing sheet has an insurance fee percentage, applied only if you use fraud insurance (for example 1%).

Shipping, freight and bulky items

2 min
  • Shipping always in: Shipping in dropshipping is part of COGS. Break-even calculations that leave out shipping, payment fees or app fees give an overly optimistic margin. Include them before scaling (growing sales and ad spend).
  • Free shipping: Free shipping is a cost. Net margin calculations subtract it, and the all-in cost includes it.
  • Shipping as a lever: Ad spend and ROAS are usually the largest costs and the biggest levers (things you can change to move net margin). Shipping costs are the second lever, especially for freight shipments.
  • Shipping quotes: Shipping prices differ by destination, so quote several.
  • Mid-priced heavy items: Watch mid-priced but heavy or bulky products, since shipping can wipe out the margin. Example: an item bought for $200 and sold for $400 loses money if shipping costs $200. **
  • Small margin gap: A product with a MAP of 300 USD and an estimated wholesale of 150 USD leaves about 50 USD after 100 USD shipping for ads, the 2.9% merchant fee and other costs. That is a weak choice unless its shipping is cheaper. **
  • Worked example: MAP 1,000 vs MAP 400. Both have 150 USD shipping, COGS at 50% of MAP and ad budget at 10% of MAP.
    • The 1,000 item keeps 500 after cost, 400 after ads and 250 after shipping.
    • The 400 item keeps 200 after cost, 160 after ads and only 10 after shipping.
    • Heavy or bulky products fail when the gap between MAP and wholesale is too small to absorb shipping and ads. **
  • Accessory check (2021-22): Before deciding on an accessory (a small add-on product), calculate the real margin after shipping, all platform fees and CapEx (money spent on equipment or long-lasting items). Example: a $30 product with a 30% margin leaves $21. Shipping of about $8 turns it into a loss once fees and CapEx are subtracted. **
  • Transit stress test (2021-22): Stress-test (test under bad conditions) each product for a transit problem (a problem during shipping) that ends in a refund combined with a discount. That combination can wipe out remaining profit on a product that already has high shipping costs. Products that fail should be repriced, renegotiated or dropped before listing. **
  • Shipping estimate procedure. Use this procedure before launching a product:
    1. Ask the supplier for shipping quotes to a few destination cities, such as New York and Los Angeles, when the supplier ships on its own account.
    2. If you ship on your own account, use a freight quote comparison site. Enter the supplier's warehouse address or zip code (US postal code), then request estimates for several zip codes across the U.S.
    3. Average the estimates to get a shipping figure for the profit spreadsheet.
  • Freight tool: FreightCenter.com offers free instant freight quotes. Enter the product size and weight, the supplier's warehouse location and several US zip codes to estimate shipping cost and check whether the spread covers it.

Returns, refunds and chargebacks (money taken back from sales)

2 min
  • Return rate (orders) = (returns + size exchanges) ÷ total orders over the same period. One return or exchange in every five orders is a 20% return rate. For an established fashion brand this was about 30,000 to 35,000 in returns per year (currency not stated). (older advice, 2019-22 context not stated)
  • Return rate (revenue) = returns ÷ gross sales before returns × 100. Gross sales means all sales before any money is given back. Net sales (what you keep) = gross sales − returns. Examples from 2021 Shopify reports (Shopify is an online store platform):
    • Infrared sauna store: 23,721.80 ÷ 251,671.35 ≈ 9.4%
    • Gazebo store: 68,596.17 ÷ 806,805.49 ≈ 8.5%
    • Air purifier store: 4,437.60 ÷ 69,607.60 ≈ 6.4% **
  • Pre-purchase return option is a paid add-on at checkout that covers returns. Net return economics per order = (share of shoppers who buy the pre-purchase return option × fee charged) − (share of orders returned × cost of one return, including return shipping, return software and handling). A positive result means the fees cover return costs, and any surplus is extra margin. Check that the result is positive.
  • Judge fees by cohort. A cohort is a group of shoppers with something in common. Do not judge a pre-purchase return fee only by the overall return rate. Opted-in shoppers (those who chose the option) may return slightly more often, and non-opted shoppers slightly less. Track both groups separately.
  • Klarna refunds. Klarna is a pay-later payment service. Klarna buyers can request refunds for about six months after purchase, so a Klarna store's real profit is only visible after that period. A store that looks profitable in month one can still show losses later.
  • New-customer refund rate. Take the refund rate from homepage metrics (for example 2.81%). Convert it into a dollar amount per order by applying it to the new-customer average order value (AOV, the average amount a buyer spends per order).
  • Buffer means extra money set aside for surprises. No source sets a fixed buffer percentage. Build the buffer from the refund rate applied per order and the return-cost formula. Then subtract returns and refunds in net profit (profit after all costs).

Margin targets and thresholds

1 min

Margin is the share of each sale you keep as profit. Two kinds matter:

  • Gross margin: profit after the product and shipping costs.
  • Net margin: profit after every cost, including ads, apps and fees.

COGS means cost of goods sold, which is what the product costs you. MAP means minimum advertised price, the lowest price the brand lets you advertise.

Gross margin tiers

1 min

Gross margin is the share of the price left after the product and shipping costs.

  • Benchmark tiers (rating levels) for gross margin percentage:
    • Elite: 80% or more
    • Sweet Spot: 70-79%
    • Workable: 50-69%
    • Insufficient: 0-49%
  • Aim for a gross margin of at least 70%. Below that, the margin usually leaves too little room to scale (grow sales).
  • Keep COGS at or below 30% of the listing price (the price shown on the store), so that the product lands in the Sweet Spot tier.
  • Aim for a dollar margin (gross profit per order) of $50 or more. With less than $50, paid ad attribution (matching sales to the ad that brought them) leaves little room to test and scale efficiently on Meta (Facebook and Instagram ads).
  • Ad spend per order must stay below the gross margin. The calculator makes ad spend available only at 50% margin or more.

  • 3× rule. Set the minimum selling price at least three times the product cost. For example, a $10 product sold for at least $30. This is the bare minimum for leaving room for ad costs. ROAS means return on ad spend: money earned for each dollar spent on ads. At a 3× ratio the break-even ROAS (the ad return where you neither gain nor lose) falls near 1.5 to 1.6. That leaves room for ad spend, apps, staff and payment processing fees. The 3× formula is used at the start. A winning product achieves better margins as its conversion rate rises.
  • Break-even ROAS limit. Only pick products with a break-even ROAS of about 1.4 to 1.5 or lower. A break-even ROAS of 2 makes profitability very difficult, because a consistent Facebook ROAS above 2 is hard to reach.
  • Wholesale spread. Wholesale is the price you pay the supplier. A wholesale-to-MAP spread of 20 to 35% often leaves no margin once advertising costs, merchant fees (card and payment fees) and shipping are included. **
  • 20% margin rule. If margins are only 20% including all costs, do not run paid ads to those products. Rely only on organic traffic (free visitors from Google listings and search). Transaction fees (2.9%), ad spend allocation (up to 10%) and operational costs will consume the entire margin.
  • Prioritise profit. Concentrating ad budget on products with healthy profit margins ensures that sales growth directly increases net profit. This optimises the profitability of each customer acquisition as volume scales.

Price points, MAP and wholesale spread

2 min
  • MAP examples:
    • MAP $2,000, wholesale $1,500, shipping $400 = $100 profit before ad costs.
    • MAP $2,000, wholesale $1,000, shipping $400 = $600 profit.
    • MAP $3,000, wholesale $1,500, shipping $400 = $1,100 profit. (2025)
  • MAP formula: MAP − wholesale cost − shipping cost = gross profit per sale. This profit must still cover advertising costs.
  • Margins by supplier. Net profit margins do not show an industry-wide decline. Electronics and car seats are exceptions with lower margins. Margins vary by supplier, not by niche in general.
  • Biggest mistake. The biggest margin mistake is a selling price too close to wholesale cost. This often happens with distributors (middlemen who resell). If a supplier cannot reach 2× MAP, ask for better pricing. If they refuse, stop selling that supplier's products. **
  • Cheap price points. Cheaper price points ($9.97-$12.97) create unsustainably thin margins. You run out of money before you can rank organically (appear in search results without paid ads). Each sale makes too little profit to pay for paid traffic. This is the main reason lower-priced products fail in competitive niches (markets with many sellers).
  • Premium pricing. Base lid at $16.99 yields $11.05 profit after Amazon fees (what Amazon charges to sell there). A premium version at $24.00 with $3.00 COGS yields $14.00 profit (58% margin). Differentiation (making your product clearly different) can justify the higher price without losing sales.
  • Price alone is no guide. Do not assume which products are most profitable based on price alone. A $1,500 product may have lower actual margins and higher acquisition cost (the cost to win one buyer) than a $500 product. That makes the cheaper one more profitable. Base all segmentation (sorting products into groups) on actual sales data and margin calculations.
  • Non-MAP flexibility. Non-MAP ad strategy must be more flexible than MAP strategy, because prices can change quickly. Stop spending on products where a competitor accepts a very thin margin, such as around 5% per sale.
  • Raise price, not just cut cost. Bundles (several items sold together as one offer) and packaged offers can lift effective margin. The calculator suggests a bundle or a higher-margin product when a result is Insufficient. Raising the price through a packaged offer can fix a margin that is too low.

Ad spend against margin

2 min
  • Ad budget follows margin. Ad budget per product is set after wholesale and shipping are subtracted from MAP.
  • Keep ad cost within remaining margin. After product margin is confirmed, the main cost to control is ad spend per sale. Keep ad costs within the margin remaining after product, shipping and processing costs. **
  • Higher-margin products. Advertising on higher-margin products costs more per customer, but it is justified by margin. Lower-margin products are more likely to be bought organically through Google search or browsing, so ads on them are less efficient. Keywords that trigger ads typically come from shoppers who already know the product they want. **
  • Low-priced items. Before removing low-priced items, ask whether they would be profitable with zero ad spend. If they would, put them in their own campaign (a separate ad group) with a lower budget instead of deleting them. **
  • Historical data. Use historical sales and margin data (past records) from older suppliers to decide which products deserve ad budget. Products with the highest sales volume and best margins stay in paid campaigns.
  • Thin margin first. Before any shipping or pricing change, look at the profit left per sale as a percentage. A product with a thin margin needs cost fixes first, before any other change. **
  • Value of a margin point (2021-22). Extra profit from a margin gain = total revenue × margin points gained. On 1.5 million revenue, each percentage point is worth about 15,000. Raising gross margin by 5 points is worth about 75,000 per year at flat revenue (revenue staying the same). **
  • Margin leak procedure. A margin leak is a cost that eats your profit. If net margin is below 20% after every cost, work backwards to find the leak:
    1. Check whether the product cost is too high as a percentage of revenue.
    2. Check whether shipping costs are too high.
    3. Check whether the return on ad spend is too low.
    4. Fix the leak: improve the ads, negotiate better shipping rates, or ask the supplier for better pricing incentives. **
  • Ad cost tracking. Track the cost of ads per sale as the single largest variable cost (a cost that changes with sales) after product and shipping.

    4. Read the benchmark tier (Elite, Sweet Spot, Workable or Insufficient) and the target ROAS and target CPA.

Per-product profitability screening

2 min
  • Confirm before you scale: before you scale a product, product category or brand, confirm it is profitable. Track this in a spreadsheet or similar tool. Choose the method based on how many products you sell.
  • Best sellers only: run the per-product calculation only for your best sellers. A catalogue (your full product list) of several hundred products usually has fewer than 20 that sell well. These give the biggest return on the effort. **
  • First screen vs decision: MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price the brand lets you advertise) divided by 2 gives only a ballpark wholesale cost. Wholesale means the price the supplier charges you. This is good enough for a first screen, but not for decisions. **
  • Before you commit, use the supplier's actual price list and real shipping quotes. **
  • Two-step screen:
    1. Spread (the gap between selling price and wholesale cost) = MAP − wholesale. Then spread − estimated shipping = money left before ad spend (the money you have to pay for advertising).
    2. Check whether what remains covers an ad budget of about 10% of the product price while keeping a good margin (the share of each sale you keep).
  • Non-MAP screen (for products without a MAP price): use a target of at least 50% margin on wholesale as the benchmark.
  • Break-even ROAS screen: ROAS (return on ad spend) is the sales you make per dollar of ads. Pick only products with a break-even ROAS of about 1.4 to 1.5 or lower.
  • Test gate: test a product with paid ads only if the margin calculator supports it. Read the Insufficient tier (the calculator's lowest rating level) as a stop.
  • Break-even CPA: CPA (cost per acquisition) is what one sale costs in ads. Calculate break-even CPA from the margin before a test is called profitable or not.
  • Pricing research inputs: for each product, record the MSRP/RRP (the suggested retail price), the MAP, the competitor price and the competitor shipping charge.
  • Compare your price with the competitor price to see room to price higher or lower.
  • Stress test: run the transit-refund-discount test (a check of the numbers against transit costs, refunds and discounts) before listing.
  • Channel fees: on high-ticket products (expensive items), check percentage commissions (the cut the platform takes, as a percent) against the sale price before choosing the channel (the place you sell).

Cost of goods, shipping and tax per order

1 min
  • COGS (cost of goods sold, what the product itself costs you) is the exact price paid to the supplier for the product units in that order. Take it from the supplier invoice.
  • Shipping and fees are not included in COGS, because they have their own columns.
  • Shipping is the amount you paid to move the order. This can be to the supplier, a freight broker (a company that arranges shipping), UPS or FedEx. It is not what the customer paid.
  • If you offer free shipping, enter your own shipping cost anyway. Keep the customer's payment in Customer Paid (the amount the customer paid).
  • Always include shipping in the supplier total (everything you paid for the order).
  • Leaving shipping out overstates net profit, even when the shipping is paid to a third-party freight forwarder (a company that moves goods) or UPS on your own account.
  • Margins must be calculated after shipping, not only on product price and supplier cost. Shipping can make a sale unprofitable.
  • Tax Collected is sales tax that Shopify (your online store software) collected from the customer. It is not your money, so deduct it.
  • Check the order in the Shopify dashboard (the admin screen) and enter the exact amount. Enter 0 if no tax was collected.
  • Cost of goods equals product price plus shipping costs plus import costs plus any taxes due on delivery.
  • Payment processing fees (what the payment company charges per sale) must be included too. For example, Shopify processing fees are about 2.4% of the transaction.
  • Include all of these before deciding whether a product is profitable.

  • Include them in every margin calculation, alongside percentage fees.

Gross and net profit per sale

2 min
  • Gross profit (before overheads, the fixed running costs) = selling price − supplier purchase price. **
  • Example: a $3,000 sale minus a $2,000 supplier cost leaves about $1,000 to cover operating costs. **
  • Gross profit (order-level) = order amount received − supplier total (product cost plus shipping). Example: 1,000 − 600 = 400. **
  • Gross profit for pricing is measured after all per-order costs, not just product cost.
  • Using only product cost overstates the margin (makes it look bigger than it is) and leads to prices that are too low.
  • Net profit (order-level) = order amount received − supplier total − payment processor fee − fraud insurance fee. Example: 1,000 − 600 − 29.30 − 10 = 360.70. **
  • Because the order amount is the amount actually received, the discount is not subtracted a second time. **
  • Net profit (per order, sheet) = Customer Paid − COGS − Shipping − Tax Collected − Merchant Fees.
  • Net profit (full e-commerce formula) = sale price − sales tax and duties (import taxes, per region) − payment processing − customer acquisition cost (CAC, the cost to win one customer; can be expressed as MER, marketing efficiency ratio: sales divided by ad spend) − cost per unit of stock (supplier price) − inbound freight (shipping goods to you, by air or sea) − 3PL pick and pack costs − last mile / courier cost − average refunds.
  • 3PL (a outside company that stores and ships your orders). Pick and pack means finding items and packing them. Last mile means the final delivery to the customer.
  • 3PL storage costs can be left out of this per-order calculation. They are small and belong elsewhere.
  • Net profit with overhead: Overhead per order = monthly overhead ÷ monthly orders.
  • Net profit per order = selling price − product cost (including product shipping and payment processor fees) − overhead per order.
  • Overhead covers the Shopify subscription, virtual assistants (outside helpers who work remotely), software and other fixed costs.
  • Example: price $100, cost $60, monthly overhead $1,500 over 100 orders gives $15 overhead per order and $25 net profit.
  • Per-order net profit is measured before ad spend and before fixed costs, such as Shopify subscriptions and app fees (small add-on programs).
  • It shows what each sale earns, not the store's final result.
  • Subtract ad spend and monthly fixed costs separately to see the real business profit.
  • Break-even price is the selling price at which gross profit per order is zero, after product cost, card fees, merchant fees, shipping and fulfilment (the work of storing, packing and sending) are subtracted.
  • Any price above it earns a margin. So the 70% target price must sit well above this break-even point.

Net margin and product margin

1 min
  • Net margin (order-level) = net profit ÷ order amount. Net margin is the share of each sale you keep. **
  • Example: 360.70 ÷ 1,000 = 36.07%. **
  • Net margin (sheet) = Net Profit ÷ Customer Paid, shown as a percentage next to Net Profit.
  • Net margin on an order = net profit ÷ amount the customer paid. **
  • Example: net profit 370.70 on an order of 1,000 gives 37.07%. **
  • Net margin (business definition) = customer price − supplier cost − marketing spend − shipping cost (including free shipping you pay for) − transaction fees − any other cost of the sale. **
  • The remainder, as a percentage of the sale, is what stays in the business. **
  • Product margin = (listing price − cost of goods) ÷ listing price. The listing price is the price shown in your store.
  • Example: $150 price and $35 cost gives 76.67%.
  • Example: listing price $150, cost of goods $35 gives a product margin of 76.67%, a target cost per purchase of $115.00 (the most you can pay in ads for one sale) and a break-even ROAS of 1.30 on Meta (the Facebook and Instagram ad platform).

Margin targets and benchmarks

1 min
  • Typical high-ticket net margin is usually between 10% and 30%, and on average closer to 20%. **
  • This is the margin after all costs of the sale, not just the product price. **
  • Judge the business by net margin, not gross margin. **
  • Net margin is what remains after supplier cost, marketing, shipping and transaction fees. **
  • Financial tracking and the terms behind it should be set up properly from the start. **
  • Keep a 3% to 5% gap in your margin for losses from returns, chargebacks (the bank reverses a payment), disputes and payment fees.
  • These costs appear on top of product cost and shipping.
  • Expected versus actual margin: a store that expected a 20% margin for a full month ended with about 10% to 11% after cash issues and missed costs.
  • Track actual costs per order instead of relying on expected margins.
  • Minimum profit per product: require about 20 euros per product, because ad costs keep rising and smaller margins will not cover them.
  • Products below this margin are not worth the testing risk.
  • Health check: if margins look weak, change the product, the supplier, the price or the discount before scaling ads.
  • Winners are identified the same way, by comparing margins across orders.
  • Compare net margins across suppliers and across products from the same supplier. **
  • Profit per order varies between both. **

Supplier price and wholesale margin

1 min
  • Judge supplier margins against ad costs and price spread.
  • If competitors sell at a 10 percent markup (a price increase on cost), there is often not enough margin left for profitable advertising.
  • Spread = selling price − wholesale cost.
  • Net per product = spread − estimated shipping cost.
  • Net is the money left to fund ad spend. A product with negative or tiny net cannot be run on ads profitably.
  • Verify marketplace commission rates on the platform's own fee page for your product category before setting prices.
  • Commission rates change over time and differ by category.
  • Using old figures from memory can leave margins negative on low-priced products.

Channel fees and cost categories

1 min
  • Track each sales channel's fees and margin separately.
  • A product profitable on one channel can lose money on another.
  • Recalculate margins before expanding a catalogue to a new marketplace.
  • Profit calculations must include: product cost, product shipping, dropshipping fees (fees for shipping orders straight from the supplier), handling fees, payment processor fees and paid advertising.
  • In dropshipping, paid advertising is the cost most often left out.

Returns, chargebacks and refund buffers

1 min
  • Keep the 3% to 5% buffer (money set aside as a safety margin) for returns, chargebacks, disputes and payment fees.
  • This buffer sits on top of product cost and shipping.
  • Jewellery is a low-return category, with return rates typically under 5 percent per month.
  • Returns (items sent back) are mostly caused by sizing issues, such as rings or necklaces that do not fit well.
  • Footwear: do not assume zero refunds for shoes.
  • Calculate an expected refund rate (the share of sales you expect to refund) into the margin before setting the price.
  • Footwear refunds are a normal cost of the category.
  • Average refunds are a deduction in the full per-order profit formula.

Shipping and bulky-item checks

1 min

Bulky items are large or heavy products.

  1. Get a real shipping figure for each product. Shipping cost depends on the distance between the supplier warehouse and the customer, so use an averaged estimate per product. The example used a made-up $150 average as a placeholder; replace it with real carrier or supplier quotes.
  2. Subtract estimated shipping from the spread to get net per product.
  3. Check that shipping is in the supplier total and in the net margin before any ad spend.
  • Costs that also apply in the per-order view: inbound freight (air or sea), 3PL pick and pack, and last mile / courier cost.
  • Build the profit sheet per SKU (stock keeping unit, one product code) and per region.
  • Tax, payment, freight, duty and 3PL rates differ by region.
  • Steps:
    • Step 1: enter the product cost and stock rate.
    • Step 2: convert to USD using the exchange rate.
    • Step 3: add inbound freight, payment charges, pick-pack, last mile, refunds and the offer discount.
    • Result: gross margin per unit.
  • BOGO (buy one, get one) on the same product: divide the shipping cost (last mile and pick-pack) by two units when calculating per-unit margin.
  • This is because two items go in one parcel.
  • Dollar-off and percentage-off offers do not change the per-unit shipping cost. Free-gift and BOGO offers do.
  • BOGO on the same product: the effective discount per unit is 50% of the price.
  • The final RRP per unit is the price the customer pays for each item after the discount. Gross margin is computed from that.

1 min
  • Edit only the input cells.
  • Do not change any cell marked in red in the margin spreadsheet, because those cells hold the built-in calculations.
  • Only the product cost and selling price input cells should be edited.

Procedure: check which products can carry ads

1 min

Check which products can carry ads before spending on them.

  1. Export all products from Shopify as a CSV (a spreadsheet file). This does not delete or change anything.
  2. Add a column with the wholesale price from each supplier.
  3. Add a column called spread (selling price minus wholesale price).
  4. Add a column with an estimated shipping cost per product.
  5. Add a column called net (spread minus shipping).
  6. Sort by net. Products with negative or very thin net are losing products for paid traffic (visitors from paid ads).
  • Check net margin per product before the first ad dollar is spent.
  • Spending thousands on ads for products without enough margin after shipping burns budget with no path to profit.
  • Track ad spend at a level more specific than the whole business.
  • A product or brand can be a net loss that only shows up once its advertising cost is included. Without this you can lose money unknowingly.
  • Focus ad budget and time on products that produce high profit per order. **
  • Products that earn little, break even or lose money should get less ad spend, be removed, or be repriced (have the price changed). **
  • Profit per order differs between suppliers and between products from the same supplier. **
  • Know your own unit economics (the profit and cost numbers for one sale), because they differ for every business.
  • Without them you cannot judge when to scale Meta ads, when to cut ads, or whether a product will work at all.
  • Avoid offers that break the margin.
  • Brands often launch offers such as a BOGO at a 25-30% MER without adjusting the marketing spend target, then lose money for the month.
  • They also skip proper triple-whale setup (a tracking app that measures profit) and daily profit tracking, so they do not notice the loss on each order.

  • Discounted price = original price x (1 - discount rate).
  • Example: a 6.5% discount on a price is calculated as the price x 0.935.
  • Use a store margin check before adding a product.
  • The price must cover the product, shipping, payment processing and an ad budget, leaving a net profit.
  • If the spread is too small, look for better supplier pricing or drop the product.
