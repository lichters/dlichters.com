Why numbers come first: profit, cash and knowing your business

Section 19.1 · 1 of 23 in this chapter · 20 min read

In short

  • Know your numbers. You cannot fix or grow what you cannot see.
  • Profit is what you keep. Cash is what is in the bank. They are different, so check both.
  • Judge each sale by its net profit, which is what remains after all costs.
  • Check cash flow every month, so you can pay suppliers and ads on time.
  • Track a few key numbers, such as profit per sale and cost per sale.
More key points
  • Set your big yearly targets first, then work backward to the 90-day goals.
  • Turn every goal into exact numbers, such as how many customers you need and how much you keep per order.
  • Growth must stay profitable, so learn your money numbers before and during growth.
  • Beginners start the beginner finance path as soon as the business launches. Owners with bookkeeping already in place use the advanced path.

Why numbers come first

1 min

Knowing your numbers is a precondition for growth

1 min
  • A precondition is something that must be true first. Accurate financial data is the basis of growth. Without it, you cannot see what causes your results, so growth stalls at a ceiling you cannot figure out.
  • Bookkeeping means writing down every amount of money coming in and going out. It is not mainly about paying taxes or having records for an audit.
  • Its main value is showing cause and effect. Cause and effect means seeing how each change in the business affects results.
  • Knowing your numbers means understanding cash flow, which is the money moving in and out of your bank account over time.
  • Knowing your numbers also means understanding profitability, which is how much you keep from each sale.
  • Knowing your numbers also means understanding revenue, which is all money from sales before costs.
  • Knowing your numbers is more than just recording expenses.
  • Budgeting means planning how to spend money in advance. Strategic planning means setting long-term goals and steps. Both are based on your financial numbers.
  • Budgeting and strategic planning can take a business to seven- and eight-figure revenue, which means millions to tens of millions of dollars per year.
  • Budgeting and strategic planning work as a framework on top of marketing and sales. They do not replace marketing and sales.

Numbers replace guessing

1 min
  • Reviewing actual figures replaces emotional guessing about the business's health.
  • Looking at real figures stops self-doubt and anxiety from building on false assumptions.
  • Real figures give a factual basis for decisions. This brings confidence, stability and stress relief.
  • Base every campaign decision on strict data analysis, not on emotion.
  • Reacting emotionally to campaign numbers is one of the most costly mistakes in paid traffic, which means visitors you pay for through ads.
  • Making ad and store decisions on gut feeling is the outdated approach.
  • Analysing actual ad and sales data is what AI is meant to replace the gut feeling with.
  • Early paid traffic gives a new store real visitor data. It shows how customers behave on the site.
  • The owner decides from measured behaviour, not personal assumptions.
  • The owner is usually not the typical customer, so personal assumptions are often wrong.
  • Track your own numbers closely. These include traffic, sales, margins and conversion, which is the share of visitors who buy.
  • Do not rely only on reports from others.
  • Most stores that struggle have figures that are poorly organised.
  • Track all customer and order metrics. A metric is a number that measures something.
  • What gets measured gets actively managed.
  • Unmeasured data invites oversights and failures.

Knowing your numbers early

1 min
  • Even if the numbers look worse than you expect, you must know them so you can change something.
  • A decline you do not see keeps running until it surfaces later and hits harder.
  • Cash flow problems happen at the current stage and later as the business grows.
  • Reviewing the numbers regularly lets you anticipate problems before they become a crisis.
  • Accurate conversion tracking shows which sources and campaigns generate sales.
  • Conversion tracking helps you make evidence-based decisions about spend allocation, which means where to put your ad money.
  • Conversion tracking also helps with bidding, which means how much you pay per ad click or view.
  • Conversion tracking shows whether you are hitting your profitability targets.
  • A healthy business needs three management systems.
  • System one is bookkeeping, to track financial numbers.
  • System two is Profit First, a method for managing cash flow.
  • System three is strategic planning, to achieve scaling. Scaling means growing sales and spending together.
  • In the build phase, track four things.
  • Track pricing and profit margins. Margin is the share of each sale kept as profit.
  • Track profit per order after shipping and fees.
  • Track a basic P&L (profit and loss statement), which is a report of income minus expenses.
  • Keep business and personal accounts strictly separate.
  • Check profit per order before scaling any product.

Revenue, profit and cash flow

1 min

Definitions

1 min

  • Profit is income minus expenses. It is also called net income.

  • Profit is not the cash in your bank account. Never compare a profit figure against the bank balance.

  • Profit equals revenue minus expenses.

  • Expenses include cost of goods, ad spend, refunds, chargebacks and operating costs. A chargeback is when a customer's payment is reversed and the money is taken back from you.

  • Only the cash left after all of these is money you keep.

    Profit = Revenue − (cost of goods + ad spend + refunds + chargebacks + operating costs)

  • Net sales equal total sales minus returns. Returns are products sent back by customers.

  • Track net sales, not gross sales. Gross sales are total sales before returns.

  • Example: $236,080.79 total sales minus $2,877.60 returns gives $233,203.19 net sales.

  • Profit cannot exist without revenue, so revenue is a necessary input.

  • The useful question is which revenue actually produces profit after all costs.

  • Revenue is not profit.

  • To find profit, subtract all variable costs from total sales. Variable costs change with sales, such as cost of goods, shipping and customer acquisition cost.

  • To find profit, also subtract all fixed costs. Fixed costs are due every month, whatever you sell.

  • E-commerce profit depends on selling each item for more than its full landed cost. Landed cost is the full price to get the product to you, including shipping and duties.

  • Know the margin before scaling.

  • Profit per order is the basic number to understand at any stage.

Net profit versus revenue

1 min
  • Judge every sale by its net profit, not its gross value.
  • Missing a few sales a month can cost $1,000 or $1,500 in net profit.
  • Judge a store by net profit after all expenses.
  • Expenses include product cost, shipping, marketing and overheads. Overheads are regular running costs.
  • Do not judge a store by gross sales. Gross sales can look far larger than what the owner keeps.
  • Net profit matters more than revenue.
  • A store with lower revenue but equal profit avoids much of the work of fulfilment, bulk buying and customer service. Fulfilment means packing and shipping orders.
  • High revenue alone is not a sign of a healthy business.
  • Net profit is the figure to track.
  • Net profit pays bills, funds growth and can become a second income stream that makes a job optional.
  • Monitor these every month to see how net profit moves: cost of goods, shipping cost, customer acquisition cost and software costs.
  • Revenue alone is a misleading success metric for an e-commerce store. A success metric is a number that shows whether the business is succeeding.
  • Gross sales can rise while the business loses money.
  • Let profit and cash flow drive decisions about growth.
  • Revenue alone can mislead.
  • Revenue can fall while profit margin rises. Profit margin is profit as a share of revenue.
  • Track profit margin alongside top-line sales. Top-line sales means total sales.
  • Compare profit and margin before judging performance.
  • Revenue growth alone does not prove a business is profitable.
  • A store can keep making sales while losing money.
  • Check profit and cash flow separately from top-line sales when judging whether growth is healthy.
  • Lower revenue with higher margins can be easier to run than higher revenue with thin margins.
  • Lower revenue with higher margins means fewer orders, less customer service and less fulfilment work for the same or better profit.
  • Margin per order is therefore a stronger target than sales volume.

Cash flow versus profit

1 min
  • Net profit on the P&L does not show how much cash is available.
  • Money can look like profit while it is already committed to supplier invoices, taxes or refunds.
  • So track cash separately from profit.
  • Profit on the P&L and cash in the bank are different measures.
  • They can diverge for a growing e-commerce store.
  • Track cash flow separately from profit.
  • Treat any gap between the two as something to investigate.
  • Cash keeps a business alive, so cash flow timing matters as much as the profit figure.
  • A store can be profitable on paper and still fail. This happens if money arrives too late to pay suppliers and ad platforms.
  • Dropshipping means the supplier ships the product straight to the customer, so you do not hold stock.
  • Dropshipping is a cash flow business.
  • A store can show a healthy paper profit while running short of cash after paying suppliers.
  • Know your real profit and loss before scaling.
  • Cash flow can destroy a business even when profit looks healthy on paper.
  • Misreading cash flow is a common way owners get into serious trouble.
  • In the grow phase, the core measures are cash flow and profit, not revenue.
  • Review profit and cash position in every reporting period, not only top-line sales.
  • Profit is the easier figure to set as a growth goal. Most people also understand it more easily.
  • Cash flow decides whether suppliers and ad platforms can be paid on time.
  • Set the headline target in profit.
  • Monitor cash flow in the same report. Do not choose one over the other.

Cost structure and the monthly profit calculation

2 min

Variable and fixed costs:

Cost type Behaviour Examples and notes
Variable Occurs only when a sale happens or when you choose to spend. With zero sales, cost of goods and shipping are zero. Cost of goods, shipping, customer acquisition cost
Fixed Due every month regardless of sales. Platform subscription, domain
Customer acquisition cost (CAC) Counted as variable because you choose whether to spend it, but it must be spent to keep sales coming in. Stopping ad spend usually stops new sales.
Domain About 12 USD per year, roughly 1 USD per month as a fixed cost.
Virtual assistant or paid software Add to the expense list once you use them, for example a virtual assistant at 400 USD per month. Beginners often don't need them at first.
  • A fixed monthly platform fee becomes a smaller cost per sale as volume grows.
  • Example: 29 USD spread over one sale is 29 USD each.
  • Example: 29 USD spread over 10 sales is 2.90 USD each.

Monthly profit steps:

  1. Add up the total sales revenue for the month.
  2. Subtract the cost of goods (COGS, what each product costs you) for every product sold.
  3. Subtract the shipping fees paid to suppliers for every order.
  4. Subtract customer acquisition cost (the ad spend).
  5. Subtract fixed costs such as the Shopify subscription and the domain. Shopify is the online store platform.
  6. Subtract card processing fees (0.30 USD per transaction plus 2.9% of sales). These are the fees your payment provider takes.
  7. The remaining amount is the net profit for the month.

Formula:

Net profit = Revenue − (COGS + shipping + customer acquisition cost) − fixed costs − card processing fees
Card processing fees = number of transactions × 0.30 USD + 2.9% of total sales

Worked example for one 1,000 USD sale in a month:

Item USD
Sale 1,000
COGS 400
Shipping 100
Customer acquisition cost 100
Shopify 29
Domain (approx.) 1
Total spending 630
Net profit 370

The example leaves out card processing fees.

Common errors:

  • The per-unit fulfilment figure of 600 USD does not match its parts.
  • Its parts are 400 USD COGS plus 100 USD shipping, which is 500 USD.
  • At 500 USD per unit, 50 units cost 25,000 USD.
  • Profit before ad spend and card fees is 24,970 USD, not 19,970 USD.
  • Profit figures that leave out customer acquisition cost and card processing fees overstate real profit.
  • Subtract both before judging how a month went.

Note: the figures in the common-errors example do not all reconcile from the text alone. Check them against the original numbers before quoting them.

Judging a store: success metrics and optimisation metrics

1 min

Which numbers decide success

1 min
  • Success metrics are the final source of truth for whether a business is on track.
  • Optimisation metrics show whether you are moving toward a goal and where you can improve.
  • Optimisation metrics support success metrics. They do not replace them.
  • For an e-commerce business, the two core success metrics are profit per sale and cost per sale.
  • These two numbers are enough to judge whether the business is succeeding.
  • Judge the business by profit, not by the conversion rate alone.
  • A store can have a low conversion rate and high profitability.
  • A store can also have a high conversion rate and no profit.
  • A high conversion rate does not automatically mean profit.
  • Average order value (the average amount a customer spends per order) and the returning customer rate often grow revenue with less effort.
  • Squeezing the conversion rate further is often harder than growing those two numbers.
  • A high conversion rate does not guarantee profit.
  • Also track average order value, returning customer rate, customer acquisition cost and customer lifetime value (the total a customer spends with you over time).
  • A store can convert well and still lose money if these numbers are weak.

Vanity metrics

1 min
  • Vanity metrics look impressive but can mislead. Examples are raw revenue, video views or subscriber counts.
  • Focus on profit, paid installations and numbers that reflect what you actually keep.
  • Publicly shared revenue screenshots rarely show costs, refunds, chargebacks or cost of goods.
  • So they say nothing about real profitability.
  • Judging your own business by someone else's gross sales is a trap.
  • A benchmark claimed for high-ticket dropshipping is an annual revenue run rate of $10 million or more for some stores. A run rate is the yearly pace taken from a short period.
  • This is the instructor's stated target and was not independently verified.
  • Treat it as an upper ambition, not a planning figure.

Early reading targets

1 min
  • After about three months, a beginner should read these numbers without help.
  • In Meta Business Manager (Meta's ad tool), read CTR, CPM and CPC. CTR is the share of people who click an ad after seeing it. CPM is the cost per 1,000 views. CPC is the cost per click.
  • In the Shopify dashboard, read Add to Cart, Initiate Checkout and Sales. Add to Cart means a shopper put an item in the basket. Initiate Checkout means a shopper started paying.
  • These numbers show which decision to take next.

Store-level example cases

2 min
  • A Shopify store had about $720,000 in online store revenue from 366 orders in one month.
  • Its conversion rate was 0.56%.
  • Total sales across all channels were about $868,000.
  • Roughly 60 to 80 orders came from other channels, such as Amazon and draft orders. Draft orders are orders the store creates by hand.
  • The store normally converts about 0.4% of its traffic. This month was above its usual rate.
  • Case: a high-ticket dropshipping store run by a small team with very low overheads.
  • In one month it made about $720,000 in online store revenue.
  • Its conversion rate was 0.56%, against a usual rate of about 0.4% of traffic.
  • Profit margin on those sales was about 12.5%.
  • That came to close to $100,000 profit that month, using roughly $14,000 in ad spend.
  • The result was judged a very successful business. This was despite the low conversion rate and thin margin.
  • Note: 12.5% of $720,000 is $90,000, so "close to $100,000" does not follow exactly from these two figures. Check the underlying numbers before quoting it.
  • Infrared sauna store, July (example year 2021): sales were 227,949.55 USD from 134 orders and 23,382 sessions. A session is one visit to the store.
  • Returns were 23,721.80 USD, so gross sales were 251,671.35 USD.
  • Net profit after all expenses was 50,148.90 USD, a 22 percent margin.
  • Single-day result from a hardtop gazebo store (July 15, example year 2021): 9 orders produced 23,175.20 USD in sales.
  • The sales had 100 USD in returns.
  • The store only needed to forward the 9 orders to the supplier.
  • Profit after all expenses was 4,171.50 USD, an 18 percent margin.
  • Most of the cost that day was marketing spend.
  • Air purifier store, about 12 months old (July example): 137 orders produced 65,170 USD in net sales.
  • Gross sales before returns were 69,607.60 USD, with returns of 4,437.60 USD.
  • The margin was 19 percent, with 12,336.68 USD net profit.
  • The owner needed about one to two hours per day to manage it.

Monthly review in the Shopify report

1 min
  • For a monthly review, read Total sales, Returns and Sessions in the Shopify Online Store report.
  • Compare Total sales minus Returns with the net profit after expenses.
  • Visitors are shown next to sessions to check conversion.

Red flags of hidden losses

2 min
  • A store can look successful on sales.
  • It can still barely break even or lose money once all expenses are counted.
  • Review the full cost picture regularly.
  • Do this so you do not discover the losses only during an emergency.
  • A store can spend $1 million on ads to generate $1 million in revenue.
  • It can still lose everything if it then owes about $500,000 in cost of goods that it cannot cover.
  • Check ad spend and product cost against revenue before scaling.
  • Spending $100 on ads with no add-to-cart actions or inbound interest is a red flag.
  • Use that signal to optimise the campaigns and the site. Optimise means improve.
  • Do not use it as a reason to close the store.
  • Plan for losses in early months.
  • A store may be negative for months while testing.
  • Profitability should be reached through data-driven tests.
  • Missing a decline is the main risk.
  • Know the numbers so that a falling trend is spotted in the period it starts.

Revenue drop case

  • In 2018 the speaker's store revenue declined. He panicked and aimed to win back sales.
  • Later analysis showed he had been more profitable on the lower revenue.
  • His lower revenue also meant less work.
  • The lesson: check profit per period before reacting to a revenue drop.

Chair store case

  • An online chair store had a record year.
  • Then sales fell sharply starting in January of the following year. They kept falling for months.
  • By May the owner had lost confidence and feared the business was failing.
  • The owner was advised to sell the store before it reached zero, since a buyer would still pay something for it.
  • Selling required four years of complete financial records.
  • The owner's accountant's reports were hard to understand and seemed inconsistent.
  • So the owner reviewed the figures personally.
  • The review showed that revenue was declining but profit was rising on lower revenue.
  • So the business was healthier than the owner believed.
  • The store was then listed on a brokerage marketplace, which is a website where businesses are sold.
  • Sales picked up within weeks of the listing.

Owner finances

1 min

Check the numbers yourself

1 min
  • Accepting accountant reports you do not understand is risky.
  • This is especially true when something looks off and you cannot get clear answers.
  • Learn to read the financial statements yourself.
  • Then you can verify what you are told.
  • If your accountant's reports are not understandable to you, they are not supporting your decisions.
  • Ask for plain-language explanations.
  • Change accountant if they cannot give answers you can act on.

Recurring financial questions

1 min

Check these questions regularly. Answer each one with numbers:

  1. Are my products profitable?
  2. Are my sales profitable?
  3. Where is my money going? If my P&L shows profit, where did that profit go?
  4. Is there more cash in my bank account than expected, and is that money actually available?
  5. How much do I owe my suppliers?
  6. How much can I afford to pay a virtual assistant, and can I afford three or ten?
  7. Can I afford a store manager or a PPC agency? PPC (pay-per-click) agency means a team that runs ads for you.

Monthly and period review

1 min

Review each period in this order:

  1. Review the store's profit and loss report for the period.
  2. Check the cash position. Compare when money arrives with when it must be paid out. Payouts include supplier costs, ad spend, refunds and chargebacks.
  3. Compare profit and cash flow with the previous period. Do not compare revenue alone.
  4. Decide on scaling or cost cuts only after profit and cash flow have been checked.

Owner's personal finances

1 min
  • Describe the owner's personal financial situation separately from the business figures.
  • Personal income is also kept separate.
  • This shows what the business is meant to provide personally.
  • Business revenue and business profit are never passive income. Passive income is money that arrives without daily work.
  • The goal is to generate cash flow from the business.
  • Then invest that cash in assets that produce passive income.

Early-stage expectations

1 min
  • Profitability within the first month is a realistic early expectation for a high-ticket dropshipping store.
  • Earning a few months' extra profit is a shorter goal.
  • Reaching millions in annual revenue may take a couple of years.
  • Example profitability curve from a Shopify store: month one earned $4,000 in revenue with a loss of $2,000.
  • Month two earned $6,000 with a loss of $600.
  • Profitability was targeted about two months after launch.
  • A beginner plan can pair a 3,000 USD profit goal over about two months.
  • Pair it with operational targets of 100 live products and 40 unique visitors per day.
  • Set a target net margin and adjust it over time. Net margin is the share of sales kept after all costs.
  • Example: aim for a 20% net margin in the first three months.
  • Then raise the target to 25% for the next three months.
  • Milestone targets for this roadmap: first, scale beyond $10k in monthly revenue.
  • Then aim for six-figure income.

Note on timing: the sources differ on when a store should reach profitability. The 2021-22 high-ticket source says the first month is realistic. The Source C example targets about two months after launch. The Source A beginner plan uses about two months for its 3,000 USD profit goal. Use the first-month figure only for high-ticket stores with very low overheads. Plan for about two months otherwise.

Growth goals as measurable numbers (numbers you can count and track)

1 min

Set the vision figures (big long-term targets)

1 min
  • Set the 10-year vision figures for annual revenue (all money coming in during one year), profit per year (money kept after all costs), and profit margin (the share of revenue kept as profit). These are the main business-level targets to put in the vision.
  • The 90-day revenue and profit targets (goals for the next 90 days) and other measurables (numbers you can count) are calculated backward from the one-year plan. Example: a one-year revenue target of $1,000,000 can translate into a 90-day revenue target of $300,000.
  • In the example, a one-year revenue target of $1,000,000 is broken into a 90-day revenue target of $300,000, which is 30 percent of the annual figure. The 90-day profit and any other measurables are then calculated backward from the same one-year plan.

Make measurables concrete (turn them into exact numbers)

1 min
  • Define measurables as concrete numbers, not vague ideas. For example, a 1-million-dollar revenue target with a 300,000-dollar profit goal should be turned into specific customer counts and per-order figures (the money from each single order).
  • Estimate the number of customers needed by dividing the revenue target by the price of the product sold. Example: 1 million dollars in revenue at a product price of 1,000 dollars needs 1,000 customers, or about 750 customers if repeat business (customers who buy again) accounts for part of the revenue (a 25% repeat share in that example).
  • Growth must stay profitable. Sustainable growth (growth that lasts) in an active store (a store with ongoing sales) means building a clear understanding of financial numbers, including bookkeeping (recording every dollar in and out), profit and cash flow (the timing of money coming in and going out), before and during expansion. The aim is to grow without losing profitability.

Beginner versus advanced finance path (which path fits you)

1 min

Which finance path to take

1 min
  • Every member should finish at least one of the two finance paths (step-by-step study tracks), regardless of stage, because finance is not optional. Which path to start with depends on whether bookkeeping is already in place.
  • The beginner finance path is for owners who are brand new to business, or who have just launched but do not yet have a solid bookkeeping method. Study it as soon as the business launches.
  • The advanced finance path is for owners who already run a basic bookkeeping system (a simple method for recording money) that tracks profit, losses, invoices (bills sent to customers), sales and costs. It focuses on using financial numbers to grow the business profitably.
  • The Know Your Numbers Advance path (Know Your Numbers means learning your key money figures) is aimed at businesses that already have a live store (a store taking real orders) and sales, and want to grow. It is more advanced than the earlier Know Your Numbers material, which is the Build Phase in the Source B program for high-ticket dropshipping (selling expensive products that a supplier ships to customers for you).

What the beginner path covers

1 min
  • The beginner finance path has 15 topics. Among them are an introduction to knowing your numbers, a topic called What You Need Now, profit per order (the money you keep from one sale), generating a profit and loss statement (a report of income minus costs), and when to level up (when to move to the next stage).
  • The beginner finance path covers calculating profit per order, generating a profit and loss statement, and recognising when to level up. These basics are needed before scaling (growing sales) any high-ticket dropshipping business.
