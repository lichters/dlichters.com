Playbook › 19. Metrics, finance and tracking

Profit tracking systems: per-order sheets, daily P&L trackers and profit apps

Section 19.16 · 16 of 23 in this chapter · 52 min read

In short

  • Track your profit with a Shopify app or a spreadsheet. Without this, you can lose money and not see it.
  • Check profit for each order. Total sales alone do not show which products make money.
  • Subtract every cost: product cost, shipping, payment fees and tax. What is left is your profit.
  • Use your own copy of each template, and check the formulas before you trust the numbers.
  • Write down all money in and all costs, daily or weekly. Profit is what is left.
More key points
  • Profit = money from sales minus all costs, and refunds count as costs.
  • Monthly fixed costs, such as the Shopify plan, are not in the per-order sheet. Add them separately.
  • Profit apps fill in most costs for you. Add the rest by hand, about 10 to 20%.
  • Type numbers only into white input cells. Never type over the calculated cells.
  • While you are still finding out which products make money, check the profit of every sale.
  • Once your products and supplier costs are stable, check less often: quick spot checks and weekly or monthly reviews.
  • Use real costs (supplier price, shipping, payment fees) and the money you actually receive, not the sales total.
  • Small typing mistakes break your totals: wrong years in dates, a "$" sign in an amount, or a missing category.

Why profit tracking is required

1 min
  • Always track profit with either a Shopify app (an add-on that connects to your store) or a spreadsheet. Without seeing your profit, you cannot scale well (spend more on what works), and you can lose money without knowing it.
  • Automate profit tracking once the store runs. Then margin (profit as a share of the sale price) is measured for each order, not guessed from totals.
  • Spreadsheets are hard to keep accurate for profit tracking. Every payment and cost source must be connected by hand.
  • Profit apps fix this. They pull data automatically from payment providers (companies that handle card payments).
  • Track profit for each individual order, not only total revenue (all money from sales). This shows which products lose money, which prices cause problems, and which products win.
  • A simple spreadsheet is enough while order volume (the number of orders) is low, for example a few orders per day.
  • Track the net profit (what is left after all costs) of every sale in a profit spreadsheet. This way, decisions about which products to scale or cut are based on real results per sale.
  • A clear view of gross spend (total money spent on ads) and net return (what comes back after costs) is essential once sales start. Without it, you cannot tell if a product makes money or loses it.
  • This matters most when a product is being tested, and when the store moves into the scaling phase.

Choosing a tracking system

1 min
  • Review the current financial tracking system and check whether it gives a clear view of the business.
  • If it does not work or leaves room for improvement, compare it with a proven tracking system. Use that one if it fits the business.
  • Google Sheets (Google's free online spreadsheet) is enough for margin tracking at the start.
  • Accounting software such as Xero (online bookkeeping software) can come later.
  • The December 2020 coaching call lists Wholesale Cost Tracking as a resource for tracking supplier (wholesale) costs. The source names it without describing how to set it up.
  • 2023: A profit tracking spreadsheet is enough for a new store's bookkeeping (recording money in and out).
  • 2023: It costs nothing and tracks revenue, cost of goods (COGS, what the products cost you to buy), shipping, ad spend and profit.
  • 2023: Use a profit tracking spreadsheet to track business profit.
  • 2023: Xero is the accounting platform used for the business.
  • 2023: Google Sheets was used for years before that and supported over $10 million in sales.
  • 2024: Use Excel (Microsoft's spreadsheet program) for product-level profit tracking. This shows which products to scale or drop.
  • 2024: The team later moved accounting to Xero, which is not day-to-day work.
  • Shopify's own analytics (built-in reports) shows all store reports and activity.
  • When Shopify Payments (Shopify's own card payment service) is used, payments and finances are also visible in the same admin (Shopify's back office).
  • Profit apps are the alternative to spreadsheets. See the profit apps section below.

Per-order profit sheets (Google Sheets)

5 min
  • Calculate profit margin on every individual order, not just blended profit across all sales combined.
  • Apply it retroactively, meaning going back and doing it for past orders, to all January and February orders.
  • A downloadable Profit Tracking Worksheet is included.
  • It is used to track ROI (return on investment, how much you get back from money spent) indicators.
  • It monitors profitability by product and by campaign (an ad push), and supports data-driven scaling decisions (choices based on the numbers).
  • Track sales price, COGS, shipping cost and profit per order in separate columns.
  • Calculate profit margin order by order.
  • procedure:
    1. Track profit per order in Google Sheets with columns: order date, sale price, COGS, shipping, total profit.
    2. Assign this tracking to a virtual assistant (a remote helper you hire) as order volume increases.
    3. Periodically review overall business profit. Take distributions (money you pull out of the business) based on monthly or weekly profit, not on a per-order basis.
  • procedure:
    1. Build a sheet with one row per order.
    2. Record the sale price, product cost, merchant fees (charges the payment company takes per sale), shipping and ad cost.
    3. Compare profit per order to see which products make real money and which need a higher price or removal.

Setting up the order sheet

  • Setup procedure:
    1. Make a copy. Open the template link and choose File > Make a Copy. Keep the default name "Copy of" and click OK. The file is then editable in your own Google Drive (Google's file storage).
    2. Duplicate the backup. Right-click the tab labelled Duplicate this PNL and choose Duplicate. This keeps an untouched backup of the formulas (the calculations).
    3. Rename the working tab. Right-click the working tab and choose Rename to give it your own name.
    4. Enter data only in the working tab. Keep the backup tab as the reset copy.
  • Keep the blank template tab "DUPLICATE THIS - P&L" unchanged. (P&L means profit and loss.)
  • Copy that blank tab for each new tracking period or store. The column structure stays the same across all reports.
  • Column structure of the order-level profit sheet, tab "DUPLICATE THIS - P&L", with one example row.

Column words: Order # is the order number. Date is when the order was placed. Net Profit is what is left after all costs. Net Margin is that profit as a share of the customer's payment. QTY is the quantity (units bought). Customer Paid is what the customer paid. Promotion Applied is any discount used. COGS is the supplier's product cost. Shipping is the delivery cost. Tax Collected is sales tax taken from the customer. Merchant Fees are the payment company's charges.

  • The Date column stores a spreadsheet date serial (a number Excel uses for dates; 36526 = 2000-01-01 in Excel). Format it as a date before reading it.
  • Net profit per order = Customer Paid − COGS − Shipping − Tax Collected − Merchant Fees. Example: 1000 − 500 − 100 − 63 − 29.3 = 307.7.
  • Net margin per order = Net Profit ÷ Customer Paid. Example: 307.7 ÷ 1000 = 0.3077, i.e. 30.77%.
  • Deduct the tax collected from order revenue. It is passed on to the tax authority (the government), not kept as income.
  • Leaving tax out overstates net profit and margin on every sale that carries sales tax or VAT (a sales tax used in many countries).
  • Record every order on its own row with the supplier, product, quantity and any promotion applied.
  • This shows which suppliers, products and discounts actually produce profit, instead of total revenue only.

Entering one order

  • Entry procedure:
    1. Replace the example data with the Shopify order number and the date the order was placed.
    2. Enter the supplier name and the product name.
    3. Enter Quantity as the number of units the customer bought.
    4. Enter Customer Paid as the amount shown in the Shopify orders section.
    5. Enter any promotion used in Promotions Applied.
    6. Enter COGS from the supplier invoice (the bill from your supplier).
    7. Enter Shipping and Tax Collected. Leave Merchant Fees to the formula (the automatic calculation).
    8. Read Net Profit and Net Margin at the top of the sheet. They update automatically.
  • Customer Paid must be the amount the customer actually paid, as shown in Shopify. It is not the product list price.
  • If the customer paid for shipping, that amount belongs in Customer Paid, not in the shipping field.
  • Promotions Applied is only a reference column. It does not change any calculation.
  • Record each discount so you can later see which promotions were linked to higher or lower profit. An example is a 10% off storewide sale (a discount on the whole shop).
  • To add more orders: highlight the full row with all order data and formulas. Then drag the fill handle (the small square at the bottom right of a selected cell) down to copy the row.
  • The copied rows keep the calculation formulas. Overwrite only the input cells (the cells you type in) for each new order.
  • Ad spend is not entered in the per-order sheet, because it varies from order to order.
  • Track ad spend separately. Analyse it with ROAS (return on ad spend, revenue divided by ad spend) alongside the net margin figures.
  • period-level formulas:
    • Profit per order = sale price − Shopify card processing fee (2.9% for Shopify Payments credit card transactions in the source year 2021) − supplier cost − shipping cost.
    • Profit after ads and overheads (fixed running costs) = total profit per period − Google Ads, Facebook Ads and Bing Ads costs − 29 dollars per month for Shopify − other fees.
  • margin tracking in a spreadsheet:
    1. Create a sheet for each week or month.
    2. For every sale, enter the sale price, Shopify fees, supplier product price and shipping cost.
    3. Calculate profit per order.
    4. Add that period's Google Ads, Facebook Ads, Bing Ads, 29 dollars Shopify fee and other fees.
    5. Calculate the margin for the period.

Acting on per-order results

  • Example: after two months with 12 sales, six sales earned $400 net profit each, three earned $200, and three earned $50.
  • Investigate the $50 sales. Check for too much ad spend or higher shipping cost than expected.
  • Then cut or raise ad spend on the products that earn most.

Order-level tracker from Source B

3 min
  • Tool: The Order Profit Tracker is a spreadsheet. It calculates net profit and net margin for each order.
  • It works out these from the sale amount minus supplier cost, shipping, payment processor fees (the fees the payment company charges) and other costs.
  • It is used to know profit per order. Its instructions include a way to automate the data entry.
  • Each order is entered with: the order amount, any discount, the payment processor fee, sales tax, the supplier total, the cost of products, shipping, other process fees and the amount received.
  • Net profit and net margin are calculated from these entries.
  • The tracker also reports a gross profit margin (profit before some costs).
  • Rely on the net margin for decisions. It shows what remains after shipping and all other order costs are subtracted.
  • During the early build phase, calculate the profit of every single order.
  • Margins only become clear once real supplier costs, shipping, processor fees and fraud fees (charges for risky or fake orders) are included.
  • Enter and check the profit of at least the first 5 to 10 orders yourself. Do this before handing the task to someone else.
  • Logging procedure for one order in the Order Profit Tracker:
    1. Note the order when the store sends a new-sale notification.
    2. Place the order with the supplier. Wait for the invoice or order confirmation that lists all costs.
    3. Open the Profit Per Order tab and add a new row with the order number and date.
    4. Enter the customer name, the product and the quantity. Quantity is for reference only and does not change the calculation.
    5. Enter the order amount: the total you actually received from the customer.
    6. Enter the discount you gave. It is recorded for reference. The order amount already reflects what was received.
    7. Select the payment method so it matches what Shopify fills in automatically.
    8. Enter the sales tax. Leave it at 0 if no sales tax was charged.
    9. Enter the supplier total: the product cost plus the shipping cost from the supplier invoice. Add the cost of any shipping you paid yourself through a freight forwarder (a company that moves goods) or UPS (a parcel delivery company) on your own account.
    10. Keep the shipping cost in its own separate cell as a reference note.
    11. Read the automatic values: processor fees, fraud insurance, total cost, gross profit, net profit and net margin.
  • For an order split across several suppliers, enter one sum of all invoices. Include each separately paid shipping cost. Use a typed equation such as =1500+500 (or =1400+100+500 if a 100 shipping cost is paid separately).
  • Example check: 3,000 − 2,000 − 87.30 − 30 = 882.70.
  • Fraud insurance is entered in the insurance section. The example is Signifyd (a fraud-check company) at 1% of the order amount.
  • The insurance section has a checkbox column "Insure Checks/Financed Orders?".
  • If you do not use fraud insurance, delete that fee.
  • dropshipping COGS: Recording COGS on an accrual basis (matching each cost to the sale it belongs to) for each individual order is effectively impossible for dropshipping (the supplier ships straight to the customer).
  • That method needs accurate supplier invoices for every order. Invoices change over time, and suppliers do not standardize their data (use the same format).
  • The work is very large, the benefit is small, and the return on investment is negative.
  • Using an order profit tracker to calculate profit on every individual order is not recommended for dropshipping.
  • For the same reason as per-order COGS accrual, supplier invoices change and are hard to keep accurate. The effort is large, and the information gained is small.
  • growth phase (the fast-growing stage): Recording every single order has diminishing returns. Each extra order teaches you less.
  • The profit per extra sale of the same item barely changes, while the time to collect invoices and costs is large. Weigh tracking time against what it returns.
  • growth-phase procedure:
    1. Use the tracker for a while without entering every order, if you do not yet have a clear profit per product or order.
    2. Spot check periodically to confirm that a product line, brand or best-selling product is still profitable.
    3. Track a new supplier or new product for a while until you know how it performs.
    4. Return to the tracker after supplier price changes or your own price changes to see how things developed.
    5. If you are still not confident about profit per order, use the tracker for a week at a time instead of continuously.

Break-in ROAS and status

Break-in ROAS is the breakeven target: the return you need so a day does not lose money.

  • Break-in ROAS is the minimum daily return target. A day only counts as profitable when its ROAS is above that number.
  • The example target is 1.3. Each ad dollar must bring back more than $1.30 in revenue.
  • Enter your break-in ROAS in the Breakeven ROAS cell at the top of the Daily P&L Tracker tab.
  • It is an editable setting, so replace the example value 1.3 with your own target.
  • A day with ad spend but no revenue shows red, because a ROAS of zero is below the break-in target.
  • Cell colors signal status. Green means the day is above the target and winning. Red means the day is negative and losing. Zero means exact breakeven.
  • The tracker labels each day with one of three statuses: LOSING (red, negative profit), BREAKEVEN (zero) and WINNING (green, above target).
  • Read the color together with the PROFIT value to see the status.
  • Judge each day by profit and net margin, not by ROAS alone.
  • A ROAS of 3.0 can still leave a thin margin once COGS and fees are deducted.

Cell categories and formulas

  • The tracker uses three cell categories: input cells you fill in each day, AUTO-CALC cells (dark purple) that must not be typed into, and PROFIT/MARGIN cells that are calculated automatically and marked separately.
  • Only the input cells change when you enter daily data.
  • Dark purple cells are auto-calculated. Never type into them.
  • Overwriting formulas corrupts the calculations. Fill only the input cells.
  • Some auto-calculated cells pass a value through with an IF formula (a formula that checks a condition) of this form. They stay blank until the source input is filled:
=IF(source="","",source)
  • Do not overwrite these cells. Replacing the formula breaks the link to the input.
  • ROAS (RAS) = Revenue ÷ Ad Spend. Example: revenue $300 with ad spend $100 gives a ROAS of 3.0.
  • PROFIT = Revenue − COGS − Ad Spend − Fees.
  • Net Margin % = PROFIT ÷ Revenue. Example: profit $140 on revenue $300 gives a net margin of 46.67%.
  • Cost per metric = Ad Spend ÷ count of the event. Cost/ATC = Spend ÷ ATC, Cost/IC = Spend ÷ IC, Cost/Purchase = Spend ÷ Purchases.
  • Example with spend $100, 14 ATC, 10 IC and 6 purchases: $7.14, $10.00 and $16.67.
  • Worked day-1 example: spend $100, CPC $1.00, CTR 3%, CPM $60, 14 ATC, 10 IC, 6 purchases, revenue $300, COGS $60, ad spend $100.
  • Results: ROAS 3.0 (green, above the 1.3 target), profit $140, net margin 46.67%, cost per ATC $7.14, cost per IC $10.00, cost per purchase $16.67.
  • Cost per ATC, IC and purchase show where money is lost in the funnel (the path from ad click to purchase).
  • Compare them day over day. A cost per IC that rises while cost per ATC stays flat points to the checkout step, not the ad.

When per-order tracking stops paying off

2 min

Sources disagree on per-order tracking. Each position applies to a different stage and case.

Per-order tracking means checking the profit of each single sale.

  • Per-order tracking applies while the store (your online shop) is still learning which products make money. A simple spreadsheet (a basic table file) is enough while volume (the number of orders) is low, for example a few orders per day.
  • Per-order tracking applies during the early build phase. Calculate the profit of every single order, because margins (the share of each sale you keep as profit) only become clear once you include real supplier costs, shipping, processor fees (charges from payment companies) and fraud fees (costs of tools that check orders for fraud). Older source (2021-22).
  • Once the store is established, tracking each order matters less. Older source (2021-22).
  • Before you hand the task to someone else, enter and check the profit of at least the first 5 to 10 orders yourself. Older source (2021-22).
  • Per-order tracking has diminishing returns (each extra effort gives less gain) in the growth phase. The profit per extra sale of the same item barely changes, but the time to collect invoices (supplier bills) and costs is large. Older source (2021-22).
  • Per-order COGS accrual (writing down the product cost of each sale as it happens) is effectively impossible for dropshipping. COGS means cost of goods sold, the price you pay for the product. Dropshipping means the supplier ships each order to the customer, so you hold no stock. The return on investment (ROI, the profit you make compared to the money you put in) is negative. Older source (2021-22). [L484.009]
  • Per-order tracking for dropshipping is not recommended, because supplier invoices change. Older source (2021-22). [L484.022]
  • When volume grows, assign the per-order tracking to a virtual assistant (a remote helper), and review overall profit periodically. Older source (2020). [L268.041]
  • Automate profit tracking once the store runs (software does the calculation for you), so margin is measured per order rather than estimated from totals. [L532.023]
  • Per-sale tracking is the rule in the 2023 source: track the net profit of every sale in a spreadsheet. Net profit is what is left after all costs. [L970.149]
  • Adding a new supplier or product: track it for a while until you know how it performs. Older source (2021-22).
  • If you are still not confident about profit per order, use the tracker for a week at a time instead of continuously. Older source (2021-22).

Profit errors after a BOGO launch

1 min

BOGO means "buy one, get one" offer. Triple Whale is a tool that reads your store's numbers.

  • Triple Whale averages cost per acquisition (the cost to win one new customer) across all orders.
  • When many new customers buy two units under a BOGO, the real average cost per order falls.
  • Because of this, the averages shown in the tracker become inaccurate.
  • Account for this effect when reading profit data after a BOGO launch.

    8. List expenses (e.g. Shopify plan $29, apps, VA (virtual assistant), fixed costs) with status and payment frequency. Fixed costs are costs that repeat, whatever you sell.
    9. Set calculation preferences (e.g. exclude unfulfilled orders, optional).
    10. Choose a plan (start with free).

  • Entering the wrong cost of goods in BeProfit distorts profit figures (makes them wrong). Enter the actual total dropshipping price for every variant you sell, as quoted by the supplier.

  • In BeProfit, after entering only the $29 Shopify plan as an expense and no orders yet, the dashboard showed a loss of about $27. A negative profit before the first sale is therefore expected when only fixed costs are entered.

Top summary block: Start Budget 1000, Start 1000, End 1437, Monthly Result 437 (profit or loss for the month), Change in % 0.437 (format as a percentage, 43.7%).

  • Monthly Result = total income Is minus total expenses Is (1037 - 600 = 437). End = Start Budget plus Monthly Result (1000 + 437 = 1437). Change in % = Monthly Result divided by Start Budget (437 / 1000 = 0.437).
  • Difference is calculated differently for each side: on expense lines it is Planned minus Is (500 - 600 = -100), and on income lines it is Is minus Planned (1037 - 700 = 337). In both cases a negative value means the line is behind plan, so overspending on costs and underperforming on income both show as negative.
  • Each expense category is entered once per line, and the Total row sums all expense lines for Planned and Is separately. The same applies to all income lines, so the totals always match the sum of their categories.
  • Planned Monthly Result is planned income minus planned expenses, which in the example is 700 - 500 = 200. Comparing this planned result with the actual Monthly Result of 437 shows whether the month beat its plan.

  • Category names in the logs must match the category names in the monthly sheet exactly, so that totals pick them up.
  • Amounts entered with a currency symbol, such as "$250", are stored as text and are not counted in the totals. Enter amounts as plain numbers and keep the currency in the header or a note.

  • Simple finance tracking sheet for a dropshipping test (trying a product to see if it sells), in order:
    1. Enter every expense as it happens, daily if possible, otherwise at least once a week.
    2. Record Facebook ad spend, fulfillment costs (fulfillment means packing and shipping orders; put the platform name in the description) and cost of goods sold per entry.
    3. Record sales not as the gross Shopify sales figure but as the payout amount from Shopify Payments or Stripe. A payout is the money the payment company sends you, already net of transaction fees.
    4. Enter the planned test budget per product (for example 500) in the 'planned' field so the summary shows the difference.
    5. Enter the starting budget and read the monthly profit from the summary.
  • Track the payout from Shopify Payments or Stripe, not the gross sales figure, because the payout already has transaction fees deducted. Payment provider fees are typically around 2-3% per transaction, so a 40 dollar sale might yield roughly 2-3 dollars less.
  • Use Shopify Payments if it is available in your country, as it is the default payment processor (the company that handles card payments). Stripe is an alternative where Shopify Payments is not available. In some markets Stripe has been integrated into Shopify Payments. Whether Shopify Payments or Stripe is available depends on the seller's country.

Setting up B Profit in Shopify for profit tracking:

  1. Open the Shopify app store via the recommended apps section and install B Profit.
  2. Create an account and open the B Profit dashboard.
  3. Select the niche (for example electronics) and the sales channels, for example Facebook Ads, and connect the ad account.
  4. Add the inventory source: choose the dropshipping provider (CJ Dropshipping, AliExpress or Auto DS can be connected automatically to fetch cost prices), or select Other for manual entry.
  5. Select the fulfillment provider, or choose Other.
  6. Enter the shipping prices and product cost per item (example: cost per item $5, handling cost $0).
  7. Click Next and connect any external payment gateway such as Stripe or PayPal.
  8. Shopify plan fees are fetched automatically. Click Finish and start with the free plan to see gross profit and costs on the dashboard.
  • B Profit is a Shopify app that automatically tracks profit, KPIs (key performance indicators, the main numbers you watch) and profit margins. It shows whether the seller is at a loss or profitable. Automatic cost fetching is possible for CJ Dropshipping, AliExpress and Auto DS. It can be used for free with a start plan.
  • Track profit daily at first, then switch to weekly reviews to see the average weekly profit. Weekly data smooths out daily noise (random day-to-day ups and downs) and gives a steadier basis for scaling decisions.

  • Weekly profit = Revenue − Refunds − COGS − Ads − Payment processor fees (Stripe + PayPal + Klarna) − Shopify fee. Profit % = Profit ÷ Revenue. Sample week: 22670.38 − 0 − 6500.86 − 9765.33 − 846.3542 − 113.3519 = 5444.4839 USD, which is 24.02% of revenue.
  • Subtract refunds from revenue in the weekly profit calculation, do not ignore them. A week with zero refunds, as in the sample, overstates profit if returns are expected. Track refunds in the same period as the sales they relate to.
  • The column header says Shopify 1% Fee, but the sample value 113.35 equals 0.5% of revenue 22670.38, not 1%. Check the actual fee rate before relying on the column. A 1% fee on that revenue would be 226.70 USD.
  • A PayPal figure of 235.38 sits next to the profit column but is not part of the profit formula. Confirm whether PayPal fees are already included in the payment processor fee column, to avoid counting them twice or missing them.
  • The total cost of running the business is entered as 400 USD, but the listed monthly expenses add up to 606 USD (605 without the designer value of 1). The daily cost is derived from the entered total, so keep the expense list and the total in sync.
  • Currency Conversion (if ad account is not USD): enter in the field '1 USD equals (in your currency)' the amount of your currency that equals one US dollar. The default value is 1.0 when the account runs in USD.
  • In the sample week of July 22 to July 28, revenue was 22670.38 USD, with COGS of 6500.86 USD and ad spend of 9765.33 USD. Payment fees were 846.35 USD and the Shopify fee was 113.35 USD, leaving a profit of 5444.48 USD, or 24.02% of revenue.
