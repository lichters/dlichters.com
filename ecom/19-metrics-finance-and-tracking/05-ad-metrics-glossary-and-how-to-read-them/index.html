Ad metrics glossary and how to read them

Section 19.5 · 5 of 23 in this chapter · 32 min read

In short

  • Reach counts different people. Impressions count every time the ad is shown. Frequency is impressions ÷ reach. Above 2, the same people see the ad too often.
  • Judge ads by money, not clicks alone. Check ROAS (money back for each dollar spent) and cost per purchase before you spend more.
  • Hook rate shows whether the first seconds of a video stop people. For Facebook video ads, aim for 30% or more.
  • Compare against your own past numbers first. General benchmarks are only rough guides.
  • For Meta ads (Facebook and Instagram), aim for a click rate of 1.5% at least, and above 2% when you can.
More key points
  • For Meta ads, keep the cost per click under 1 USD.
  • Keep the cost per 1,000 views under 10 USD, and treat 30 USD or more as a warning.
  • Clicks alone prove nothing. Check sales and return on ad spend before you scale.
  • Before you compare a click rate with a benchmark, check which kind of click rate you are reading.

Delivery: impressions, reach, frequency and CPM

2 min
  • Reach is the number of different people who saw the ad. Impressions is the total number of times the ad appeared on screen. If one person sees it twice, that counts as two impressions.
  • Impressions count every display. This includes several shows to the same person across placements (the places an ad appears, such as Facebook and Instagram). Reach counts only unique Meta accounts (one account per person). So four impressions to one person equal one reach.
  • Frequency is impressions ÷ reach. It shows how many times the average person saw the ad. A value of 1.2 means each person saw the ad about 1.2 times on average.
    • Example: 2,017,463 ÷ 862,845 = 2.34.
  • Frequency checks:
    • A frequency above 2 means the same ad is shown too often and may tire people (they stop reacting). Watch it closely, especially with image ads that are cheap per click and served repeatedly.
    • In longer-running campaigns, frequency normally rises from about 2.5 to about 4. The expected range depends on how long the ad has been live.
    • Top-of-funnel frequency (for people who do not know you yet) is about 1.03 to 1.2. Low frequency does not rule out fatigue.
    • Reading the two figures together: treat 2 as the point to check the ad. In a campaign that has run for a while, a frequency of 2.5 to 4 is normal drift (slow change over time), so judge it against campaign age (how long it has run).
  • CPM (cost per thousand impressions) is what you pay for 1,000 ad impressions across all placements. It is your cost of advertising.
    • Formula: CPM = (ad spend ÷ impressions) × 1,000. Ad spend is the money you paid for the ads.
    • Example: $50 spent over 1,500 impressions gives a CPM of about $33.33.
    • Facebook formula: CPM = amount spent ÷ impressions × 1,000, the cost paid for 1,000 impressions across Meta placements (Meta is the company that runs Facebook and Instagram).
    • Cost of impressions = (impressions ÷ 1,000) × CPM. Example: 500,000 impressions at a $15 CPM cost $7,500. The figure of about $33,000 for the same case is an arithmetic error (a math mistake).
    • CPM means cost per mille (mille is Latin for thousand), the cost to show the ad to 1,000 people. It counts impressions, not clicks or engagement (likes, comments and shares). It appears as a column in Meta Business Manager (Meta's tool for managing ads).
    • CPM is set by the auction (the instant bidding contest for each ad slot). Better ad rankings can lower it, so the creative (the ad's images, video and text) is the main lever (the thing you can change to move the cost).

Clicks: CTR (click-through rate) and CPC (cost per click)

4 min

CTR (click-through rate)

  • Formula: CTR = clicks ÷ impressions × 100, as a percentage.
    • Examples: 5 clicks from 100 impressions = 5% CTR.
    • 100 link clicks (clicks on the link to your store) over 1,000 impressions = 10%.
    • 2 clicks from 100 viewers = 2%, meaning 2 people out of 100 impressions clicked through to the website.
    • 100 clicks from 1,000 impressions = 10%, and raising it to 12% is the optimisation goal (making the number better).
  • Google Search organic CTR: Impressions are how often the store appeared in organic search results (free results, not ads). Clicks are how often people then clicked through. Both are shown in Google Search Console > Performance (Google's free report tool).
  • Link clicks vs. all clicks: Use link clicks, not all clicks, when calculating Facebook CTR and CPC. Link clicks show people who went through to the store.
  • Ads Manager (Meta's ad control panel) can also show a CTR (all) figure that counts all clicks. Confirm you are reading the link-click version before comparing against the 3% to 7% benchmark.
  • Unique metrics count each person once. Repeated clicks by one person count as one unique click. Non-unique clicks count every click. Use unique CTR and unique clicks for analysis.
  • Facebook CTR variants:
    • CTR (all) measures attention, meaning how many people noticed the ad. Unique Outbound CTR measures interest, meaning how many different people clicked.
    • Outbound Unique CTR is the main CTR metric, since it counts unique clicks that send users to the website through the call-to-action (the button or line that asks people to act).
    • Use the unique link click-through rate, not the standard CTR, when judging Facebook ads.
  • Scope in Google Ads: CTR can be measured at account, campaign, ad group (a set of ads sharing keywords), ad, keyword (the search word that can trigger your ad), PLA (Product Listing Ads, shopping ads with product photo and price) or search term level, and across different time periods.
  • Data sufficiency: CTR analysis needs enough data. If an ad has run for one day with minimal impressions, extend the analysis period.
  • Variation: CTR varies by ad type and keyword position. Top-of-funnel keywords have different CTRs from bottom-of-funnel keywords (bottom-of-funnel means people ready to buy). Search ads have different CTRs from PLAs, display ads (banner ads on websites) or YouTube ads.
  • Cold traffic: Link clicks, CTR and CPC are top-of-funnel metrics, measured before the click. They show how cold prospects (people who have never seen the product) react to the ad.
  • Early test signal: A CTR of 2% to 3% held over 7 to 10 days shows the hook is working (the hook is the opening that grabs attention). It does not prove the offer converts (leads to sales).
  • Lead generation ads: Lead generation ads (ads that collect contact details) are expected to reach very high CTRs compared with competitor product ads on the same term, because the unusual angle makes them stand out.

CPC (cost per click)

  • Formula: CPC = ad spend ÷ link clicks.
    • Examples: $50 spent with 100 clicks = $0.50. $50 with 25 clicks = $2.00.
    • $8.92 for six clicks = about $1.49 per click.
    • $50 spent with 25 link clicks = $2.00, above the 1 USD target, meaning the CTR is too low and the ads need to change.
    • Average CPC = total ad spend ÷ total clicks. Example: $330 over 430 clicks = about $0.77.
  • Formula through CPM and CTR: CPC = CPM ÷ (1,000 × CTR as a fraction). A fraction turns a percentage into a decimal, so 1.5% becomes 0.015.
    • CPC = CPM ÷ (1,000 × CTR). Example: CPM of $20 and CTR of 1.5% gives about $1.33. A low CPM does not guarantee a low CPC when the CTR is low, so use this formula to find the source of a high CPC.
    • CPM of $10 and CTR of 2% gives 20 clicks per 1,000 impressions, so each click costs $0.50.
    • CPM of $30 and CTR of 2% gives 1,000 impressions, 20 clicks and a CPC of $1.50.
    • CPM of $10 and CTR of 1.5% gives a CPC of about $0.67. A CPC below $1 alone does not guarantee the 1.5% CTR target.
  • Worked check: At a CPM of $10, $50 of spend gives about 5,000 impressions. If 25 link clicks come from them, the CTR is 0.5%, below the 1.5% target, and the CPC is $2, above the 1 USD limit.
  • CPC is an outcome of CPM and CTR. A rising CPC can come from a higher CPM (costlier placements, meaning the ad slots cost more) or a lower CTR (a weaker ad). Find out which one drives it.
    • A high CPM means fewer impressions and fewer people reached. A low CTR makes the CPC high because few people click. Judge them together.
    • A high CTR with a high CPM can still produce a high CPC.
    • A high CPM can still produce a low CPC when CTR is strong.
  • CPC on its own: Treat a CPC above 1 USD alone as no clear sign, since sales and return on ad spend matter more.
  • Google Ads CPC: Quality Score work is not about the lowest CPC. Relevance raises Quality Score (Google's rating of how well your ads match searches), which can lower CPC. See the Google Ads section.

Video attention: hook rate, hold rate and retention

4 min

Definitions

  • Hook rate (3-second version) = 3-second video plays ÷ impressions × 100. A play is one start of a video. The hook rate is the share of impressions that watched past the first three seconds.
    • It measures how well the first 3 to 5 seconds stop the scroll (people swiping past) and grab attention.
    • Video attention rate is the same calculation: (3-second video plays ÷ impressions) × 100. It shows what share of impressions watched the first three seconds of a video ad.
    • Hook rate applies to video ads only, not to static images (still pictures).
  • Hold rate = ThruPlays ÷ impressions × 100. ThruPlays are plays of at least 15 seconds or plays that reached the end.
    • Through-plays (another name for ThruPlays) are views beyond 15 seconds, so the metric shows how well the video keeps viewers after the hook.
    • Use hook rate and hold rate together to judge the hook and the body (the middle part) of a video.
    • Conflicting definition: Hold rate is also described as the share of people who watch the video up to 50% of its length. Check which definition your report uses.
    • Hold rate is only meaningful with large traffic (many viewers). Longer videos naturally show lower hold rates than short ones, for example 15 seconds against one minute.
  • Hook and hold together: Hook rate measures how well the first seconds stop viewers. Hold rate measures how long they keep watching. Read them with CTR and CPM.
  • Main test metric: Hook rate is the main metric for hook performance in a creative test (a side-by-side test of different ads). Compare it across the hook variations (different openings) to find the winner. Then check hold rate for how long viewers keep watching.
  • Retention in steps (an analysis framework, a step-by-step way to read the data). Retention is the share of viewers still watching at each point:
    • Hook rate (25% version) = 25% plays ÷ impressions.
    • Retention 25 to 50% = 50% plays ÷ 25% plays.
    • Retention 50 to 75% = 75% plays ÷ 50% plays.
    • Retention 75 to 100% = 100% plays ÷ 75% plays.
    • Completion rate = 100% plays ÷ impressions.
    • Conflicting hook definition: The framework defines hook as 25% plays, while the other definitions above use the 3-second version. Name the definition you use.
  • Drop-off points: Video ad analytics show viewer completion at 75% watched (three-quarters through) and 95% watched (nearly finished). They also show where viewers most commonly drop off (leave). This shows which hooks and problem-solution formulas hold attention.
    • Drop-off can be analysed at 25%, 50% and 75% views to see exactly where viewers leave. This deeper analysis is only worth it when the account has a lot of traffic.

Sales and funnel: CVR (conversion rate), add-to-cart, CPA (cost per acquisition), ROAS (return on ad spend) and purchases

3 min

Conversion rates

  • Ad conversion rate (CVR) = purchases ÷ link clicks, as a percentage. This is the share of clicks that end in a sale.
    • For Facebook analysis, use purchases ÷ unique outbound clicks, shown as a percentage. Facebook tracks page views poorly, while outbound clicks reliably reach the website.
  • Store conversion rate = (purchases ÷ visitors) × 100. Example: 10 visitors with 1 purchase = 1%.
    • Conflicting definition: Ad CVR divides by clicks, and store conversion rate divides by visitors. Use the ad version when judging the ad and the store version when judging the site.
    • A website conversion rate of about 2% is treated as a good benchmark.
  • Store check: A strong CTR with a weak site conversion rate points to the landing page (the first page visitors see) or the offer, not the ad.

Add-to-cart and checkout

  • ATC% (add-to-cart rate, the share of clicks that add a product to the cart) = add to carts ÷ link clicks, as a percentage.
  • IC% (initiate checkout rate, the share of clicks that start checkout) = initiate checkout ÷ link clicks, as a percentage.
  • Read add-to-cart ratios in the Shopify backend (Shopify's admin area, the store's control panel):
Channel Add-to-cart ratio Notes
Facebook ads Minimum 5% Read in the Shopify backend
TikTok ads Minimum 3% Read in the Shopify backend
Google Ads Around 7% Higher because search is intent-based
  • Add-to-cart and sales checks: cheap clicks that produce no add-to-carts and no sales are a warning sign, not a success. Judge campaigns on downstream funnel metrics (results further along the buying path), not on click metrics alone.

Cost per acquisition (CPA)

  • Formula: CPA = total ad spend ÷ number of conversions (completed goals, such as sales). Example: $500 ÷ 18 conversions = $27.78.
  • CPA is also called CPP or cost per purchase. It is the ad cost of one sale: ad spend ÷ number of purchases.
  • Cost per result (purchase objective, meaning the campaign goal is sales) = amount spent ÷ website purchases.
  • Cost per purchase shows the spend per purchase.
  • CPA should not be your primary profitability metric (the main measure of whether you make money). ROAS is more important.

Return on ad spend (ROAS)

  • Formula: ROAS = revenue from ads ÷ ad spend. A spend of $1 returning $3 gives a ROAS of 3.
  • ROAS equals the revenue from an ad set (a group of ads sharing one budget and audience) divided by the ad spend over the same period. A ROAS of 4 to 5 means about $4 to $5 of revenue for every $1 spent on that ad set.
  • Purchase ROAS = purchase conversion value (the revenue from purchases) ÷ amount spent. A ROAS of 2 means $2 is returned for every $1 spent. A ROAS of 1.7 means $1.70 is returned for every $1 spent.
  • Decide on scaling (spending more on what works) only by comparing ROAS and cost per purchase against the breakeven (the point where you neither win nor lose money) and desired values, not by delivery numbers.

Purchases and revenue

  • Purchases shows the number of purchases. Cost per purchase shows the spend per purchase. Purchase conversion value shows the revenue from those purchases. Together they show whether the sales are worth the ad spend.
  • Platform revenue = website purchases × AOV (average order value, the average amount per order). Example: 306 × $129.20 = $39,535.20.
  • Content views fire when a visitor lands on the product page the ad links to. Cost per content view shows what each landed visitor costs.

Google Ads: Quality Score (Google's ad quality rating), Ad Rank (your auction position), impression share and YouTube

1 min

Ad Rank and Quality Score

  • Formula: Ad Rank = Quality Score × Bid Amount. A bid is the price you offer for each click. Your position in Google Ads auctions is determined by this product, not by bid alone. A high Quality Score on a smaller bid outperforms a low Quality Score on a large bid.
  • Ad relevance is the match between your copy (your ad words) and the search intent (what the person wants). It makes up a large part of Quality Score. When headlines and descriptions directly address the search query (the words typed into Google), Quality Score rises, impressions increase, cost per click decreases, and you can outrank competitors who bid significantly more.
  • The goal of optimising Quality Score is not the lowest possible CPC. It is the ability to bid more than competitors while staying profitable at the target ROAS. Advertisers who can afford higher bids dominate auctions in the niche (your market segment).
  • Lowering bids as far as possible is not the goal of paid traffic. Cheaper clicks should come from better ad quality, not from underbidding (bidding too low to win).

Search impression share

  • Search Impression Share is the percentage of relevant search queries your ads show for, given current bids and negative keyword lists (lists of words that block your ads from showing). It is the main measure of how competitive your bids are.

YouTube in-stream pricing

  • Example: about 3,500 views for about 4 cents each, which is about $0.0011 per view.
    • Note: these numbers do not match as written. 3,500 views at $0.0011 each comes to about $4 in total, so "4 cents each" is probably a typo for "$4 in total."

Decision thresholds: when a campaign is a winner

1 min
  • Clear winner: at least 3 sales with positive ROAS. One sale may be an exception.
  • Scaling signal: a creative (the ad content) used in 8 or more ads means Meta is scaling it, meaning spreading it wide.
  • Profitable sales before scaling an interest (an audience group targeted by what people like): 6 or more.
  • Expansion threshold to push a new ad to the next audience tier (the next wider audience group): 3% CTR or 5% engagement.
  • Data before judging: 3 to 5 days. Avoid judging on one day.
  • Chargeback rate (the share of payments customers reverse through their bank): about 1.5%. Above this, fines and processor problems (problems with the payment company) follow.

Reading the metrics together

2 min
  • Judge CTR, CPM and CPC together, and always check CPC, conversion rate and margin (the profit left per sale) before deciding.
  • Healthy baseline for Meta: CTR above 1.5%, CPC below 1 USD and CPM below 25 USD together. If all three hold but sales are missing, the work moves from the ads to the store.
  • Store problem: If the CTR is above roughly 3% to 5% and the ads still produce no results, the problem is almost always the store, not the ad.
  • Traffic is cheap, audience right: Benchmark link-click metrics over a few days included a CPM of $23.28, a cost per link click of $0.93, a CTR of 2.49% and 313 link clicks. Such top-of-funnel numbers signal that traffic is cheap and the audience is right.
  • Brand-specific targets: Top-of-funnel metrics such as CPM, CPC and CTR are common benchmarks. Cost per purchase and ROAS targets are unique to each brand, so compare each brand against its own margins.
  • Own history first: Compare against your own past numbers in your own ad account before judging against general figures.
  • Low CTR is not automatically a failure if the product converts well for its qualified buyers (people who fit your target customer). Look for improvements relative to your own past numbers.
  • Relevance and attention: A creative with low video attention but good CTR or sales still needs review. Low attention with sales may mean the traffic is relevant but small.
  • Hook and sales: A high CTR does not prove a good ad when no purchases follow. A high attention rate with irrelevant traffic gives misleading signals. Check whether audience and message are aligned, not only the percentage.
  • Platform comparison: Compare platforms by CPM and CTR together, not by CTR alone. Snapchat can deliver more clicks that do not necessarily bring quality traffic.
  • Post-test check: After the first test spend, check CPM, CTR and CPC. A CTR above about 3% is a strong signal, but it must be read together with conversion rate and store data.

Diagnosis sequence for video ads

1 min
  1. Check the video attention rate (3-second plays divided by impressions) first, to judge whether the hook works.
  2. Check the click-through rate, to judge whether the middle of the video and the offer hold attention.
  3. Check add-to-cart and purchase data, to judge whether the page and sale convert.
  4. Use the combined picture of these metrics to decide what to improve.

Hook benchmarks are rules of thumb, applied mainly when sales are weak.

Build a new creative from hook and hold data

1 min
  1. Add the Hook Rate and Hold Rate columns to the dashboard (your report screen).
  2. Find the video with a strong hook rate and the video with a strong hold rate.
  3. Take the first three to five seconds from the strong-hook video.
  4. Take the rest of the video from the strong-hold video.
  5. Combine them into one new video and test it as a new creative.

1 min

  • It usually reaches fewer.
  • Target: under $10, ideally near $5.

  • A healthy setup has a CPM below 25 USD.

  • A competitive audience means many advertisers want the same people.

  • A lower CPM means cheaper reach.

Reconciling the CPM figures:

  • The sources range from about $10 (average) to $70 (poor).
  • Under $25 and under $30 are the healthy limits.
  • The sources give about $50 as decent.
  • The Source D 2026 figures are its own reading of current competition.
  • The Source C limit (under $25) and the T4/T5 rule (under $30) are the stricter checks.
  • Apply the one that matches your market and period, and state which one you used.

Benchmarks: TikTok

1 min

CBO means campaign budget optimization. The platform shares the budget across ad groups by itself. An ad group is TikTok's group of ads with its own settings.

  • CPC: target below $1 per click.
  • For a US audience, a CPC of about $0.50 to $0.62 is good.
  • Any CBO campaign or ad group with a CPC above $1 should be cut.
  • CPM: target below $10 per 1,000 impressions.
  • TikTok ads have a much lower CPM than Facebook.
  • The main difference between the platforms is the CPM, not the ideal CTR.

CTR, with conflicting readings:

  • Target: above 1.5%, meaning the share of viewers who click the ad.
  • In TikTok product tests, a CTR above 1% is strong.
  • A like rate of 1% or more signals engagement. Engagement means how much people interact with the ad.
  • A CTR around 0.8% can be acceptable on TikTok if traffic, conversion rate and profit are good. Traffic means visits to the store. Conversion rate means the share of visitors who buy.
  • Apply the 1.5% target as the default.
  • Use the 1% and 0.8% readings only when traffic, conversion and profit support them.

Formula:

  • CTR is clicks per impression.

  • CPC = CPM ÷ (1,000 × CTR)

  • CTR = CPM ÷ (1,000 × CPC)

  • Example: at a CPM of $10 and a CTR of 1.5%, the CPC is about $0.67.

  • A CPC below $1 alone does not guarantee the 1.5% CTR target.

  • Add-to-cart ratio: minimum 3%, read in the Shopify backend. The add-to-cart ratio is the share of visitors who add an item to their cart. Shopify is the store software; its backend is the admin area.

  • TikTok ads usually cost more per impression than Pinterest for similar targeting. Targeting means choosing who sees the ad.

Setting up custom metrics in the ad platforms

2 min

A custom metric is a number you build yourself from two other numbers in the ad tool.

Hook Rate and Hold Rate in Ads Manager (procedure):

  • Open the column or custom metric option, and choose create custom metric.
  • Name the first one "Hook Rate", and set the format to percentage.
  • Use 3-second video plays divided by impressions.
  • Name the second one "Hold Rate", and set it to percentage.
  • Use ThruPlays divided by impressions. A ThruPlay is a view where the viewer watched the whole video or at least 15 seconds.
  • Add both columns to the dashboard, to compare creatives side by side. A dashboard is your summary screen. Creatives are the ads themselves.

Funnel analysis metrics:

  • In Facebook Ads Manager, go to Columns > Customize columns > Custom metrics.
  • Create the custom metrics used for funnel analysis.
  • Set the Hook rate metric as a percentage of impressions where viewers watched the first three seconds.

Hook rate definition as a custom metric:

  • Hook rate: people who watch the first 3 seconds of a video, divided by all impressions, as a percentage.

Video attention metric:

  • Video attention is a custom metric: 3-second video plays divided by impressions, as a percentage.
  • In Meta Ads Manager, create it via Customize columns > Create custom metric.
  • Name it "Video attention", and set the format to percentage.
  • Enter the 3-second video plays field divided by the impressions field.

Video attention, step by step:

  • Open Columns > Customize columns.

  • Click Create custom metric, and name it "Video attention". Set it as a percentage.

  • Use 3-second video plays divided by impressions.

  • Save it, and add it next to click-through rate in the reporting view.

  • Frequency is found in the Facebook Ads columns. Frequency is the average number of times each person saw the ad.

  • Video attention benchmark for the first three seconds: about 25% is acceptable.

  • 30% and above is good.

  • 40% and above is excellent.

  • A value around 20% is considered low, and the hook should be improved. The hook is the opening seconds of a video.

Checking numbers by hand

1 min

Ad set metrics can be checked from the raw totals. Raw totals are the plain numbers from the report.

  • Frequency = impressions ÷ reach. Reach is the number of different people who saw the ad. Example: 2,017,463 ÷ 862,845 = 2.34.
  • CPM = amount spent ÷ impressions × 1,000. Example: 10,832.32 ÷ 2,017,463 × 1,000 = $5.37.
  • CPC = amount spent ÷ link clicks. Link clicks are clicks on the ad's link. Example: 10,832.32 ÷ 27,761 = $0.39.
  • CTR = link clicks ÷ impressions. Example: 27,761 ÷ 2,017,463 = 1.38%.
  • Cost per result (purchase objective) = amount spent ÷ website purchases. A result is the goal the campaign is set to get. A purchase objective means the goal is sales. Example: 10,832.32 ÷ 306 = $35.40.
  • Platform revenue = website purchases × AOV. AOV means average order value, the average amount per order. Example: 306 × $129.20 = $39,535.20.

If you estimate impressions from spend, use:

  • Impressions ≈ spend ÷ CPM × 1,000.
  • Example: at a CPM of 10 USD, 50 USD of spend gives about 5,000 impressions.
  • 25 link clicks from those impressions is a CTR of 0.5%. That is below the 1.5% target.
  • The same spend gives a CPC of 2 USD. That is above the 1 USD limit.

Common mistakes

1 min
  • Spending on Facebook ads without knowing what CTR and CPM mean makes results impossible to read.
  • It also wastes budget.
  • Learn these metrics before testing products.
  • Meta Ads Manager metrics such as CTR, CPC and CPM describe ad delivery and click efficiency. They do not describe profitability.
  • Decide on scaling only by comparing ROAS and cost per purchase against the breakeven and desired values. Scaling means raising the budget. Breakeven is the point where you neither earn nor lose money.
  • A high CTR does not prove a good ad when no purchases follow.
  • Judging hook benchmarks from two different sets, without naming which one you used, is a mistake.
  • Pick one set, state it, and apply it consistently.
  • Reading the CTR (all) figure as link CTR when comparing against benchmarks is a mistake. CTR (all) counts every click, not only link clicks.
  • Confirm the variant before you compare.
  • Treating a low CPM as proof of a low CPC is a mistake.
  • CPC also depends on CTR.
  • Chasing the lowest possible bid, rather than better ad quality, is a mistake. A bid is the most you offer to pay for a view or click.
  • Do not use an arithmetic error from a source as a benchmark.
  • In one example, the figure of about $33,000 for 500,000 impressions at a $15 CPM is wrong.
  • The correct cost is $7,500.

More rules worth knowing

1 min
  • Click-through rate = clicks ÷ impressions.
  • An impression is counted each time the ad is shown.
  • A low result signals that the ad, image or price is not attracting clicks.
  • In the book niche, sustainable CPC and CPM sit around $0.80 to about $2. A niche is a narrow market, and sustainable means costs you can keep paying.
  • Costs in this range keep early tests affordable. Early tests are small first trials.
