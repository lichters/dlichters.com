Test budgets, kill rules and scale decisions

Section 19.11 · 11 of 23 in this chapter · 49 min read

In short

  • Check every place your product sells before you stop it.
  • Stop a product only when its numbers stay weak after you try fixes.
  • Spend more only after several days of steady profit, not one good day.
  • Change one thing at a time, so you know what worked.
  • Judge ads by money, not clicks. Check profit first. Clicks and views only help you find the problem.
More key points
  • Give each test enough time and money before you decide: usually 3 days or more, and about one week for a batch of ads. Do not stop on one bad day.
  • Stop at clear limits. If a product has spent about 300 USD on ads and is not at break-even (the point where sales just cover costs), and its add-to-cart rate is poor, stop it.
  • Raise an ad's budget only when its return on ad spend (ROAS: sales money earned per dollar spent on ads) is at least 1 above break-even ROAS.

Starting budgets and first spend

2 min
  • start with $10 to $30 USD per day across all campaigns. The goal is to reach return on ad spend (ROAS) within two to three days.
  • a starting daily ad budget of about $10 to $30 per day is typical for new stores. Promotional ad-credit vouchers can cover roughly one week of advertising at no cost.
  • a few hundred dollars is enough as a starting ad budget for the first months, provided ad spend is monitored closely and optimized. Profits should then be reinvested into ads.
  • leaving campaigns running without checking them can drain money quickly. For example, a $100 per day budget left unmonitored for a month would leak a large amount of money.
  • For a new store, $1,000 in ad spend covers about 30 days of paid traffic. Free traffic methods can reduce how much must be invested in ads each month.
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): a daily budget of about $30 equals roughly $1,000 per month and is a reasonable entry level. Other examples mentioned are $50, $100 and $1,000 per day, depending on what the advertiser can comfortably afford.
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): only put a daily ad budget into a shared budget that you could lose completely in a single day without financial stress. If you cannot afford to lose a given amount daily, do not set it as the budget.
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): a daily budget set very low, for example $2 per day, produces too little data to learn anything. Sales may come only once every three or four months. The budget must be realistic enough to generate meaningful results.
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): ad spend aims at long-term profitability, not profit every day. Days with zero return or a full loss on ad spend will happen, so judge performance over time rather than daily.
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): beginners without advertising experience often spend money on ads that bring no return during trial and error. Plan a test budget for this learning period and track returns from the start so losses are limited.
  • Treat money spent on ads and tests as a purchase of data. Every dollar spent should produce a lesson that brings the business closer to profitability.

Lead generation and warm audience budgets

1 min
  • Daily lead budget = target leads per day × maximum cost per lead. Example: 10 leads per day at a $4 maximum gives about $40 per day.
  • Start each lead generation campaign at $5 to $10 per day. The customer goodwill campaign can run on $5 per day because it only needs to show ads to a few people.
  • As a starting point, the three warm audiences together need about $15 to $20 per day. The promotions budget depends on the size of the audience.
  • When entering a promotion budget in Meta, the screen shows the audience size and the estimated daily reach. Try different budget amounts to see how reach changes before launching.
  • Budget needs grow with traffic. Retargeting a large audience, such as 100,000 site visitors, costs far more to reach than a 1,000-visitor audience, so size budgets to the audience.
  • Ad fatigue in cold lead generation took roughly half a year to over a year to appear. Earlier, ads had to be swapped out about every week.

Product test procedure (budget and kill rule)

  1. Spend about $25 to $30 per day across 5 to 6 ad sets at $5 each.
  2. Let the ads run for the first three days without interfering.
  3. Gather data until about $100 is spent and check that CPM is below $25, CTR is above 1.5% (ideally 2%), and CPC is below $1.
  4. If these numbers are met, check the store's conversion rate, which should be at least 1.5%.
  5. If conversion is too low, improve the offer and store for two to three days.
  6. If there are still no sales after $300 spent, switch the ads off and move to the next product.

Price-based spend threshold (product page test)

  • Use this procedure when a product reaches its first spend threshold, about 10% of price, with no sale.
    1. Do not switch the ads off yet.
    2. Check the search terms for irrelevant traffic and add those keywords as negative keywords.
    3. Open the product pages of your future competitors for the exact same product and compare them with yours.
    4. Change your page to match or beat what the competitors do better, which is where the conversion rate can improve.
    5. Keep spending while you optimize, and stop only at about three times the first threshold if there is still no sale.
  • Example: for a $1,500 product, the first spend threshold is about $150, meaning 10% of price. If $150 is spent without a sale, keep the ads running and start optimizing. Stop at about $450 (three times the threshold, roughly 30% of price) if there is still no sale.

Ad and ad set budgets by platform

2 min

Meta (Facebook)

  • Test ad sets with a daily budget of $5 each. The presenter starts 4 to 6 ad sets, so the test spends about $20 to $30 per day in total.
  • Check ad performance after about four days of spend (about $20 per ad set), starting with CTR, then CPM, then sales.
  • Ad testing budget: start with about 5 USD per ad set (or 5.10 USD). Run five ad sets at 5 USD per day for about four days, then analyze. Alternatively, 20 USD per ad set for two days.
  • Ad budgets for a single ad start at $10 to $50 per day. The ad is judged when it has spent three times the desired cost per acquisition (CPA). Example: for a product bought at $15 and selling at $45 with a target CPA of $15, the ad runs with $45 per day until it is switched off or scaled.
  • Budget rule for a single Facebook ad: daily budget = 3 × target CPA. Example: CPA $15 gives a daily budget of $45 per ad. Target ROAS example: product cost $15, selling price $45, CPA $15 = 3:1 target ROAS.
  • Limit a Meta daily budget correctly. The daily budget shows three limits: average daily spend equals the daily budget, maximum daily spend equals daily budget × 1.75, and maximum weekly spend equals daily budget × 7. Example with a $25 daily budget: average $25.00 per day, maximum daily $43.75, maximum weekly $175.00.
  • Set the spend limit for an ad set at the product's cost of goods, so the first test measures whether the product covers its cost. Return on ad spend (ROAS) is the revenue from the ad divided by the amount spent on it.

TikTok

  • Starting daily ad spend for a new product test: break-even cost multiplied by 10, divided by 4, which equals the daily ad spend for one ad set with no targeting.

Pinterest

  • Pinterest test budget: start with a daily budget of exactly $5 per campaign, with $10 as the maximum to start with. A single product can be tested for under $5 per day, and multiple products can run in parallel at $5 each. Test three to five products if the budget allows.

Google and YouTube

  • A YouTube ads test needs a budget of at least $1,000 to $2,000. Expect to wait two to three weeks before judging results, and a single month of testing often only improves creatives.

Test cost before the first-sale gate

  • Test cost before the first-sale gate equals the daily budget times the number of test days. At $5 per day for six days, the spend is $30, so a product without a sale by then is dropped.

Kill rules by platform

6 min

Meta (Facebook)

  • Use CTR as the first gate: a click-through rate of about 1.5% or higher suggests real interest in a product, and around 2% is a stronger sign. Give a product a longer test when the rate sits around 1.5%, and stop it when the rate is well below that.
  • Primary metric for a new store is CTR: aim for at least 1.5%. The presenter sees 2 to 5% as a sign that the ads are working.
  • A CTR above 1.5% is considered good for Facebook ads. Creatives at around 1.3 to 1.5% CTR can still be scaled if the ROAS is 2 or higher.
  • Day-one ad-level optimization:
    1. After day one, review only the ads inside each ad set, not the ad sets.
    2. Find ads that have spent at least 8 USD (normally 8 to 10 USD).
    3. Mark an ad as poor if its CPC is above 3 USD or one USD above the average CPC of the account.
    4. Turn off ads that have spent that amount with a poor CPC and no sales, so Meta moves budget to the other ads.
    5. Keep ads with a good CPC running even if their CTR is lower.
  • CPC and profitable spend matter more than CTR. A low CTR is acceptable when CPC is cheap, while a high CPC means the budget goes to the wrong ad, and CTR and creatives should be improved.
  • Do not kill an ad set before it has spent its break-even cost per purchase. At a 5 USD daily budget and a 20 USD break-even, wait 4 days (20 ÷ 5) before judging it.
  • Use the break-even cost per purchase as the maximum spend an ad set should reach without a sale before it counts as unprofitable. Without a sale after spending the break-even amount, the ad set is unlikely to become profitable in the long run.
  • Decision after an ad set reaches its break-even cost per purchase:
    1. If there was no sale and the CPC and CTR are poor, turn the ad set off.
    2. If there was no sale but the CTR is above average and the CPC is low, give it one more day, then turn it off if it still has no sale.
    3. If there is at least one sale, let it spend another break-even cost per purchase to check whether the sales are consistent.
    4. Two sales by day two can mark a potential winner.
    5. Repeat the same check after each break-even amount until the audience reaches four or more sales with a profitable ROAS, then it is ready to scale.
  • Meta campaign spending around 150 EUR over three days across several ad sets with only one add-to-cart: stop the campaign and investigate rather than leaving it running. Such a result points to a broken setup, tracking or a fundamentally weak offer.
  • The purpose of stopping a losing test early is to cut losses before money is spent on a product that does not work.
  • A product with 350 USD spent, a ROAS of 0.42, a link CTR under 1% and a conversion rate under 1% was judged as not worth more spend. Investing the energy in the next product was the recommended choice.
  • Do not judge a test ad by CTR alone, because a high CTR can still be unprofitable. Compare the spend per ad set with the break-even cost per purchase and the resulting sales and ROAS.
  • Facebook ads should be judged after the test has spent enough to give data. Without enough spend the data is not reliable, so one can only rely on signals such as add-to-carts for early judgment.
  • Cut losing ads early using soft metrics: cost per ATC, CTR, CPC, CPM and engagement rate ranking. Keep an ad only if these metrics improve day over day, otherwise cut it.

TikTok

  • Product kill thresholds for a TikTok test:
    • If no video passes 10,000 views in the first week after consistent uploading, the product is probably dead.
    • If you cannot reach around 500 followers or a few thousand views in the first week, kill it.
    • If you cannot reach 1,000 followers within two weeks, or if revenue per million views is under 100 USD, drop the product.
    • Kill early to save time.
  • Revenue per million views after the second week:
    • Keep products that reach at least about 1,000 USD per million views on average.
    • At 500 USD per million, it is still worth it.
    • A product with 10 million views and 5,000 USD in sales (50% profit) is still considered good.
  • Strong product test performance (example): CPM $4 to $6 on Facebook ($10 in Germany), under $5 on TikTok; CTR 1 to 2% on Facebook, under 1% on TikTok; add-to-cart ratio 13 to 18%; 4 sales total (1 from Facebook under $100 spend, 3 from TikTok $120 spend). These top-tier metrics indicate creative resonance and audience fit before scaling.

Google and YouTube

  • If a Google Ads campaign spends budget early but produces no sales, delete it and create a fresh campaign rather than making multiple bid or audience adjustments. The algorithm struggles to recover from poor learning phase performance, so restarting from scratch allows more effective optimization.
  • Pause a product or ad group when it has accumulated clicks equal to about twice the expected clicks per conversion without a conversion, provided there is enough data (roughly 10 to 15 conversions per month in the campaign). Example: with 38 expected clicks per conversion, pause at about 70 to 76 clicks without a conversion.
  • Judging a campaign after only three sales over three months and concluding the ads are not working is a mistake. Three sales with a cost per sale that is too high relative to product prices is still usable data, and it points to product selection rather than a lack of data.
  • procedure when ads are unprofitable:
    1. If ads are unprofitable (ROAS is too low), first try cutting negative keywords and removing underperforming products.
    2. Monitor for two weeks.
    3. If ROAS still does not improve, consider pausing ads or significantly cutting budget.
    4. Do not pause ads if ROAS is stable just because traffic is low.

Checks before pausing or cutting

2 min
  • Check all channels before you pause a product. A channel is a place where you sell or advertise, such as Google, Meta (Facebook and Instagram) or email. Also check every touchpoint, which is each step a customer sees on the way to buying. Look at Google Analytics (a free tool that counts visitors), Shopify transaction data (the sales record of your store) and email conversion sequences (automatic email series that lead to a sale). Confirm the product really is weak and is not making sales through other paths.
  • Do not pause a weak variant without checking the others. A variant is one version of a product, such as one color or size. A weak color can pull down the total numbers while other variants of the same SKU (stock code, one product code) sell well.
  • Change one thing at a time when results drop. Changing creatives (the ads themselves), pages and audiences together makes the cause hard to find.
  • A creative that worked in the first days but dropped suddenly after a new asset was added: cut the newest creative for a few days. Then see whether the old ads recover.
  • Optimize (improve) in this order: first the creatives, then the account structure (how your campaigns are organized), then the store. Stop the product only after these steps have been tested within the test budget (the money you set aside for testing).
  • Before you blame the store, check the funnel (the path from ad to sale). If the product does not reach its breakeven KPIs (breakeven is the point where sales cover all costs; KPIs are the key numbers you track), treat that as a signal to check the funnel. If no fix works, consider killing the product, which means stopping it for good.
  • Evaluate ads every two weeks rather than switching them off early. Stop a campaign only after months of optimization fail. In the meantime, fix negative keywords (search words that stop your ad from showing), bids (how much you pay per click or view) and budgets.

Product-level kill thresholds

2 min
  • Set a kill threshold for each product. This is the number at which you stop spending. Stop spending when conversion (the share of visitors who buy) stays below 1% after optimization. A conversion rate under 1% is a fatal sign. Keep test budgets to about 200 to 250 USD before you decide.
  • Seriously consider dropping a product if its CTR (click-through rate, the share of people who see the ad and click it) falls below 1.5%, sales are very hard to get, and it is still not profitable after about $10k in sales. Before that point, improve the offer (what you promise the buyer) and the store first.
  • A product with a barely profitable CPA (cost per acquisition, the cost to get one sale) should be tested with new interests (audience groups based on hobbies or topics) and a changed offer before you drop it. Keep testing until the margin (the money you keep from each sale after costs) is positive.
  • Accept break-even in the early stage if the goal is proof of concept (proving the idea works) and brand positioning (how people see your brand), not immediate profit. Judge the product over two to three months or a full year.
  • Decide whether to continue a product by its numbers, not by attachment. Continue if the numbers are good. Move on if they are not.
  • Falling in love with a product and spending too much on it causes losses. Ask what the data says instead of why the product is not working.
  • A product that only just breaks even on ROAS (return on ad spend, the money earned back for each dollar spent) and does not reach the break-even point will eventually die out. Do not scale it. Scale only after you find a clear winner.
  • Ad-level example: one product was tested with two Facebook interests. The interest around personal-care buyers reached about 40% attention rate (the share of people who watch the start of the ad). But it produced no add-to-carts (ATC, when a buyer puts the item in their cart) and no sales. The home and garden interest had a poor attention rate but generated the sales. So the decision was based on sales, not attention.

Scale-or-hold decisions

1 min

Scale means spending more on something that works. Hold means keeping the spend flat.

Proof required before scaling

3 min
  • Before scaling an ad set (a group of ads with one audience and one budget), wait until it has about six or more sales. Several ad sets with six or more sales are a stronger signal than one ad set with a single high-performing result.
  • Scale an interest only after it has produced about six or more profitable sales. Below that, the pixel (tracking code on your store that records sales) does not yet have enough purchase data to optimize.
  • A winning audience means two to three interests that each produce at least three purchases at a ROAS above break-even within about 3 to 5 days of data. Test ads with winning creatives together with winning interests. A winning asset is the combination of an interest and a creative.
  • Four or more sales on one audience, with a profitable ROAS, is the consistency signal to scale that audience. Three to four sales is an early sign. It needs more spend to confirm.
  • A product is ready to scale once a winning interest reaches more than six sales. Start scaling only after the sales signal appears, not after a few clicks.
  • A product has proof of concept when it earns about $100 profit per day consistently for three days. Check this on the profit and loss sheet (a report of money in and money out). Consistency is the signal to act on, not a single good day.
  • Do not scale the ad budget while the metrics are inconsistent or swing strongly. Scale only when the metrics hold steady. A lack of stable results means no break and no scale.
  • Judge ads by hard metrics first: sales, profit and add-to-carts. Hard metrics are numbers tied to money. Soft metrics (early signs such as clicks and views) matter only if an ad is not making money.
  • Scale only the ads that are profitable. Do not overspend on unprofitable ads.
  • Check margin and ROAS before any ad budget is increased. Spending on ads that do not produce profit drains the budget quickly.
  • Ad spend is a real cost. Before you scale any ads, check that the spend is justified by the numbers. Spending without reading the metrics properly burns money with no clear return.
  • For a clear winning product, track only ROAS and sales. Ignore CTR, CPC (cost per click) and CPM (cost per 1,000 views). Turn off or cut the budget for any ad set that stops performing.
  • Make daily optimization decisions on purchase-related metrics: cost per purchase, total spend, purchase value and ROAS. CTR, CPC, CPM and conversion rate alone should not drive major decisions.
  • In the first testing days, do not judge the whole campaign by profitability. Product, interest, creative and website are all still variables, which means they can still change. ROAS per ad set on daily data is the main decision metric during testing.
  • A low CTR combined with a high ROAS shows there is room to improve the creative. But ROAS still decides whether the ad set stays. Profit comes from ROAS, not from CTR alone. Example: CTR 0.92% with ROAS 3.21.
  • Break-even cost per purchase is the reference for whether a campaign is working. A test campaign that spends about one to one-and-a-half times the break-even without a sale should be stopped.

  • Scale aggressively only when the measured ROAS is above the profit-target ROAS. If it is below, keep the ad spend flat and test new creatives or angles (new images, videos or hooks).
  • Use the desired ROAS to decide on scaling. If ROAS is below the desired level, do not scale aggressively. Keep the spend level and test changes.
  • Accept the economics, not the headline ROAS. A product with 4 to 6:1 ROAS at $50 per day was the benchmark. The outcome depends on the spend level.
  • Break-even ROAS and margin check: £400 spend at 2.5× ROAS equals £1,000 revenue. Cost of goods (COGS, what the product costs you to buy) at 33% of revenue is £330, so profit is £670. The same £400 spend at 1.6× ROAS equals £1,280 revenue. Cost of goods is £422, so profit is £858. Even though 1.6× is lower ROAS, it nets more profit (it leaves more money after costs), so keep budget at £800 (double the original) rather than spending at 2.5×. (Note: 400 × 1.6 = 640. The £1,280 figure matches an £800 spend, not £400.)
  • A campaign with a few large orders can show a very high return in one month and a much lower one the next. One month of results is not a reliable basis for decisions.
  • ROAS typically falls as ad spend grows. Scaling should be preceded by improving the return at the current spend level.
  • Average ROAS and conversion: measure against the first-month baseline (your starting point). Adjust the budget only when return on ad spend is well above target.
  • Increase the ad budget only while return on ad spend stays at 10× or above. Optimize the campaigns before each increase.
  • Target ROAS for ad scaling is 10×. Only increase ad spend when ROAS consistently reaches or exceeds this threshold.
  • Seasonality (sales that change with the time of year) changes both demand and ROAS. For a worked example, see the seasonality section below.

Budget ramps

3 min

A budget ramp means raising spend in steps.

Doubling and step ramps

  • 4-Hour Budget Doubling Method:
    1. Start one ad with a £200 budget.
    2. After 4 hours, calculate ROAS from sales in that 4-hour period only. Ignore earlier sales.
    3. If ROAS is profitable (above break-even), double the budget to £400.
    4. After 4 more hours, calculate ROAS for the new 4-hour period.
    5. If it is profitable, double again.
    6. Repeat until the budget reaches the target or ROAS drops below the threshold.
    7. Example progression: £200 → £400 → £800 → £1,600 → £3,200 → £4,000.
  • Black Friday 2023 scaling (Black Friday is the big shopping day after Thanksgiving): the budget started at £200 and scaled over the day to about £4,000. Final weekend (Friday to Sunday) ad spend was £13,491. Total weekly ad spend was £33,661. Revenue was £76,000.
  • Pinterest scaling rule: once a campaign is profitable, double the daily budget (e.g. $5 to $10). Require consistent profit for two days, for example ROAS above 2.5 while breakeven ROAS is 1.5 to 1.6. Increase the budget at the start of the day, around 12:00 midnight UTC (world clock time) or up to 4 to 5 a.m. UTC, not in the evening. This avoids a sudden spend spike.
  • Step-by-step ramp: a first-month test with a $1,000 ad budget produced $20,000 in sales, a 20× return. After optimizing, the budget was raised step by step. At the next step, sales doubled while the return stayed above 10×. A later large increase of tens of thousands of dollars dropped the return well below 10×. This shows why big jumps are risky.
  • Avoid abrupt launches and large budget changes. Performance swings on the first day and usually stabilises around day three or four as the account learns the audience (the ad platform learns who to show the ad to).
  • Example with a budget of at least 20 USD per day in a dedicated structure (a campaign set up only for this product): Meta ad sets were fragmenting (splitting into too many small pieces) after a sales milestone. A week of weak results followed. Raising the daily budget from 5 to 7 for three to four days did not help. Combining ad sets at about 20 per day still gave a good CTR but no sales. Earlier, the store had at least 200 sales a day. Afterward it dropped to zero or one sale a day, which led to a funnel and offer review.
  • One student reached $1,300 in sales in a single day at a profit margin of about 40%. This shows that a product with healthy margins can produce strong daily sales. Scaling should begin only after the numbers are checked.

Horizontal and advanced scaling (Pinterest)

  • Horizontal scaling means reaching new, similar audiences instead of spending more on one. On Pinterest, after a winning interest is found in the Targeting breakdown (the report that shows results per interest), create new campaigns that target the winning sub-interests (narrower topics inside the winner). Or add 50 to 80 keywords with audience sizes of 10 to 30 million. Then judge each new campaign by its profitability.
  • A more advanced option on Pinterest is CPA-based targeting. This sets a cost-per-acquisition cap, for example sales under $20. Use it after about 10 to 15 days of consistent results. It is similar to the bid cap on Facebook (a maximum you will pay for each result).

Bottom-of-funnel and campaign-level ramps

Bottom-of-funnel (BOF) means ads aimed at people close to buying.

  • If you are not profitable at $50 per day on a bottom-of-funnel campaign, the ad concept or product validation (proof the product sells) is likely the problem, not the budget level. Lowering the spend further (to $30 or $20) does not solve this. Test new ad concepts or new products, or stop and focus elsewhere.
  • If you have a video campaign spending $300 per day with a given ROAS, run bottom-of-funnel at roughly $100 per day ($300 × 30%). Monitor ROAS. Scale bottom-of-funnel only if it stays above your comfort threshold (for example, 2.5× if breakeven is 1.5×). This prevents overspending on a limited warm pool (a small group of people who already know your brand).
  • Keep ROAS as the primary success metric. If costs drop 20% but lead volume increases 20%, and ROAS stays the same, you are succeeding even if absolute profit has not changed.
  • Track the daily pixel purchase volume. That number decides when ABO (budget set on each ad set, not on the whole campaign) becomes an option.

Audience, market and ad-type comparisons

2 min
  • Savings from a cheaper audience = (precise-interest cost per conversion − lookalike cost per conversion) ÷ precise-interest cost per conversion. (Precise interest means targeting specific hobbies. A lookalike audience is people who resemble your buyers.) The reported $0.93 versus $1.20 works out to about 22.5% lower, not 30%. So calculate the saving from your own numbers, and do not trust rounded claims. The comparison came from only two days of data, so repeat the split test (testing two versions against each other) before relying on it.
  • Compare markets by cost per lead (the cost to get one contact, such as an email sign-up) and by the first profitable sales. The market with the lowest cost per lead that also produces profitable sales is the one to scale.
  • A store about 30 days into its ads had around $2,500 in sales and was breaking even (earning back its costs, with no profit yet). This is typical for the early testing phase.
  • A German market with a CPC of about €0.50 and a conversion rate of 2.5 to 3% was judged a clear candidate to scale, because the margin should allow a decent profit. A low CPC with a healthy conversion rate gives room to increase spend.
  • For a Facebook ad to be scaled, check whether its ROAS, CPA and attention rate are good at a small spend. Most ads that are good at $10 per day do not scale easily. They need a higher budget structure to hold.
  • In a review of hundreds of ad accounts over roughly 6 to 8 years, each ad was classed as a workhorse (a reliable ad that keeps working) or a warhorse (the other class). Revenue was attributed to each category with the help of AI. About 80% to 81% of ad revenue (81% by exact figure) came from workhorse-type ads, not warhorses. The result suggests that low-effort, relevant ads drive most sales.
  • How closely you review performance and how aggressively you bid depend on how much risk you accept. Aggressive operators push bids (raise the price they pay for each click or view) and fund more testing. Restrained operators keep tighter spend limits. Set ROAS and break-even targets to match that choice.

Account-level allocation (scale score framework)

1 min

A scale score is a number that ranks each ad for budget. The account is your whole ad account. Efficiency means how well an ad earns back its cost.

Efficiency vs scale quadrant

Quadrant Spend Efficiency Action
Scale hard High High Increase budget
Unlock scale Low High Investigate why spend is limited
Diagnose and fix High Low Find the funnel leak
Kill Low Low Turn off immediately
  • Goal: more than 70% of spend should go to ads above target ROAS. ROAS segments: exceeds, meets, below, failing. CPA segments: excellent, good, acceptable, poor, failing.

Scale score ranking formula

  • Capped at 1.5 means the number cannot go above 1.5.
  • Fatigue means the same people see the ad too often. Frequency is how many times one person sees it on average.
Scale Score = (ROAS Index × 30) + (CPA Index × 30) + (Spend Velocity × 20) + (Fatigue Index × 20).
ROAS Index = Ad ROAS / Target ROAS, capped at 1.5.
CPA Index = Target CPA / Ad CPA, capped at 1.5.
Spend Velocity = Ad daily spend / account average daily spend.
Fatigue Index = 1 − (Frequency / 6), with a minimum of 0.

Dollar allocation output

  1. For each scale candidate, state the percentage budget increase.
  2. For each ad to kill, state the budget freed.
  3. List ads to monitor with explicit triggers.
  4. State the expected impact on ROAS and CPA.
  5. For each scale candidate, also assess fatigue risk (frequency and age), concentration risk (what the backup is) and learning-reset risk if scaling too fast. A learning reset means the platform starts learning again, and results can drop for a while.

Hard versus soft metrics

1 min

Hard metrics are numbers tied to money, such as sales. Soft metrics are early signs, such as clicks and views.

Hard metrics decide, soft metrics diagnose

1 min
  • Hard metrics such as sales outrank soft metrics. If an ad makes sales, a high CPC is acceptable. Keep improving the soft metrics. If there are no sales, improve all the metrics across the funnel.
  • Hard metrics such as profitable sales matter more than soft metrics such as attention rate or click-through rate. An ad that makes sales should be kept even if its soft metrics are weak. Improve the soft metrics afterwards.
  • A low attention rate and low CTR can still be acceptable when the ad makes sales. The people who engage may be more qualified buyers. A high attention rate with no sales is not a good sign.
  • Soft metrics are early funnel signals: link clicks, add to cart and initiate checkout (the moment a customer starts paying), together with their cost columns. Hard metrics are purchases and ROAS. Read the soft metrics first to judge whether an ad is worth scaling or needs more budget.
  • Read soft metrics (engagement and early signals such as CPC and CVR, the conversion rate) alongside hard metrics (ROAS, CAC, which is customer acquisition cost, and profit). Use them to decide what to fix. A product that fails may need better work on its landing page (the page people arrive on), creative or product detail page before it is judged dead.
  • Add-to-cart activity signals real buyer interest. If CPC, CPM and CTR look bad but add-to-carts are happening, let the campaign run another day before cutting it.
  • The primary decision metric for a new Facebook product test is CTR in the first phase. CTR is read before sales, since sales need more spend and time to show up.

Conflicts on the metrics that matter

1 min
  • Daily decisions (Source E to 26): use only purchase-related metrics for daily optimization. Do not make major decisions based on CTR, CPC, CPM or conversion rate alone. These secondary metrics inform analysis but should never drive primary decisions.
  • Early test phase: CTR is the main early metric in the first phase of testing a product on Facebook. It compares the people who saw the ad with those who clicked it.
  • New store: the primary metric for a new store is CTR, at least 1.5%.
  • Add-to-cart classification: Source C lists add-to-carts among hard metrics (judge ads by sales, profit and add-to-carts). Source D lists add-to-cart among early soft signals, with purchases and ROAS as the hard metrics. Use add-to-carts as early signals, not as the final verdict, since the sources differ on the label.
  • Phase 5 ad testing: optimize using four metrics: CTR (click-through rate), CPM (cost per thousand impressions), ATC (add-to-cart rate) and conversion rate. These show creative strength, targeting efficiency, funnel engagement and sales conversion.
  • Ad metrics targets for a test: CPM below $25, CTR above 1.5% (ideally 2%), and CPC below $1.
  • Store conversion rate target during a test: at least 1.5% of visitors buying.
  • For a clear winning product, track only ROAS and sales.

Cut-early signals and metric discipline

1 min
  • Cut losing ads early using soft metrics (signs of interest, such as clicks and views, not money). Keep an ad only if these metrics improve day over day; otherwise cut it.
  • CPC and profitable spend matter more than CTR. CPC is cost per click. CTR is click-through rate, the share of people who see an ad and click it.
  • Do not judge a test ad by CTR alone.
  • Judge ads by hard metrics first (money numbers, such as profit and sales). Soft metrics matter only if an ad is not making money.
  • Judge performance using a product's numbers, not by attachment (liking the product).

Seasonality and volatility

2 min
  • Many products sell less in summer because customers spend more time outdoors. Many stores see a dip in that season unless they sell summer products. Plan lower spending for that period rather than treating it as a store failure.
  • Seasonality (sales rising and falling through the year) changes both demand (how many people want to buy) and ROAS for a product. Outside the selling season, traffic (visitors) and data are lower. A product that reached 4 to 6:1 ROAS at $50 per day dropped to about 4:1 at $10 per day, which was not enough to scale (raise budget to grow sales). Rising in-season demand can lift the same ad's ROAS again.
  • Seasonality affects results. Demand can be lower outside the peak season, which reduces traffic and ROAS. It rises again during the season, which can push ROAS higher and make scaling easier.
  • Outside the season, a product reached about 4:1 ROAS at $10 per day, which was not enough to scale in its own campaign with an aggressive budget.
  • With small order volumes, sales vary randomly from day to day and week to week, even when traffic and ads stay the same. Do not react to a few quiet days.
  • Do not increase ad spend because Pinterest or Google account representatives (the platforms' sales staff) push for bigger budgets. Their pressure does not lead to more sales.
  • Monthly sales are volatile. A few large orders can drive a month, so judge by sample size (the amount of data) over several weeks, not one month.
  • A campaign with a few large orders can show a very high return in one month and a much lower one the next. One month of results is not a reliable basis for decisions. Make judgments on a larger sample, usually several weeks of data.
  • Early results are unstable, because the pocket (the test money set aside for ads) and the pixel (Meta's tracking code on your store) are still learning.
  • Stable campaign versus first day: in a stable campaign, the source reported CPM of $3.38 (CPM is cost per 1,000 views), CTR of 2.81% and CPC of $0.12. A campaign on its first day showed CPM of $0.75, CTR of 0.63%, which shows how much early data fluctuates.
  • A new Meta campaign sold lower early results. It made a single sale from four ad sets (groups of ads with their own audience and budget) with about $3.50 each, for a $24.99 product. This is an early start, not a verdict.

Testing structure and tracking

1 min
  • Set a weekly spend limit for product testing before any campaign launches. A campaign is one ad push with its own budget. The limit determines how many products you can test in a week. A test that runs out of budget without a winner should be cut quickly.
  • Run each product in its own campaign rather than sharing one campaign. Testing two products in a shared campaign can record the same sale for both products. This makes it hard to see which product drove add-to-cart (a visitor putting an item in the basket), checkout and purchase events (recorded actions).
  • Install UTM tracking (short tags added to links that show where each visit came from) so every sale can be traced to its source campaign. Without it, Business Manager figures (Meta's ad control panel numbers) alone are not reliable for product comparison.
  • Analyze results in Business Manager and in your tracking data to see which product performs better.
  • Change only one variable at a time, such as the creative (the ad's image or video), the audience or the store. This lets you see what caused a result. Once a store and product have proven themselves, test creatives while keeping the audience and settings identical.
  • Optimize in steps so each change can be attributed to a single fix.

Ad structure and budget minimums

1 min
  • Use ABO (ad group budget: each ad group has its own budget and spends it independently) as the default structure. Use at least $25 per ad group, since the platform's minimum is $20 and new accounts often underspend at lower levels.
  • If ABO ad groups show spending problems, switch to a CBO campaign (campaign budget: one budget for the whole campaign, which the platform shares out) with about $50 to $60 at campaign level, containing about four ad groups. Let the campaign distribute budget across ad groups instead of fixing a budget per group.
  • Optimize an ad group after about every $5 to $10 of spend rather than making large decisions early. Keep a test creative running for at least two days before deciding it has failed.

Early signals before purchases

1 min
  • During product testing, the goal is not purchases but early signals: add-to-cart ratio, cost per add-to-cart, highest CTR and lowest CPC. Purchases are expected only after the page and ads are working.
  • Judge a tested product by its CTR and CPM first (and CPC). CTR shows whether people are interested in the product. A CTR above 1 percent is considered good, and 1.5 percent is considered better.
  • Review click-through rate after about 10 USD of ad spend. Send the ad screenshots for a detailed check, since per-country differences can drag down the overall CTR.
  • Roughly every fifth or sixth tested product becomes a winner, according to one expert's experience.

Learning phase and wait times

1 min
  • Facebook's learning phase (the period when the platform's automatic targeting is still finding the right buyers) requires about 50 purchase events per week for the ad algorithm to find the right people. Below that volume, the algorithm lacks data, so the creative, product and offer must carry the early results.
  • Traffic systems (ad platforms) typically need 2 to 4 weeks after setup and approval before they show their full performance. Do not judge a campaign's results before this window has passed.

Kill rules by platform (rules that tell you when to stop)

2 min
  • Break-even cost per purchase (the most you can spend on ads to win one sale before you lose money), for both TikTok and Facebook. This is the strict rule for media buying (paying for ads). Once an ad or campaign has spent that amount with no sale, cut it, especially in the scaling phase. A campaign that gives sales consistently at or before each break-even interval is the kind worth keeping.
  • Facebook, no-sale threshold: once ad spend passes about $60 without any sales, the store is likely not converting (turning visitors into buyers). At that point, test new offers and new marketing angles before spending more.
  • Facebook, near break-even: a product that is less than about 100 USD away from break-even after roughly 300 USD of ad spend, with a poor add-to-cart ratio, is not close enough to move forward with. A product that is nowhere near break-even after the test budget should be dropped.
  • Facebook, stop rule: after roughly 300 USD of ad spend, if the store is not at break-even and the add-to-cart ratio is poor, stop the product.
  • Facebook, own kill rule: turn off an ad after six days if it has spent less than $50 and sits below break-even ROAS. This is the source's own kill rule and is a starting point to adapt to the product's margins (the profit left per sale after costs).
  • Per offer: before judging a new offer (the deal you present, such as price or bonus) or ad, spend at least one break-even cost per purchase on it. Around $20 per ad is a minimum example.
  • TikTok, review: after 3 to 4 days, check each ad set against your break-even ROAS. Turn off ad sets below break-even and keep the ones with a good positive ROAS. Then scale them by horizontal scaling (copying the winner into new groups) or vertical scaling (raising its budget).
  • TikTok, ad group reduction: check the campaign about 8 to 12 hours after launch and note spend per ad group. Compare CPC, CPM and CTR across the ad groups. Cut ad groups whose CPC is clearly above the others, for example $1.50 to $2 or more against $0.50 to $0.77.

Quit checklist for organic and content tests

1 min

Checklist before quitting a product:

  1. Confirm you uploaded consistently for at least about two weeks.
  2. Rule out your store, your content type, the region where the video went viral, and a shadow ban (the platform quietly limiting your reach without telling you).
  3. If views are still low and the factors in step 2 are excluded, the product is a candidate to quit.
  4. If a product reaches 1 million views with zero sales after steps 1 to 3, treat it as a strong sign the product is wrong.
  5. If the store has more than 1,000 Shopify sessions (visits to your Shopify store, the online shop platform) with no sale, treat that as another strong quit sign.
  • On average, about 700 to 750 Shopify store sessions were needed before the first sale. A store with 1,000 sessions and no sale is a warning sign for the product.
  • A low view count that is not caused by shadow banning, content or region can also mean the product is not attracting attention. Once that is ruled out, low views are a reason to stop the product.

Hard and soft metrics

1 min
  • Hard metrics (decide on these): break-even ROAS, break-even cost per acquisition (CPA: the average ad cost of one sale), profit, and purchases once the page and ads work.
  • Soft metrics (diagnose with these): add-to-cart ratio, cost per add-to-cart, CTR, CPC, CPM and views. They signal whether a product is worth more spend, but they do not prove profitability.
  • Use the break-even ROAS, not revenue or spend, as the threshold for keeping or cutting a campaign.
  • A consistent profit of about $100 per day is a real proof of concept (proof the idea works). $1k per day is not needed yet.

Scale or hold

1 min
  • Scale a product or ad set only when its ROAS is at least breakeven ROAS plus 1. For example, with a breakeven ROAS of 2.5, scale once the ROAS reaches 3.5 or higher.
  • Scale a campaign only based on its break-even ROAS. Campaigns below break-even ROAS are not profitable, and campaigns above it can be scaled.
  • During a sale, check ROAS every four hours to decide whether to add budget. Continue scaling only while the ROAS is still strong.
  • Judge ads only after testing a batch of creatives over about a week, not on a single day. A ROAS that holds at or above break-even over that period signals profitability.
  • Products of this type can show a high ROAS when they are scaled fast. Speed of scaling matters because the product's window of demand (the time people want it) is short.
  • A high ROAS after two or three purchases usually falls back to a lower plateau (a level it settles at). Expect a drop from an early high, for example 9 ROAS, toward a more realistic level, for example 4 to 5. Plan around that, not the early number.
  • Facebook ads were expensive and inconsistent even early on, which made profitable scaling difficult. Test budgets and expectations with this in mind before committing large spend.
  • A successful TikTok ad winner does not have to be a unique product. A widely dropshipped product (sold by many stores that buy from the same supplier, who ships the order) can perform well if the ad itself is well executed. Judge the ad on execution, not on product uniqueness.
  • A store running three campaigns at about $230 a day had seven-day ROAS values of about 4.3, 3.1 and 5. The owner saw this as good enough. The advice was to build more cash flow (money coming in) before adding a new campaign structure.

Budget ramps and ROAS targets

1 min
  • Use different ROAS targets for different funnel stages (steps from first sight to buying). Bottom-of-funnel YouTube campaigns (retargeting: showing ads again to people who already visited) should use a different return-on-ad-spend target than top-of-funnel awareness campaigns (ads that make people aware of you). Top-of-funnel campaigns are judged on cheap views and later retargeting, not direct sales.
  • Scaling example from the formula: with a breakeven ROAS of 2.5, the scale trigger is a ROAS of 3.5.

Case: TikTok dropshipping, new product, April

  • Day 1: the testing structure of four ad groups at $25 to $30 each, with three creatives per group, ran. About $75 was spent for two sales and about $95.80 revenue. The winning creative had a CTR above 1 percent.

Seasonality, volatility and red days

1 min
  • Mixed green and red days are normal. Green days are profitable days and red days are losing days. Do not kill a campaign on one red day.
  • When a campaign has a red day, pump out more creatives (make and launch more ad visuals), sharpen the messaging through research, and keep CBO 2 and CBO 3 fed (budget and ads flowing) so the system can spend and learn.
  • Do not judge a TikTok test on one day of data. Give ad groups at least two days.
  • Single days can be off, so spread the spend over three to four days rather than one day when testing an offer or ad.
  • Cut a product if demand is weak, the messaging does not work, or the market is saturated in Meta Ad Library (Meta's public list of all ads, which shows how crowded a market is). Do this only after testing for at least three days.
  • Per-country differences can drag down the overall CTR, so check creatives by country before deciding.
