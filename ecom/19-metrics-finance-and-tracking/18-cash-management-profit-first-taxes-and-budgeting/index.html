Cash management, Profit First, taxes and budgeting

Section 19.18 · 18 of 23 in this chapter · 22 min read

In short

  • Money coming in is not free money. Set cash aside to pay suppliers later.
  • Plan cash ahead with realistic estimates. Do not assume early sales will last.
  • Split income into set accounts with fixed shares, and use one fixed rhythm.
  • Check the bank balance against what you owe before you spend or hire.
  • Spend money only where it reliably brings back more money.
More key points
  • Keep six months of living costs in easy-to-reach savings before you invest.
  • Buy cheap index funds (funds that hold the whole stock market) and avoid trying to time the market.
  • Keep risky bets like crypto and single stocks to 0% to 5% of your net worth.
  • Property deals and loans can earn about 7% to 10% a year, but nothing is guaranteed.

Cash flow in dropshipping

3 min
  • In dropshipping (a supplier ships each order for you), a customer pays by card. A payment processor (the company that moves card money to your bank) pays you out. Cash flow means the timing of money coming in and going out. **
  • You order from the supplier and usually pay them when the item ships. **
  • Often you pay the supplier on a credit card, which settles about 30 days later. Cash comes in before it goes out, but you still must set money aside to pay suppliers later. **
  • Inventory-based dropshipping (you buy and hold stock) generally has a positive cash flow pattern. **
  • Service businesses pay their team before they get paid. They often wait around 30 days for payment. This is a negative cash flow pattern. **
  • COGS (cost of goods sold, what the product itself costs you) is recorded when the supplier is paid. **
  • Businesses with long lead times (the wait from order to delivery) show large profit months when income arrives before supplier payments. Lower-profit or loss months follow. **
  • This pattern is normal. It does not mean there is a problem. **
  • Dropshipping cash moves in a wave. In a rising season, the store collects customer cash faster than it pays suppliers. **
  • When demand falls, the store pays suppliers for earlier orders. Supplier bills can lag orders by weeks or months. So cash can look worse than the sales trend. **
  • In a seasonal store (sales rise and fall with the seasons), income often rises before expenses. Supplier payments lag sales by 4 to 6 weeks for made-to-order products (goods made only after an order). **
  • Cash builds early and is spent later in the season, even as income falls. Some periods show profit while cash decreases. **
  • Do not treat one profitable month as cash you can spend. **
  • Treating a strong profit month as available cash leads to overspending. Then the bank balance drops suddenly when supplier bills are paid. **
  • A loss month after a profit month can be normal timing. Decide using reports and cash flow, not one month's result. **
  • Save cash from profitable periods to pay suppliers later. Supplier payments may come due after the income is already received and spent. **
  • The smooth seasonal profit and cash curve in the source is idealized, meaning it is a neat, perfect version. Real dropshipping businesses are far more volatile, meaning they swing up and down much more. **
  • Plan reserves (money set aside) for sharp swings, not the smooth average. **
  • A surprise supplier bill means you did not track what you owe. It can turn an apparent profit into a serious loss. **
  • A bank balance higher than expected is not automatically free profit. Unpaid supplier bills may still be owed from that money. **
  • Compare the bank balance with what you owe suppliers before you decide how much to spend or hire. **
  • High-ticket dropshipping (selling expensive products) is a cash flow business. Money constantly moves through sales and supplier payments. **
  • Keep cash flow positive. If you spend customer money on other expenses, you will quickly owe suppliers. **
  • A growing debt balance is a warning sign, even when cash looks healthy. Liabilities (what you owe) that exceed assets (what you own) leave the business exposed. **
  • Track card and loan balances every month. Aim to pay them down over time. **

Cash planning and startup funding

2 min
  • Bookkeeping (writing down money that has moved) records the past. **
  • Cash planning decides in advance what each incoming dollar will do. **
  • Bookkeeping alone does not prevent cash shortages. You need both. **
  • Cash flow management means knowing how much you can safely take out of the business without harming it. **
  • Without a planned split, the owner cannot answer that question from the bank balance alone. **
  • Cash flow is named as the biggest struggle for entrepreneurs (business owners).
  • Forecast cash realistically. Do not assume early sales will last. Keep reserves, so that a slow month does not threaten your personal finances.
  • You will need to front cash (pay before you get paid back) to buy inventory. You must get it into your warehouse, a 3PL (an outside company that stores and ships your products), or Amazon. **
  • Plan cash for this entire period. It can last a while, depending on lead times and origin (where the product comes from). **
  • A common mistake is planning the budget around the first order only, such as the cash now in the bank. Then you forget to plan for the next orders. **
  • Keep a cash plan that covers the whole cycle until the business generates positive cash flow. **
  • Early ad spend (money paid for ads) is usually funded from a credit card. The aim is to turn that spend into sales quickly. **
  • The full daily budget does not have to be paid out of pocket every day. **
  • Plan about $1,000 in marketing spend for the first few months. That is about $30 per day. **
  • Sales should cover much of this spend as the store starts earning. So little of your own money is needed. **
  • Plan a first-month budget of about 1,000 dollars for collecting data and generating sales. **
  • You can start with less. But you will collect data more slowly. **
  • Keep the starting budget balanced between marketing and inventory.
  • Too much inventory without demand leaves stock stuck, meaning it does not sell. Too much demand without enough product means orders cannot be filled.
  • Professional videos and photos for a clothing brand can cost about $1,000 per month. This is a workable starting budget for content that drives early sales.
  • Total investment plus advertising spend since the start was estimated at over a quarter million, up to half a million. The currency is not stated.
  • Plan total funding needs with this scale of spend in mind.
  • A US company can unlock funding options. These are ways to borrow. Examples are credit lines from Shopify Capital (Shopify's lending program), 8fig and Sellers Funding.
  • These credit lines can finance inventory or scale ad spend (grow how much you spend on ads).

Inventory forecasting and stock tracking

1 min

  • Inventory forecasting (predicting how much stock you will need) is non-negotiable.

  • Spend at least one hour per week on it. Half a day with your team is the optimum (best).

  • It is the founder/CEO's direct responsibility (the company's top leader). It is not delegated alone to an inventory planner.

  • Inventory forecasting directly prevents bankruptcy.

  • It is the single most important operational task for founders. Operational means the day-to-day running of the business. It drives everything else.

  • Inventory forecast process:

    1. Review what inventory and sales happened in the same period last year.
    2. Create predictions for the coming period.
    3. Write the reasoning behind each prediction.
    4. Adjust forecasts weekly based on actual results.

  • Days of stock (how long your stock will last):

    Days of stock = units on hand ÷ average units sold per day
Example: 300 units ÷ 10 units sold per day = 30 days, about one month of stock

  • The 80/20 rule for inventory means 20% of products generate 80% of sales. **

  • Update inventory in real time (the moment things change) only for the top 20% of top 20%, not for all products. **

  • Track each store's stock daily, per supplier.

  • A rapid drop in monthly gross sales (total sales before costs) signals out-of-stock problems. Out of stock means items you have run out of. For example, sales can fall from $50,000 to $20,000.

  • These problems need new supplier approvals. **

Tax obligations and deductions

1 min

  • Financial forecasting is essential. Tax authorities will not wait for payment.

  • Schedule and predict sales tax (tax charged on sales) and income tax (tax on your profit) monthly or quarterly.

  • If you cannot forecast inventory and revenue accurately, you cannot forecast the cash available for tax payments.

  • Government can seize products and inventory (take them) for unpaid taxes. This forces business failure.

  • In the US, shipping costs, Shopify fees (the store platform's charges), transaction fees (2.9%, a fee on each payment), ad spend and product cost are all tax-deductible business expenses. Tax-deductible means you can subtract them before paying tax. **

  • You pay income tax on revenue minus total expenses. **

  • Net profit (what is left after all costs), and what is taxed:

    Net profit = gross revenue − deductions (COGS, shipping, ad spend, merchant fees, other expenses)
Example: 1,000,000 gross − about 800,000 deductions = 200,000 net profit
Only the net profit is taxed.

  • ROAS (return on ad spend, the sales made per dollar of ads) does not include taxes.

  • Taxes are not included in the ROAS or ad budget calculation. They are paid out of profit.

  • In the Profit First allocation (a method that splits income into set accounts), taxes take a set share of real revenue (the money left after product costs). See Profit First allocations below.

  • Amazon pays FBA sellers every two weeks.
  • Use that cycle to fund reorders (ordering more stock). Pay the 30% supplier deposit (a part paid upfront) right after the product launches.
  • Then cover the 70% balance when Amazon deposits sales revenue into your account 14 days later.
  • Store sales through platforms like Shopify are paid out right away. So ad spend that returns well can be reinvested quickly.
  • A 10% reserve on payouts (money held back from each payment) ties up roughly $0.80 of profit for every unit sold. This is when profit is about $8 per product.
  • The reserve is calculated on payouts. So it grows with sales volume.
  • Prepaying Google Ads (paying the bill in advance) is an optional billing method, not the default.
  • For a new store, prepaying is generally not advisable. The normal billing-threshold setup (you are billed once spend reaches a set amount) keeps cash free until spend builds up. **

Profit First allocations

5 min

  • Setting spending as percentages of real revenue creates a budget limit. Money not allocated to an account should not be spent. **

  • This counters the tendency for spending to grow to match whatever cash is available. This is called Parkinson's law (spending expands to use what is available). **

  • Real revenue is monthly revenue minus cost of goods sold (COGS). **

  • With COGS at 80% of revenue, real revenue is 20% of revenue. **

  • The profit, owner salary, taxes, staff and operating expense allocations are all percentages of real revenue. They must total 100%. **

  • COGS and real revenue formulas:

    COGS amount = income in the period × COGS percentage
Real revenue = income − COGS amount = income × (1 − COGS percentage)
Example: income of 100,000 per month at 80% COGS gives 80,000 COGS and 20,000 real revenue.
Example: income of 25,000 per week at 80% COGS gives 20,000 COGS and 5,000 real revenue.

    **

  • The percentages assigned to real revenue accounts must add up to 100%. This way, every dollar of real revenue has a job. **

  • Each account amount equals real revenue multiplied by that account's percentage. **

  • Example allocation of real revenue. The percentages are examples, not fixed rules. **

    Account Share of real revenue Example: real revenue 20,000 per month Example: real revenue 5,000 per week
    Profit 10% 2,000 500
    Owner pay 20% 4,000 1,000
    Taxes 10% 2,000 500
    Staff 30% 6,000 1,500
    Operating expenses 30% 6,000 1,500
    Total 100% 20,000 5,000

  • Profit First example, monthly, with COGS at 80% of revenue. The source's own allocation differs from the table above. Owner salary is 10% and operating expenses 40% here. The table above has owner pay at 20% and operating expenses at 30%. Both total 100%. **

  • Use the allocation that a Profit First assessment gives for your business. A Profit First assessment is the method's own tool for setting your shares. **

    Line Share Example at $20,000 revenue Example at $40,000 revenue
    Revenue 100% $20,000 $40,000
    Cost of goods sold 80% of revenue $16,000 $32,000
    Real revenue 20% of revenue $4,000 $8,000
    Profit 10% of real revenue $400 $800
    Owner salary 10% of real revenue $400 $800
    Taxes 10% of real revenue $400 $800
    Staff 30% of real revenue $1,200 $2,400
    Operating expenses 40% of real revenue $1,600 $3,200
    Total allocation 100% $4,000 $8,000

  • Allocation percentages depend on the business. No universal set of numbers can be given. **

  • Use a Profit First assessment to set them before implementing the plan. **

  • Weekly example with 45,000 in sales and 80% COGS. **

    Item Amount
    Sales for the week 45,000
    COGS (80%) 36,000
    Real revenue 9,000
    Profit (10%) 900
    Owner pay (20%) 1,800
    Taxes (10%) 900
    Staff (30%) 2,700
    Operating expenses (30%) 2,700

  • Lowering the COGS percentage from 80% to 75% on 45,000 in weekly sales raises real revenue to 11,250. It lowers the COGS amount to 33,750.

  • Lowering operating expenses from 30% to 20% frees 10% of real revenue. You can allocate that elsewhere, for example to profit. **

  • Regular allocation routine. **

    1. Check how much income arrived in the income account since the last allocation.
    2. Multiply that income by your COGS percentage. Transfer that amount to the COGS account.
    3. The remaining money is real revenue. Calculate each other account's share from it.
    4. Transfer the profit share to the profit account, the owner pay share to the owner pay account, the tax share to the taxes account, the staff share to the staff account, and the operating share to the operating expenses account.
    5. Pay suppliers only from the COGS account, staff only from the staff account, and other business costs only from the operating expenses account.
    6. Pay the owner's salary from the owner pay account at every allocation. Leave profit untouched until the quarterly distribution.
    7. Repeat the allocation on a fixed rhythm, either twice a month or weekly. Review the percentages once a quarter.

  • Allocations can run twice a month, as the book suggests. Or they can run weekly, as the presenter's company does.

  • Choose one fixed rhythm and keep it. Balances are checked and split at that interval. **

  • For planning, use a monthly calculation rather than a weekly or fortnightly one. Fortnightly means every two weeks. Base it on the whole month's income. **

  • Start from a long-term target. This could be a lump-sum exit (one large payout when you sell or leave the business) or a revenue and profit goal. Then work backwards to a monthly revenue or profit target. **

  • Review allocation percentages once a quarter (every three months). Adjust them when a balance keeps building up unused, or when margins (what you keep per sale) change. **

  • Surplus (extra money) from an account that is not being used can move to profit or owner pay. **

  • Keep allocating in this way even when the business is young. **

  • Keep profit low during startup, to reinvest in the business. But never take zero profit. Owners should always pay themselves some profit. **

  • Operating expenses (the day-to-day costs of running the business) in the Profit First plan must include advertising and PPC costs. PPC means pay-per-click ads, where you pay each time someone clicks. **

  • Make sure the operating expense budget can cover paid ads before you increase spend. **

  • Using accounting software to separate money, without real bank accounts, is a mistake.

  • Software only shows numbers. It does not hold the money apart. Use separate accounts in addition to bookkeeping. **

  • Taking unplanned, irregular withdrawals creates instability and burnout risk. An example is a large payout one month and nothing for several months.

  • Set a fixed owner pay percentage instead. **

  • Staff budget formula and example. **

    Staff budget = real revenue × staff allocation percentage
Remaining budget = staff budget − total current staff pay
Example: 30% of 4,000 real revenue = 1,200 budget; a 1,000 customer service hire leaves 200.

  • Profit First planning steps. **

    1. Write down the long-term vision, such as target revenue, target profit, or a lump-sum exit amount.
    2. Work backwards from that target to a monthly revenue or profit goal.
    3. Enter current monthly income and the cost of goods sold percentage to calculate real revenue.
    4. Set the allocation percentages for profit, owner salary, taxes, staff and operating expenses. Use a Profit First assessment, so they total 100% of real revenue.
    5. Calculate the monthly staff budget from the staff allocation.
    6. Enter the current team and total staff pay. Then read the remaining budget.
    7. Enter the monthly cost of the next planned hire. Make the hire only when the remaining budget covers it.
    8. Enter the maximum revenue capacity the current team can handle. Also enter the target capacity the owner is aiming for.
    9. Each month, check remaining budget and capacity. Hire when the budget and team allow it.
    10. When the staff budget keeps building up without need, lower the staff allocation (the example used 20%). Move more into profit and owner salary.

Owner pay, distributions and draws

2 min

  • Pay yourself a fixed salary, for example 2,000 per month in two installments (payments). **

  • Set monthly sales or profit goals that trigger a larger distribution (a larger payment to the owner) when reached. For example, an 8,000 bonus after 10,000 in profit. Keep the remaining profit in the business. **

  • Owner pay is transferred at every allocation. Profit is distributed to the owner only once a quarter. **

  • Keeping profit in its account until the quarterly payout builds reserves before any profit is taken. **

  • Early-stage profit distributions (2020 source): take distributions immediately after generating net profit. Do not wait for a set accounting period. **

  • Example: after several weeks of sales generate $5,000 net profit, take $3,000 as a distribution. Reinvest $2,000 into growth. **

  • This is the older approach. The 2021-22 Profit First method above holds profit until the quarterly payout. **

  • Owner withdrawals and profit distributions do not reduce the profit shown on the P&L (profit and loss report). **

  • They appear on the balance sheet (a snapshot of what the business owns and owes) through equity (the owner's share of the business). They also appear in the cash flow statement under financing (money moving in or out from owners and lenders). **

  • So an owner can take money out and still see a profit for that month. **

  • Owner contributions (money the owner puts in) increase equity. Owner draws (money the owner takes out) reduce equity. **

  • Both show up as financing flows in the cash flow statement. Keep these separate from operating expenses, so the profit and loss still shows true business performance. **

  • Pay yourself first from the business. Know what your household costs per year. Include children's education, food and clothing. Take that amount out as income. **

  • Reinvest the rest of the profit into the business. Taking out all profit leads to heavy taxes and slower growth. **

  • Reinvestment amount (money put back into the business):

    Reinvestment amount = annual business profit − annual personal living costs
Example: 300,000 profit − 100,000 yearly living costs = about 200,000 reinvested

    **

Business money versus personal money

1 min
  • Couples or partners running a store together should set a separate budget for the business.
  • Keep savings for emergencies and family needs out of the business budget.
  • Plan the money before starting, not after the first sales come in.
  • Partners should openly discuss how much financial risk each is willing to take before spending money. Tolerance for risk often differs.
  • Agree on spending limits for each risk level.
  • Track profit and expenses in dedicated business accounts, so the store's performance can be measured cleanly over time. **
  • Do not run a skeleton-budget business (a bare-bones business running on the minimum) that spends every dollar and leaves one dollar after taxes.
  • Lawsuits require cash reserves for legal defense and settlement. Maintain financial buffers.
  • Saving what you keep matters more than the amount you earn.
  • Create a budget and live within your means.
  • Limit small recurring (repeated) and impulse purchases (sudden buys), such as takeout, daily coffee, or replacing equipment that still works, to build savings.
  • For small accounts in a mail business, the difference between profit and loss depends more on how promptly payments are collected than on any other single factor.
  • Set a clear timeline for following up on overdue accounts (bills not yet paid).

Reinvestment and budgeting

1 min
  • Money is a tool in business, not an emotional object. Spend it when it reliably makes more money.
  • Example: if each dollar of ad spend returns 1.50 dollars, spending more on that channel helps you reach more customers and grow sales. Ad spend means money paid for online ads. Limit spending only when the returns do not justify it.
  • Some offers pay back right away. Others take months to recover costs. The payback period is how long it takes to earn back what you spent. Know it before you spend. Fund a campaign according to how long its payback takes. Only take on the cash risk you can carry.
  • Brex is a business credit card that pays rewards (points or cash back). With Brex, about 10,000 USD of Facebook ad spend returned roughly 100 to 300 USD in rewards. The rate changes with the card and the type of spending.
  • Set a minimum monthly savings amount: 3,000 USD per month in the example, with better months saving up to 8,000 USD. Put this money into income-producing assets (things that pay you money, such as rental property) before optional spending.

Personal investing and wealth building

1 min

Context and order of priorities

1 min

  • Net worth means everything you own minus everything you owe. US figures given: the average American has under $1,000 in net worth. About 85% of people have under $10,000, even when many earn $100,000 or more. Treat these as rough US-based context, not current statistics. (Source undated.)

  • Order for turning income into wealth:

    1. Cut monthly expenses as far as possible.
    2. Calculate your monthly living expenses and multiply by six.
    3. Keep that amount as an emergency fund in a liquid (easy to access), no-fee online savings account.
    4. Once the emergency fund is full, send a fixed amount every month to investments instead of the savings account.

  • Keep an emergency fund of six months of living costs in liquid savings before you invest. Liquid means cash you can reach quickly. Without this fund, you may have to sell investments during a downturn (a market drop) or pay early withdrawal penalties (fees for taking money out early). Both can wipe out your gains.

  • Store income means income from running a store. It is semi-passive, because automation (software that runs tasks for you) still needs checking. Invested capital (money put into investments) can grow without monitoring. Investment income is therefore the most truly passive income stream.

Compounding and entry methods

1 min

  • Compounding means growth earns growth. Example: $1,000 invested every month at an assumed 8% annual return, compounded monthly (growth added to the balance each month). Each year's contribution is added on top of earlier growth. Results change with the actual return, so re-run the numbers with a current calculator.

    Years Total contributed Approximate total value Approximate interest earned
    10 $120,000 $186,000 $65,000
    20 $240,000 $600,000 about $360,000
    30 $360,000 $1,500,000 about $1,140,000

  • Dollar-cost averaging means investing the same amount on a schedule. Instead of investing a lump sum (all the money at once), invest a fixed amount regularly. This reduces the risk of entering at a bad price. Example: $100,000 invested as $10,000 per month over 10 months.

  • Check the math of any plan that spreads a lump sum over months. $5,000 per month for six months only covers $30,000. So a $60,000 sum at $5,000 per month takes 12 months. Work out the number of months from the total and the monthly amount before you commit.

  • Modern online platforms let small monthly amounts (200 USD or 1,000 USD in the examples) enter assets that once needed large sums. One example is apartment buildings shared with many co-investors (other people investing in the same deal). A large starting capital is no longer required.

  • Real estate investing in income buildings (buildings that earn rent) no longer needs large capital. Monthly contributions of $200 to $1,000 can be enough to start. The average expected return cited for these investments is 9-10% per year. Expected return means the yearly gain you expect.

Index funds, fees and not timing the market

1 min
  • Do not try to time the market, meaning guess when prices will rise or fall. Holding large amounts in cash while you wait for a dip can cost more than the dip would have saved. Example given: staying in cash during a year when the market gained about 22% cost that gain.
  • An index fund buys a small share of the whole publicly traded stock market. Vanguard is cited as one provider. The strategy is to hold it long term rather than trade it.
  • Over long periods, most highly paid financial advisors, mutual fund managers (people who run pooled investment funds) and hedge fund managers (people who run private, high-risk funds) do not beat the total stock market. Fees reduce returns further. Low-fee index investing is therefore the default option the text recommends.
  • Low-cost index funds that buy the whole stock market (Vanguard was named) avoid the 2-4% annual fees some financial advisors charge. Fees reduce long-term returns.
  • Selling during a downturn turns a paper loss (a loss on paper, not yet final) into a real loss. Example: if you own 100 shares (units of ownership) of an index fund and the market falls 50%, you have lost nothing until you sell. So hold unless you need the money.
  • Investment platforms named as examples: Betterment, a robo-advisor (an app that invests for you automatically), and Fundrise, a real estate investment platform. Check fees, minimum deposits (the least you must put in) and regulation (government rules) in your own country before you use any platform.

Expected returns (sources differ)

1 min
  • Long-term expected return on diversified investments (spread across many assets) such as stocks, real estate or funds is cited as about 10% per year. The compound interest example assumes 8%. Use a conservative rate (a cautious, lower estimate) when you plan.
  • Real estate income buildings: 9-10% per year (see Compounding and entry methods).
  • PeerStreet, a property-backed loan platform (investors lend money secured by real estate), reported rates of about 7% to 8%. Loans carry default risk, meaning the borrower may not pay back. The claim that you never lose money should not be treated as a guarantee.
  • Pure Street offers secured hard-money loans (short-term loans backed by property). Average return given: 7-9% per year. Some banks paid around 2% at the time. (Source undated; bank rate is a historical comparison.)

Risk limits and speculation

1 min
  • Speculation means high-risk bets for quick gains. Limit speculative investments such as Bitcoin, cryptocurrency (digital money) and individual stocks to 0% to 5% of total net worth. Never put more into speculation than that. Avoid anything risky you cannot afford to lose.
  • Speculative assets such as cryptocurrencies and newer platforms can lose the entire amount invested. Some were lost completely when their platforms failed. Limit these holdings to an amount you can afford to lose completely.

Property and lending

1 min
  • Turnkey rental properties (houses bought ready to rent out) are often the most expensive house on the block. This raises the risk of losing value and tenants in a downturn. A larger apartment building of 40 or more units with a full-time property manager (a person who runs the building day to day) spreads that risk. The example assumes about 95% occupancy (share of units rented), even if a few tenants leave.
  • Property-backed loan platforms such as PeerStreet let investors lend against real estate. If a borrower defaults (stops paying), the lender can take the collateral (the property pledged as security) and sell it to recover the money. Returns of about 7% to 8% were reported. This is not guaranteed. It depends on the loan and the quality of the collateral.
  • Unsecured peer-to-peer microloans (small person-to-person loans with no property behind them) can lose the full amount if the borrower stops paying. Loans secured by property let the lender recover funds by selling the property. Spreading a loan across many investors (about 1,000 USD each) further reduces risk.
  • Pure Street is a platform where many investors each fund a small slice of a secured hard-money loan backed by property. If the borrower defaults, the platform handles eviction (removing a person from a property through court) and other court steps. It sends monthly progress updates.
