Bidding: cost cap, bid cap and automated rules

Section 9.10 · 10 of 22 in this chapter · 11 min read

In short

  • Beginners should start with automatic bidding, where Meta sets the prices for you.
  • Manual bidding only fits big spenders, roughly $10,000 or more on one account.
  • A cost cap aims for an average cost per sale. A bid cap is a hard limit on each sale.
  • Set your cost cap below break-even, the cost per sale where you stop making money.
  • Use automatic on/off rules to stop ad sets that waste money.
This section has 19 parts. Tap one to open it.

Manual and Automatic Bidding: The Options

1 min

Bidding means the price you offer Meta each time your ad could show. Meta is the company that runs Facebook and Instagram ads. Automatic bidding means Meta sets the prices. Manual bidding means you set the limits yourself.

Campaign Bid Strategies in Ads Manager

1 min

Ads Manager is Meta's tool for building and running ads. A bid strategy is the rule that tells Meta how to bid. ROAS means return on ad spend: the money you earn back for each dollar spent.

Ads Manager name Type What it does Typical use
Highest volume or value Automatic, default Meta gets the most results or purchase value for the budget Standard setup, beginners
Cost per result goal (cost cap) Manual Aims for an average cost per result while maximizing volume Control over cost per purchase
ROAS goal Manual Aims for a set return on ad spend (example: 2.5) while maximizing value Control over return
Bid cap Manual Sets the highest bid Meta may pay in any auction Hard maximum per result
  • If you never change the bid strategy, Meta uses Highest volume or value by default. This is the automatic strategy. All standard campaigns run this way unless the bid strategy is edited.
  • A ROAS goal asks Meta to reach a set return on ad spend, for example 2.5. In plain words, 2.5 means $2.50 back for every $1 spent.
  • The ROAS goal maximizes the total value of results, such as average order value (the average amount per order).
  • Unlike a cost cap, which sets a target cost per purchase, this option sets the target by return on spend.
  • The Cost per result goal field is editable only when the campaign bid strategy is cost per result goal.
  • With Highest volume, that field shows none and cannot be changed.

Automatic Bidding as the Default

1 min
  • Automatic bidding typically outperforms bid capping. Bid capping means setting a maximum price per auction. An auction is each moment Meta picks which ad to show.
  • A lifetime budget is a total budget for the whole run. It is worth testing.
  • Focus on making creative (the image or video) and copy (the text) very relevant to your audience.
  • Then let Facebook's algorithm (its automatic system) find your audience.
  • For a product test, set the campaign bid strategy to highest volume when creating the CBO sales campaign.
  • CBO means campaign budget optimization. You set one budget for the whole campaign, and Meta moves it between ad sets.
  • Automatic bidding is still the recommended default, so test manual bidding on your own account first.
  • Beginners should start with automatic bidding because it usually performs well.

When Manual Bidding Makes Sense

1 min
  • An ad account is the Meta account that holds your ads.
  • Manual bidding is meant for advanced ad accounts that spend roughly $10,000 to $20,000 or more on a single account.
  • Higher spend, up to about $100,000, gives Meta more purchase data (records of who bought).
  • Below that level, use the scaling and testing approach with automatic bidding instead.
  • Use manual bidding when you want control over the cost of each result.
  • Or use it when you must spend a large amount in a short time, for example $10,000 in one day for an offer.
  • Keep a stop-loss in place. A stop-loss is a limit that stops spending once losses get too high.
  • A cost cap (a target average cost per result, explained below) is the most successful manual setup.
  • Bid cap and ROAS goal campaigns are built in almost the same way.
  • In the author's experience, the bid cap approach did not outperform automatic bidding.
  • Cap the bid, then raise it slowly until you find a sweet spot. A sweet spot is the best value, not too high and not too low.
  • Still, default to automatic bidding with relevant creative and copy, and let Facebook find the audience.

Cost Cap and Bid Cap Explained

1 min
  • A cost cap (set as Cost per result goal) targets an average cost per result. It is not a limit on each single result.
  • Example: with a $20 cost cap, some purchases may cost $22 and others $19, as long as the average stays near $20.
  • A cost cap lets some purchases run above the target, for example $26 or $27, as long as the average is around the cap.
  • A bid cap is a maximum bid per auction.
  • With a $25 bid cap, Meta cannot pay more than $25 for any single purchase.
  • Every result is held under that limit, which is stricter than a cost cap average.
  • With a $25 bid cap, all purchases should land under $25.
  • Do not assume a cost cap guarantees that every purchase costs less than the cap.
  • Meta only aims for the average.
  • If you need a hard per-purchase maximum, use a bid cap instead.
  • The same method used for cost cap works for bid cap, with the same campaign structure.
  • The method is: test a set of caps, apply automated rules (on/off switches that run on their own), and narrow the sweet spot.
  • The max CPC (the highest cost per click you accept) from the calculator applies to any cost-per-click campaign, on Google or Facebook.
  • Use the same break-even and target values for both. Break-even is the cost per sale where you neither win nor lose money.

Setting the Baseline Cost per Purchase

1 min

Find your baseline cost per purchase (the normal price you pay for one sale) before you build a cost cap campaign:

  1. Open a campaign (the main container for your ads and budget) or ad set (a group of ads with one audience) that is profitable and has spent more than $1,000.
  2. Read its cost per purchase (cost per conversion, the average money spent to get one sale) in Ads Manager.
  3. Round it to a working number, for example $50.79 becomes $51. This is the ideal cost per purchase.
  4. Optionally use the account-wide average cost per purchase if the whole account is profitable.
  5. Set this value as the first cost cap to test, then test a lower value (for example $49) to look for a better sweet spot.
  • Example: a campaign with about $45,000 in spend and profitable ROAS showed a cost per purchase of $50.79.
  • It was rounded to $51 as the reference cost cap.
  • The next test was a lower cap of $49, to check whether Meta still delivers profitable results.
  • The cost cap should always be set below the break-even cost per purchase.
  • A cap above break-even makes the campaign unprofitable, even with many sales.
  • Example: a $70 cap when break-even is $60 gives an average purchase cost of $70.

Building the Cost Cap Test Campaign

2 min

Build the cost cap test campaign (a trial campaign to find the right cap) in Ads Manager:

  1. Click Create and select the Sales objective (buying type as usual).
  2. Turn on Advantage campaign budget (Meta's name for CBO, the budget set at campaign level).
  3. In Campaign bid strategy, click Edit and select Cost per result goal.
  4. Set a high campaign budget, starting at $1,000 to $2,000.
  5. Go to the ad set level and scroll down to the Cost per result goal field, then enter the first cost cap, 51.
  6. Select the times (ad scheduling, meaning the hours your ads run) if needed.
  7. In targeting (who sees the ads), create one ad set with a broad audience (a wide group with few limits). Then duplicate it to create one ad set with a stacked winning interest audience (several interests combined that have already worked). Keep countries, age and other settings the same.
  8. Duplicate each of these two ad sets four more times and change the cost cap for each pair: 51, 49, 47, 45, 43. Each cost cap value is used by one broad and one stacked interest ad set.
  9. Result: one high-budget CBO campaign with 10 ad sets.
  10. Add the automated rules described below to all 10 ad sets.
  • The structure is one CBO campaign with 10 ad sets.
  • Each cost cap value (51, 49, 47, 45, 43) is shared by two ad sets, one broad and one stacked winning interest.
  • The cap is reduced by $2 for each step down.
  • Use a higher campaign budget, starting at $1,000 to $2,000.
  • A higher budget gets more auction priority and spends more.
  • A very low budget, such as $100 to $200, often does not spend at all.
  • This happens because the campaign loses auctions.
  • Mapping conflict: this build puts the cost cap on a CBO campaign.
  • Source D 2026 maps the bid strategies the other way round: CBO goes with bid cap, and ABO goes with cost cap. ABO means ad set budget optimization, where the budget is set on each ad set.
  • Use the CBO-cost-cap build above when following the Source C test.
  • Use the Source D mapping when your structure is ABO or you are testing bid cap on CBO.

Automated Rules for Cost Cap Campaigns

1 min

Creating the Rules

1 min

Create the automated rules for the cost cap campaign:

  1. Select all ad sets inside the cost cap campaign.
  2. Click Rules, then Create a new rule, then Custom rule.
  3. Enter the rule name (Safeguard rule, Hard stop, Trim the fat or Revival).
  4. Enter the conditions (the checks the rule runs) and set the action to turn off the ad set (or turn it on for the revival rule), as described in the individual rules.
  5. Set the time range to from today and continuously.
  6. Save the rule and repeat for each of the four rules.

Safeguard Rule

1 min
  • Rule: turn off an ad set when spend is greater than 3x the cost per add to cart.
  • Add to cart means a shopper puts an item in their basket. Cost per add to cart is what you pay for each one.
  • The Meta pixel is the tracking code on your website that reports shopper actions to Meta.
  • The rule only applies when add to cart (Meta pixel) is less than 1, and website purchase is less than 1.
  • Example: with a $10 cost per add to cart, the threshold is $30.
  • Purchase is added to the Safeguard rule.
  • The reason: Meta sometimes fails to track add to cart while still tracking purchases.
  • An ad set with zero add to carts but at least one purchase should not be turned off.
  • Purpose: the rule targets ad sets with no add to carts at all.
  • This signals a delivery or relevance problem, not a sales problem. Delivery means how Meta shows your ads. Relevance means how well they fit the audience.
  • Because it checks early engagement (how people react early), it can remove weak ad sets before they reach the larger spend thresholds of the later rules.

Hard Stop Rule

1 min
  • Rule: turn off an ad set when spend is greater than 2x the cost per purchase and purchase (Meta pixel) is less than 1.
  • Example: with a $25 cost per purchase, the threshold is $50.
  • Base the cost per purchase used for the hard stop and trim the fat rules on the average cost per purchase of the last week, not on your best days.
  • Use a profitable average as the normal cost per purchase.

Trim the Fat Rule

1 min
  • Rule: turn off an ad set when spend is greater than 3x the cost per purchase, purchases are greater than 0, and website purchase ROAS is less than 2.0.
  • Example: with a $25 cost per purchase, the spend threshold is $75.
  • Purpose: an ad set can earn one or two early purchases. That keeps it out of the hard stop rule, and it can then keep spending at poor returns.
  • Turning off ad sets that have spent about three times the cost per purchase, with ROAS below 2.0, stops this drain.
  • This happens before it uses up a large campaign budget.

Revival Rule

1 min
  • Rule: turn an ad set back on when its ROAS is greater than 2.0 and its spend is greater than $10.
  • This restores ad sets that were turned off earlier but later received purchases.
  • Meta sometimes credits a sale to an ad late, after half a day. This is called late attribution.

Conflicting Figures in the 1.5x Breakeven Rule

1 min
  • Rule: set an automatic rule that turns off any CBO which spends 1.5 times the breakeven cost per purchase with zero sales.
  • Formula check: with a 60 USD breakeven, 1.5x gives 90 USD.
  • The worked example uses a 45 USD threshold, which does not match the formula.
  • Verify the number before setting the rule.

Spend Limits by Product Price

1 min
  • CTR means click-through rate: the share of people who see your ad and click it.
  • An automated rule can turn off an ad set when it has spent $10 and its CTR is below 1.5%.
  • For a higher ticket item (a more expensive product), use a higher spend limit.
  • Example: $20 to $30, especially if CTR is above 1.5%.

Morning Ad Sets

1 min
  • A morning ad set is an ad set that runs in the morning.
  • If it is losing money and the losses do not recover by the end of the day, shut it down or cut its budget by half.
  • If the ad set usually recovers by evening and makes money, let it run.

Finding the Sweet Spot

1 min
  1. Check which caps are profitable in the campaign. Example: 51, 49 and 47 are profitable, 45 and 43 are not.
  2. Launch a new test campaign with caps only between the last profitable and first unprofitable value, for example 47, 46.5, 46 and 45.5.
  3. Repeat the narrowing until a profitable range of 0.5 to $1 is found.
  4. Use the lowest profitable cap in that range as the best cost cap for the account or product.

Automated Rule Testing for Interest Audiences

1 min

Interest audiences are groups of people chosen by their interests.

  1. Launch a batch of new interest audiences at once.
  2. Let each audience run for two to three days.
  3. An automated rule switches off any audience that does not meet the set criteria.
  4. Keep running the audiences that meet the criteria.

Estimated Bid Ranges Before Launch

1 min
  • Facebook shows an estimated bid range for an audience. This is its guess at the price per click.
  • The range gives an average, a maximum and a minimum price per click (CPC).
  • Example: the average was 50 cents, the maximum about 65 cents and the minimum about 20 cents.
  • The range helps you choose a starting bid (your first price offer) before you launch.
