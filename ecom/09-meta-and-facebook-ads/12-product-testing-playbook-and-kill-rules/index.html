Playbook › 9. Meta and Facebook ads

Product testing playbook and kill rules

Section 9.12 · 12 of 22 in this chapter · 44 min read

In short

  • Spend enough before you judge a product. Test budgets run from about $50 to $300 per product, depending on the goal.
  • Judge an ad set only after about $20 to $30 of spend. Stopping earlier means you decide too soon.
  • Stop a product at its spend cap if it makes no sale. Don't keep pouring money into a loser.
  • Change one thing at a time. Each ad set tests one interest, and every ad set uses the same ads.
  • Let each ad set (a group of ads with one audience and one budget) spend its break-even cost per purchase (the most you can pay for one sale and still not lose money) or run three days, whichever is later, before you judge it.
More key points
  • If there is no sale, check the CPC (cost per click). Under $1.50, wait one more day. At $1.50 or more, replace the ad set.
  • Do not judge a product after one bad day. Wait three to four days and read the trend.
  • If ads get clicks but no sales, the problem is usually the store. Fix the store before the ads.
  • If the whole campaign has zero sales after three to four days, stop it and troubleshoot.
  • Test a new product in several countries first, then put money behind the best one.
  • Spend at least $50 per country before you judge a product.
  • Do not stop a product after one day or a few hours.
  • Check your store page and supplier before you spend money on ads.

Test budget and total spend

3 min
  • Test a product with a total budget of $150. This is faster than smaller tests. The lowest test budget mentioned for this purpose is $50.
  • Budget about $1,500 per month for Facebook testing. Facebook ads run through Meta, which also runs Instagram. Plan roughly $300 to $400 per product, and aim to test one to five products per month.
  • A per-product test budget of $300 to $400 covers changing landing pages (the page visitors land on after clicking), creatives (the ad images or videos), countries and offers (the deal you make, such as a discount or bundle).
  • Spend at least about $50 per campaign before you judge its numbers. A campaign is the top folder that holds your ad sets. Meta needs enough spend and data to learn which audience to target.
  • A test budget of only $20 to $40 per campaign is too small to produce enough purchases to read a result. Fund a test so that several purchases can realistically happen before the decision.
  • Spend at least about $100 to test a new product. Split it across five ad sets of about $20 each. An ad set is a group of ads that share one audience and budget. This is the minimum needed to read CTR (click-through rate, the share of people who see an ad and click it). Use it to decide whether the product or creative deserves more budget.
  • For each country, spend at least $50 in total, with at least $10 on each of five ad sets, before you judge a product. This gives enough data to read CTR.
  • Set a fixed spend cap (the maximum you will spend) for each product test. Stop the product when the cap is reached without a sale. Running one product for a full month across carousel (ads with several swipeable images), image and video creatives with no sale wastes far more budget than the EUR 50 kill threshold (the spend level where you stop) used in EU general-store testing.
  • For a product test that spends about 225 AUD (around 150 USD) with zero or one sale and a low CTR, review the ad rather than funding it further.
  • Test budget per product: around 200 to 300 USD when CTR is good. Stop earlier, around 100 USD, when CTR is poor. If there are still no sales after 200 to 300 USD, stop testing the product.
  • Daily testing budgets ranged from $50 to $150, and sometimes reached $200 while many interests were being tested. An interest is a topic a person likes, which Meta uses to find an audience.
  • A daily budget of $50 suits early testing. It runs over a full week and gives roughly four weeks of data, rather than spending everything in three days. Increase the budget only when cash flow (the timing of money coming in and going out) allows it.
  • Use a daily ad budget of about $100 or more only with daily checks on creative, audience and trend. CBO (campaign budget, where one budget covers the whole campaign and Meta splits it across ad sets) campaigns at $100 or more per day need this. Testing ad sets at $5 to $10 per day is fine for the early phase.
  • Interest test ad sets run as ABO (ad set budget, where you set the budget on each ad set) at 10 EUR per day, with 50 EUR as the overall test budget.
  • A single country with four ads needs at least about $10 per day for a useful test over several days. Five dollars per ad set per day is likely too little for five ads in five countries.
  • Example budget: $8 per day per interest (about $10 USD), split across multiple interests, with a total daily spend limit of $50.
  • Spreading a small budget across many ad sets leaves too few clicks and conversions (completed sales) per market to judge. For example, five sets at about 10 EUR each for roughly 200 EUR in total is too thin.
  • Reframe ad spend as buying data, not losing money. The early ad budget is for learning what converts (what makes people buy) and whether the page can turn traffic (visitors) into sales.
  • Set a minimum spend threshold at the ad set level: spend at least your break-even cost per purchase (the most you can pay to win one sale without losing money). At the ad level, spend more to confirm the ad itself is profitable.

Campaign and ad set structure

1 min

Standard ABO product test (procedure)

1 min
  1. Create and connect the Meta pixel (tracking code that records sales) before choosing the objective (your goal in Meta), because a new account has no pixel yet.
  2. Create an ABO testing campaign. Name it with the product name, ABO or CBO, the objective and, for long runs, the start date.
  3. Choose Website as the conversion location and Purchase as the only objective. Leave Dynamic creative (Meta automatically mixes parts of your ad) off at the start.
  4. Set the ad set budget to $10 for the US, Canada, Australia and UK (5 to 10 USD range), or $5 to $7 for European and Gulf countries. Schedule the start for the next day at 12:00 AM in the ad account time zone.
  5. Target one single interest per ad set, with all genders, age 20+ or 21+, and a broad audience size (for large countries about 5 million or more). Use all languages unless the store and ads are in another language.
  6. Keep placements (the spots where your ad shows) on Advantage+ (Meta's automatic choice of spots and audiences).
  7. Add 3 to 5 creatives with ad copy (the written text), and set the destination to the product page or landing page with UTM parameters (tags added to a link that show where a click came from).
  8. Duplicate the ad set and replace only the interest until the campaign has 5 to 10 ad sets (normally 6 to 10).

Ad set count and structure rules

3 min
  • Structure each product test as one ABO testing campaign with 5 to 10 ad sets (normally 6 to 10), each holding a single interest. All other settings stay identical, so the interest is the only variable (the one thing being changed and compared). Start with 5 to 6 ad sets when budget is tight.
  • Test structure in the testing phase: about five interest audiences and three creatives per ad set.
  • Procedure for a five-ad-set product test on Meta: the first ad set is broad with no interests selected. The other four each use one specific interest. Use the Business Manager pixel (tracking code from Meta's business account tool) for tracking.
  • Starting test structure: five ad sets with three ads each, a daily budget of about $5 per ad set, run for two to four days before you analyse the data.
  • Test budget structure for a new product: start with $5 per ad set and run 5 to 6 ad sets, so spend is about $25 to $30 per day. Let each ad set run for at least 4 days.
  • Test structure for a new product (procedure):
    1. Run one campaign with five ad sets at $5 each.
    2. Send the traffic to the advertorial (a sales page written like an article).
    3. Analyse CTR, CPM and CPC per ad set after the test period. CPM is the cost per 1,000 views. CPC is the cost per click.
    4. Keep the best-performing creative and continue testing offers before judging the product.
  • Creatives: each test ad set contains 3 to 5 different creatives, and the same set of creatives is duplicated across all test ad sets.
  • The same 3 to 5 creatives are duplicated into every test ad set. This keeps the creative constant, so the interest is the only thing compared between ad sets.
  • For a first launch, build separate ad sets that each target one interest, and place 3 creatives in each ad set. Compare results per interest and creative before drawing conclusions.
  • Source D 2026 test structure: CBO with three ad sets, all creatives placed in each ad set, and a $100 daily budget. A CBO test can later be followed by an ABO version.
  • Source D 2026 initial ad test (procedure):
    1. Create one CBO campaign with a $50 daily budget.
    2. Split the campaign into 3 ad sets.
    3. Assign 4 to 5 creative variations (versions of the same creative) to each ad set.
    4. Run the ads for 48 to 72 hours.
    5. After the evaluation window (the set time for judging), assess performance on CTR, CPM, ATC (add to cart, when a customer puts an item in their basket) and conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
    6. Pause underperforming combinations and optimise based on data.
  • Keep the same interests and audiences within one product's ad assets. Separate each product into its own campaign so results stay comparable.
  • Run one ad campaign per platform, per store, per time period. Mixing traffic from several platforms makes the conversion rate in Shopify (the online store software) impossible to analyse.
  • Test one pixel per product. Each product should have its own product page and website URL in its own ad.
  • Run ads to a collection page (a page that lists several products) instead of a single product page, so the customer can choose a product. Test which products win, then manually move the winning products to the top of the collection.
  • Facebook test structure for a product (procedure):
    1. First, a control ad (the current ad, used as the baseline) with the existing setup.
    2. Second, a single change tested as a separate ad in the same ad set, or a new ad set with identical interest, copy and hook (the opening line or image that stops people scrolling).
    3. Third, compare the lift (the gain) in sales and metrics between the two.
  • Test a new product with interest targeting first. Once it reaches roughly 10 to 20 to 30 sales, add an Advantage+ broad campaign (a wide audience with little narrowing, where Meta does most of the targeting) alongside it.
  • Results differ by product and account. One product flopped on Advantage+ from the start. Another worked alongside interest targeting. A broad campaign with 20 ad sets gave only okay results, while interest targeting performed better. Test both approaches with the budget you have.

Marketing angle tests

1 min
  • Targeting test with marketing angles (the reason a buyer should care, such as comfort or saving money) (procedure):
    1. Pick 5 different marketing angles.
    2. Choose 10 interests for each angle.
    3. Create one ad set per interest, which gives 50 ad sets.
    4. Write a separate ad copy and headline for each angle, and reuse the same video if the angle is covered in it.
    5. This setup (written as "500 campaign setup" in the source, most likely meaning the 50 ad sets above) needs a total budget of about $200 to $250 per day.
  • For a smaller budget, test 5 to 15 different interests on Facebook. Place all marketing angles on the landing page, so visitors who relate to one angle get an emotional trigger (a feeling-based push to act) there.
  • Split test marketing angles for one product (procedure). A split test compares two versions by changing one thing.
    1. Identify several marketing angles for the same product.
    2. Create one campaign per angle, each with interests that match that audience.
    3. Duplicate the product page once per angle and change the images, description and matching reviews.
    4. Link each campaign to its matching page and run them at the same time.
    5. Compare results, close the weaker pages and continue with the strongest angle.

Interest and audience testing

2 min
  • Test around 20 to 30 interests in ad sets to find the few winners. Each ad set is given roughly $20 to $30 of spend. If it brings no sales after that, close it and replace it with a new interest.
  • Test at least 12 to 15 different interests before judging a product. If you have not found 2 to 3 profitable audiences after 12 to 15 tests, change the creatives and test 3 to 5 more interests. If the product still fails, switch to another product.
  • Testing 10 to 15 audiences gives a product enough chance. Audience, landing page, videos or creators (people who make the content) and the product itself all affect results. Judging after one audience is unreliable.
  • Each interest needs roughly one to two break-even spends before there is enough data to judge it. Testing five or six interests one after the other can take weeks. That is why faster budget choices help.
  • Interest testing workflow (procedure):
    1. Before starting, set your own rules for when an interest counts as a winner, for example a number of sales and profitability (making more than you spend).
    2. Start each interest at a daily budget of about $5 for products under about $30, or higher if you want data faster.
    3. Let each interest run without budget increases until it reaches your sales threshold (the sales number you set as the goal).
    4. Once an interest reaches the threshold and is profitable, increase its budget to scale it (spend more on what works).
    5. Shut down interests that do not reach the threshold.
  • Do not increase the budget of an interest while it is still being tested. Scale an interest only after it reaches the sales threshold you set in advance, for example six to seven sales, and is profitable.
  • Once 2 to 3 ad sets are profitable, find similar winning audiences by testing related interests, including interests Facebook suggests.
  • Test rule for interests: do not assume that a method working in one ad account will work in another, because every ad account differs. Test everything in your own account before scaling.
  • Split test interests, mobile, desktop, operating system, gender and age brackets as much as you can, because the winning combination is not predictable. Use a split test tool (software that compares versions) when you spend around $2,000 a month or more on Facebook.
  • Split test toggle (a switch in Meta's setup screen): it appears only when creating a new campaign. Choose creative as the variable, upload the two creatives, and Meta shows the budget and days needed to declare a winner.

Budget per ad set

1 min

Minimum and standard ad set budgets

2 min
  • Give each test ad set at least $10 per day, and let it spend for 3 days or until it reaches its break-even cost per purchase (BECPP). For mid-high ticket products (higher-priced items) priced at $90 or more, start each ad set at $15 per day instead.
  • Start testing with a budget of $5 to $10 per ad set, then check the break-even ROAS (the return you need to avoid losing money). Products are not given a fixed budget in advance. If a product is not profitable, switch to the next product.
  • Start Facebook ads with about $5 to $10 per ad set. Scale spend (spend more on what works) only after results show that the audience responds.
  • Test budgets at about $5 per day per ad set during the testing phase. Move an ad set into scaling only after it has produced around four purchases.
  • A new product should start with a small test budget per ad set.
  • Starting ad set budget per day: $10 for the US, Canada, Australia and UK, where CPCs (cost per click) are higher. Use $5 (or $5 to $7) for European and Gulf countries, where clicks are cheaper.
  • A $5 budget may need 3 to 5 days to gather enough pixel data. A $10 budget gathers it faster. In cheaper countries such as Germany or France, $5 to $7 can still gather enough data.
  • Daily budget per ad set during testing, based on selling price: under $30 = $5; $30 to $60 = $10; $60 or above = $15. Higher-priced products have a higher cost per acquisition (the cost to win one customer), so each ad set needs more budget to win one customer.
  • Example: a product priced at $34.99 falls in the $10 tier. Five ad sets at $10 per day means the testing campaign spends at most $50 per day.
  • For audience testing of products priced under about $30, start each interest with a daily budget of about $5. This is a guideline, not a strict rule.
  • A higher daily budget, such as $10 to $15, gets data faster, which is the main goal of interest testing. For cheap products, $5 per day may still be preferable. It spreads data over several days and avoids judging a product on one unusually bad day.
  • Testing ad sets at $5 to $10 per day is fine for the early phase.

Why budgets are sized this way

2 min
  • A higher ad set budget sends more data to a new pixel. This can produce early sales and a faster read on whether an audience works.
  • A higher ad set budget feeds more data to a new pixel, which can produce early sales sooner. This makes the early read on an ad set more reliable.
  • Higher-priced products need more audience reach (how many people see the ad) and more spend to produce one sale. So the daily budget should match the product price. To test fast, set each ad set's daily budget at the BECPP or slightly above it, so some ad sets can produce an early sale as a positive signal.
  • For higher-priced products that need more reach to convert, setting the daily ad set budget at the BECPP or slightly above speeds up testing. An early sale is a good sign.
  • Starting with a high daily budget on a new Meta campaign spends money before the pixel and audience have enough data to optimise (improve by learning). Begin with a low daily budget per campaign. Raise it only after results show which audiences perform.
  • Daily test spend equals the number of ad sets × the daily budget per ad set. For example, 5 ad sets at $5 each means $25 per day. Running them for 2 to 4 days gives enough data to decide.
  • Low-budget product testing (procedure):
    1. Launch the new product with a low daily budget, for example about $10 per day.
    2. Check whether the ads gain traction (people start to show interest).
    3. If they do not, move to the next product.
    4. If they do, test different offers before deciding to scale.
  • To find a winning Facebook ad with a small budget (procedure):
    1. Start each new ad with a small daily budget, for example from 5 pounds per day.
    2. Increase the budget in small steps while watching which creative gets results quickly.
    3. Scale only the creative that performs, and stop the rest early.

Break-even rules and formulas

1 min

Break-even cost per purchase (BECPP)

2 min
  • Break-even cost per purchase is the threshold for an ad set. Spend at least that amount before you judge it.
  • Let an ad spend up to the break-even cost per purchase, for example the full profit per sale, such as 17 USD, before you judge it.
  • Spend up to the profit margin (what you keep from each sale) per ad before deciding. If an ad is still unprofitable at three times the target cost per acquisition (the cost to win one customer), shut it off.
  • Kill (switch off) an ad set in testing once it has spent the break-even cost per purchase without a single sale.
  • Testing budget per ad set from the desired CPA: spend three times the desired CPA on each ad before deciding to turn it off or scale it. In the example, a product costing $15 that sells for $45 targets a CPA of about $15, so each ad is tested with $45 spend. Daily ad budgets for testing in the example ranged from $10 to $50 per ad.
  • Testing budget per ad set = 3 × desired CPA. Desired CPA is roughly the amount the business can afford to pay for one customer, based on the margin. With a product cost of $15 and sale price of $45, the margin supports a CPA of about $15. Example: $15 CPA × 3 = $45 spend per ad before judging it.
  • Before deciding to switch off an ad, let it spend up to the target CPA. For example, if the desired CPA is 15 units and ROAS shows 30 percent margin, spend about three times that before deciding. Then shut the ad off if it does not work.
  • Set the target CPA at about half the profit margin per order. Example: with a $30 margin, aim for $15 per customer, and cut unprofitable ads once they reach about $45 spent.
  • Check the ad-level spend. If an ad has spent around $8 to $10 or more, or about 1,000 to 1,500 impressions (times the ad was shown), and shows poor CPC and CTR, it is taking budget from other ads. Turn it off.

Break-even ROAS

1 min
  • Break-even ROAS = selling price divided by gross profit per sale, where gross profit = selling price minus cost of goods sold (COGS, what the product costs you) and fulfilment costs (packing and shipping). Example: a $30 flower pot with $11 COGS and a price of $29.9 gives a break-even ROAS of 1.6.
  • The break-even ROAS for a given product is set by its margin. A product with a $6 cost sold at about $7 allowed a break-even of about 1.1, while another brand's break-even was 1.5. Calculate break-even ROAS before deciding whether an ad with a given ROAS is profitable.
  • Break-even ROAS and the scaling conversion goal: a conversion goal (your target number of sales) for a new interest or ad set is at least four to five sales within the next couple of days. The return on ad spend should be the break-even ROAS plus 1.

Kill and keep rules

1 min

When to kill an ad set or interest

3 min
  • Cut an ad set that has not produced a sale after spending its break-even amount. If the ad set shows promising add-to-carts or initiate checkouts (the customer starts checkout), or a good CTR, let it run one more day before deciding.
  • Use this procedure to decide when to turn off an ad set:
    1. Let the ad set spend an amount equal to the cost of goods of the product (for example, $20 for a product costing $20).
    2. Analyse the CTR of the ad set.
    3. Check whether it brought a sale and whether the return on ad spend (ROAS, money earned per dollar spent on ads) is good.
    4. If the sale exists and the ROAS is good, keep the ad set running.
    5. If not, turn it off and replace it with another interest or audience.
  • After a test in which ABO has lost money, test a new interest in CBO with a $100 budget. Cut it if it has no add to cart after about $15 to $20 spent (for a $40 product with a $25 margin). Also cut it if it has no sale after $40 spent.
  • Judge an ad set after roughly $20 to $30 of spend. Pausing it earlier is premature impatience. Once it has passed that spend and still performs poorly, pause it and duplicate the best performing ad set instead.
  • Spend roughly $20 to $30 on an ad set before judging it. That amount shows whether the CPA is affordable against the break-even point. Pausing an ad set before it reaches that spend usually means judging it too early.
  • Another operator (an advertiser) used a stricter cut-off of about $10 to $15 of spend for a new ad that had not converted, and paused it at that point. This is a faster test than the $20 to $30 guideline. It fits a tight starting budget.
  • Wait about four days after publishing, so each ad set spends around $20. Then analyse the results. Keep ad sets running only if they meet the thresholds for CTR and cost.
  • Close an ad set in a test if it has spent $10 and its CTR is below 1.5%. If an ad has spent only around $0.30, leave it running because there is too little data.
  • An ad with a CTR around 1% after more than $10 of spend is a weak signal and can be paused. An ad with a clearly higher CTR and low spend (around $4) can be kept running, because the spend is still too small for a verdict.
  • Ads with spend of $20 to $30 and no sales can be closed. If all ads produce sales, keep them running together in the same ad set.
  • Remove a creative only when it performs badly in a consistent way across all ad sets. If results are mixed, keep it and retest it before deleting it.
  • Ads that show sales stay in place.
  • Keep ad sets that are profitable, close ad sets that are not, and test a different interest.
  • Profitability cannot be expected immediately. Finding the right audience, the right ad and the right offer on the website usually takes time before an ad set becomes profitable.
  • If CTR stays at or below 0.3 to 0.5 after about $100 in spend, replace the creative. If CTR stays very low after replacing it, stop the product earlier.
  • A winning product shows initial traction within the first week of running Facebook ads. Early positive signals include audience engagement with the creative, website visits, and actions such as adding to cart or saving to a wishlist. These signs that the audience connects with the product justify continuing to test that product-audience combination.
  • Dropshipping (selling products you do not stock, where the supplier ships them) is about testing rather than overthinking. Launch the product, gather data and decide based on the results. If the data shows good CTR and low CPC, the product is worth more investment. If not, move on.

Kill when no add to cart or no sale (combined with rules 1 to 4)

1 min
  • Kill an ad set if it has made no sale after testing, but apply Rules 1 to 4 first. Details are in the Rules section below.
  • Test procedure step: after spending another BECPP, check whether ROAS is above BE-ROAS. If yes, let it run. If no, kill it.

Kill and reset rules for ads and products

1 min
  • Judging a new product's ads after a day or two is premature, and it wastes money on losing ads. Cutting an ad that has spent beyond its profit margin without a sale is the usual decision, but check the margin first.
  • Judging an ad as a failure after $10 to $15 of spend with no add-to-carts is premature. First check whether the offer and product page are attractive enough. Test different offers and bundles (products sold together) before turning ads off.
  • Judging a new ad after a few hours on a $5 daily budget is premature. Small budgets need a day or more before the data shows what works. Facebook normally spends well, so give it time before cutting.
  • Do not judge a Facebook ad or ad set before it has spent roughly $20 to $30.
  • Remove an ad set in testing when it spends beyond break-even without a sale, unless it shows add-to-carts or CTR signals.
  • Pushing a product at audiences who do not respond to it leads ad platforms to penalise the advertiser's content. Founders who rely on their own intuition that a product is great, instead of letting the market react, often run into this.
  • Running ads in a market with strong competition is normal. Some advertisers scale a product in a market, while others run only a single ad for it. A single ad in a market does not prove the product fails there.
  • Cutting ad creatives and age groups too quickly after a few good sales can make CTR and sales drop sharply. Changes to a working test need careful evaluation, not sudden large cuts.
  • Test a product for at least the spend set out in its budget before deciding. Do not replace a product after one bad day.

Rules 1 to 4 and safety rule

4 min

These are the Source C decision rules for an ad set (a group of ads with one audience and one budget) after launch. Apply them in order.

  1. Rule 1: Let each ad set spend its break-even cost per purchase (BECPP: the most you can pay for one sale and still break even) or run for three days, whichever comes later. Do not make changes to improve it before then.

    • For products with margins under $20 (margin: the profit you keep from each sale after costs), use the three-day minimum to gather enough data.

  2. Rule 2: After Rule 1 is complete, check whether the ad set made at least one sale.

    • If yes, let it keep spending another full break-even cost per purchase.
    • If there is no sale, check the CPC (cost per click: what you pay for each click on the ad).
    • If CPC is below $1.50, extend the ad set one more day.
    • If CPC is $1.50 or above, deactivate it (switch it off) and replace it with a new interest (a topic your audience likes).

  3. Replacement procedure: To replace an underperforming ad set (one that is not earning) while testing new interests:

    1. Find the ad set that achieved at least one sale. This is the winning ad set.
    2. Duplicate (copy) that winning ad set.
    3. Open the Audience section and click the "Suggested" button. This shows interest suggestions based on the current audience.
    4. Choose a new suggested interest. For example, if the current interest is "Carpet," try "Living Room" or a related suggestion.
    5. Check that the audience size is reasonable. For example, 100+ million for broad targeting.
    6. Click Publish to make the new ad set go live.
    7. Deactivate the old underperforming ad set.
    8. Apply all rules starting from Rule 1 to this newly created ad set.

  4. Rule 3: If an ad set survived Rule 2 with a one-day extension but made no sale in that 24-hour period, deactivate it. Replace it using the same duplication and interest-suggestion method described in Rule 2. Start the new ad set through Rule 1 again.

  5. Rule 4: Once an ad set clears Rules 2 and 3, let it spend an additional amount equal to double the break-even cost per purchase.

    • This means double the initial threshold, not double the total.
    • By the time that extra amount is spent, the ad set must reach a return on ad spend (ROAS: sales money earned per dollar spent on ads) equal to or higher than its break-even ROAS (the ROAS where you neither gain nor lose money).
    • If it falls below break-even ROAS at that point, deactivate it and replace it.

Safety rule:

  • If, after three to four days of ads, the whole campaign has zero sales across all ad sets, stop the campaign immediately.
  • Do not keep spending in hope that sales improve on their own.
  • Go to troubleshooting instead.

Safety rule for a campaign with no sales: pause the campaign immediately if no sale happens within the first $50 of spend.

Reduce, do not kill outright: If results are consistently poor for the last 4 days, reduce the budget (the money set aside for the campaign). Test a reduced version before pausing completely.

First-day rule for high-budget campaigns: Pause the campaign for the day if it spends twice the break-even cost per purchase without a sale. CPA (cost per acquisition: what you pay for each sale) is the same idea as cost per purchase.

  • For example, with a break-even CPA of $30 and a $100 launch budget, turn it off once it has spent $60 without a purchase.

Sources disagree on the first-day stop

  • Sources disagree about stopping on day one.
  • Source D 2026 says do not cut a product after one day or after only $50 without sales, because Meta (the company behind Facebook and Instagram ads) needs time to test segments (groups of people).
  • The Source C safety rule stops a campaign with no sale within $50 of spend.
  • The first rule is about deciding whether to cut a product. The second is about a campaign running with no sales.
  • For a high-budget campaign, the 2x break-even stop at applies.
  • The Source D case in which day one looked like failure and became profitable on day three shows why a day-one cut can be wrong.

Patience against day-one panic

  • A new Meta test with a high cost per purchase on day one should not be switched off right away.
  • One day of spend and one sale is too little data to judge a product.
  • Leave the test running for several days and read the trend (the direction the numbers move over time).
  • Cutting a test after the first day with a high CPA, then moving on to the next product, is called paper handing (giving up on a product too early). It often kills products that would have become profitable.
  • Judging a product on the first day alone is the main reason good tests get lost.

Scaling thresholds

1 min
  • Scale an ad set (increase its budget because it works) once it has produced around four purchases.
  • Scale an interest only after it reaches the sales threshold set in advance (for example six to seven sales) and is profitable.
  • If any running ad set brings 4 or more profitable sales, scale it.
  • Once a sale comes in, spend up to the break-even cost per purchase again.
  • Example of a scaling outcome:
    • A new product runs five ad sets at about $12 each.
    • Three are switched off for weak performance.
    • The other two keep running until they reach seven profitable sales, which can take about one to one and a half weeks.
  • When scaling into CBO (campaign budget optimisation: Facebook spreads one campaign budget across its ad sets), look for at least three purchases on the ad set before trusting the result.
  • Test a new product with interest targeting first (showing ads to people who like related topics).
  • Once it reaches roughly 10 to 20 to 30 sales, add an Advantage+ broad campaign (a wide campaign where Facebook chooses the audience) alongside it.
  • Once a sale starts, the ads work: find the winning interest, country and offer. An offer is the deal you make to the buyer, such as price or bundle.
  • Keep ad sets with sales running, and put the budget behind the country that performs best rather than spreading it evenly.
  • Scale only the creative (the ad image or video) that performs, and stop the rest early.

Day-of-week and schedule

  • Product-testing campaigns are commonly run from Thursday to Sunday. Running them Monday to Wednesday is acceptable too, as long as the data is broken down afterwards by country, age group and gender (if several genders are tested).
  • Do not assume that weekends or Thursday to Sunday are always the best days to test audiences.
  • Day-of-week patterns differed strongly between months and products, so check your own data over a longer period before using them.
  • Spread a test budget across every day of the week rather than a short burst.
  • A three-day run misses buyers who are paid on a particular day, such as Thursday, and gives weaker data.
  • Schedule the start for the next day at 12:00 AM in the ad account (the account that holds your ads and payment) time zone.

Editing during the test window

  • Once a test campaign is running and the store, creatives and product page are ready, do not edit the campaign during the test window.
  • In the example, leaving every setting untouched for two days brought CPA down sharply on its own.
  • Source D day checks (procedure):
    1. Launch a CBO test with three ad sets at a $100 daily budget, with all creatives placed in each ad set.
    2. Make no edits to the campaign for three days.
    3. Compare each day's CPA against the break-even CPA for the product.
    4. Once the campaign produces purchases below break-even, scale it (the scaling method was not covered in the source).
  • CBO test rhythm:
    • Let the test run for two days.
    • Check the metrics and P&L (profit and loss statement) on days 1 and 2.
    • On day 3, scale only if the product is green (its numbers are good) and the margins are good.
    • Keep the same CBO and duplicate it with new creatives on day 3, instead of restarting.
  • Changing audience, placement (where the ad appears, such as the feed or stories) or other ad set settings during a product test muddles the results.
  • Keep default settings unless a legal or practical constraint requires a change.
  • Keep ad sets under review by checking daily data on creative, audience and trend when a CBO runs at $100 or more per day.

Product rhythm

  • Run product tests in a repeating loop: test a product, give it enough time and budget to read the result, and only then move on to the next product.
  • Do not replace a product after one bad day.
  • Testing a product: products are not given a fixed budget in advance. If a product is not profitable, switch to the next product.
  • If no ad set brings any sale after testing at least 10 interests, switch to the next product.

Store versus ad diagnosis

5 min
  • The job of the ads is to drive traffic (visitors to the store). The job of the store is to make the sale.
  • If traffic arrives but there are no sales, change the store (design, offer, bundle, pricing or copy, meaning the written words) before changing the ads.
  • Before launching a test, make sure the store has a branded product page (a product page that looks like your own brand) and updated, good-looking creatives.
  • A weak store or old creatives make the test result less reliable.
  • Testing procedure with checkpoints (procedure):
    1. Set a test budget of $300 per product and split it into blocks of $100.
    2. Spend the first $100, then check CPM, CTR and CPC in Ads Manager (Facebook's ad dashboard).
    3. Check the store data after the first $100. If ad metrics are good but there are no sales or add-to-carts, the problem is the store. Change the theme, price, offer or copywriting and spend another $100.
    4. If the ads have no good metrics, change the audience or the creatives and test again with the next $100.
    5. Once sales start, the ads work: find the winning interest, country and offer.
    6. If the store is changed and there are still no sales after the tweaks, and the offer seems good, move on to the next product.
  • Spending the full $300 on a product in one go, without a checkpoint, makes it impossible to tell whether the ads or the store are failing. Split the budget into $100 blocks and check the data after each block.
  • Ad account and payment check (procedure):
    1. Check the ad account and payment setup after buying an existing Facebook account, especially if it had a restriction.
    2. If spend stays far below the budget, duplicate the campaign and run it again.
    3. Let each ad set run about 4 days until it has about $20 spent.
    4. Judge the ads by CTR and CPC first.
    5. If the ad metrics are good but there are no sales, change the store.
    6. If there are no add-to-carts, first test the offer and bundles before concluding the ads fail.
  • Debugging weak results in new international markets (procedure):
    1. Check the market settings and shipping settings for each market.
    2. Place a test order through the checkout (the steps where a customer pays), using two random addresses from each country, to confirm it works end to end.
    3. Break down the ad results by country in the ads dashboard (age and gender are also possible breakdowns).
    4. Find the country that received most of the spend but produced no sales, and pause it.
    5. Test the remaining countries, ideally one at a time, and check the results again.
    6. Check the store for broken buttons and wrong language text.
    7. Review the full funnel, including add to cart and checkout steps, to find where visitors drop off.
  • A market that looks dead after a small spend may just lack data, so split tests by country in a way that gives each one enough spend.
  • Troubleshoot a campaign with zero clicks before building broader audiences.
  • Do not start top-of-funnel Facebook ads (ads aimed at people who don't know you yet) while a campaign is getting zero clicks. Troubleshoot that campaign first, or turn it off and restart.
  • Ad campaign with no clicks: a campaign targeting Australia spent 143 USD in total.
  • Dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships straight to the customer) rests on the funnel: ads showed CTR of 3.1 to 5.45 percent, but there were zero add-to-carts and zero Shopify analytics (Shopify is the store platform).
  • Check the store and the offer after strong CTR without sales, using the funnel review step.

Case: cheap clicks, no add to cart (complete)

  • A student ran a Meta campaign that targeted Australia only. They spent 143 USD in total.
  • CTR ranged from 3.1 to 5.45 percent across the ads.
  • CPC ranged from 0.60 to 1.15 USD, with an average of 0.67 USD.
  • The campaign produced zero add-to-carts.
  • Shopify analytics also showed zero, so the pixel (a tracking code that reports store activity) alone was not the likely cause.
  • The advice: stop the campaign, review the funnel with the mentor, and look for a new product. Cheap clicks without add-to-carts show weak interest in the product or offer.

Case: dominant creative and suspected tracking fault (complete)

  • A student ran a Meta campaign for three days, spending 150 EUR across five ad sets until the spend reached break-even.
  • Most of the budget went to one video creative, and Meta kept sending the spend to it.
  • The store's general conversion rate was about 1 to 1.1 percent.
  • The campaign produced 91 clicks at a CPC of 1.76 EUR and a CTR of 1.08 percent, but only one add-to-cart.
  • The student stopped the ads and cut the dominant creative (the one taking most of the spend) from the ad sets.
  • The mentor suspected a tracking fault, a funnel problem, or an outdated product.
  • The mentor noted that influencer traffic had produced more add-to-carts and at least one purchase.

Case: day one failure that became profitable (complete)

  • Starting point: a new dropshipping product was tested with a CBO campaign, with three ad sets.
  • All creatives were placed in each ad set. The daily budget was $100. The store had a branded product page with updated creatives.
  • Day 1: the campaign spent about $104.49 and produced one purchase, a CPA of $104.49. That would normally lead to switching it off.
  • Day 2: nothing was edited. CPA fell to about a quarter of day one (around $26).
  • Day 3: nothing was edited again. The campaign produced about $1,000 in revenue at about $15 CPA, with more purchases than Facebook reported.
  • Day 4 morning: CPA started at about $4.
  • Break-even CPA was around $20 to $21, with a $30 selling price and a $10 product cost.
  • Result: the test looked like a failure on day one but became profitable by leaving it running. The winning campaign was then scaled.
  • The speaker estimated roughly $200 profit on day three.
  • These figures are as stated in the source.

Testing variables and order

2 min
  • When testing, fix the variables (the things you change in a test) in this order:
    1. The offer and the website at the end of the funnel,
    2. The audience,
    3. and only then vary the creative.
  • Otherwise a weak result cannot be traced to a single cause.
  • Facebook test structure (procedure): see the standard ABO product test above. ABO (ad set budgets: each ad set has its own budget) is the setup used there.
  • All settings identical except the interest.
  • Isolation rule for ads: when Facebook favours one ad over others in an ad set, initially trust Facebook's choice.
  • If you want to test underperforming ads, isolate them in a separate ad set and force spend into them for 3 days to see if they perform better in isolation.
  • Ad testing procedure in an ad set (procedure):
    1. Create 3 ads in one interest ad set.
    2. Run for 3 days minimum at break-even cost per purchase spend level.
    3. Review which ad Facebook favours by looking at spend and CTR.
    4. If one ad significantly underperforms but other metrics are promising, isolate it in a separate ad set and force spend into it to test further.
    5. If metrics across the ad set look good overall (good attention and interest), proceed to creative review and hypothesis testing (a guess you test with data).
    6. Do 2 to 3 rounds of testing iterations (repeated test cycles) minimum before killing a product (dropping it for good).
  • Ad testing with an isolated ad: a per-ad test with high spend in one set needs a separate ad set to read the ad itself.
  • Split test (an A/B test: two versions compared) interests, mobile, desktop, operating system, gender and age brackets as much as you can.
  • Start new campaigns with a low budget and one variable at a time, choosing creative as the variable when creating the test.
  • Test at least 3 to 5 ad sets per product before judging.
  • Keep the same creatives across ad sets so the interest is the only variable.
  • Do not test many variables at once. Change one thing per ad, keep copy and hook (the first few seconds or opening line that grabs attention) identical, and compare the lift (the extra gain).
  • Source D 2026 product rule: test one pixel per product. If a product in the test fails several times in a row, move the successful ad set to a CBO and turn off the failing ad set.

Country and market tests

1 min
  • Procedure for a multi-country market test (testing one product in several countries):
    1. Launch the product in about five countries, including the countries where the competitor runs ads. A competitor is a rival store selling similar products.
    2. Break down ad results by country.
    3. Compare CTR (click-through rate: how many people who see the ad click it), CPC (cost per click: what one click costs), CPM (cost per 1,000 views: what 1,000 views of the ad cost) and conversion rate (how many visitors buy) for each country.
    4. Narrow the test to the countries with the best results.
  • For each country, spend at least $50 in total, with at least $10 on each of five ad sets, before judging a product. An ad set is one group of ads with its own audience and budget settings. This gives enough data to read CTR.
  • Put the budget behind the best country instead of spreading it evenly. Budget is the money you set aside for ads.
  • Split test (compare groups side by side) by country, age group and gender after a test campaign has run. A test campaign is your first round of ads, run to learn something.

Testing pitfalls

2 min
  • Do not cut (stop) a product after a single day, or after spending only $50 without sales. Meta (the company behind Facebook and Instagram ads) needs time to test different audience segments (smaller parts of your target group) and show results. The audience is the group of people your ad is shown to.
  • Judging a new ad after a few hours on a $5 daily budget is premature. A daily budget is the most you spend per day.
  • Cutting ad creatives (the images, videos or text of an ad) and age groups too quickly after a few good sales can cause CTR and sales to drop sharply.
  • Assuming weekends are always the best days to test.
  • Removing a creative after mixed results, rather than retesting it.
  • Running a product for a full month across carousel (an ad with several swipeable images), image and video creatives with no sale, rather than setting a fixed cap (a fixed limit on spend).
  • Spreading a small budget across many ad sets.
  • Pushing a product at audiences who do not respond.
  • Spending the full $300 on a product in one go, without a checkpoint (a planned pause to review results).
  • Changing audience, placement (where the ad appears, such as feeds or stories) or ad set settings during a product test.
  • Assuming a method working in one ad account (your Meta advertising account) works in another.
  • Judging an ad as a failure after $10 to $15 spend with no add-to-carts (a visitor putting an item in their basket).
  • Cutting a test after the first day with a high CPA (cost per acquisition: what one sale costs). This is called paper handing, which means quitting too early.
  • Judging a product on the first day alone.
  • Judging a product after one bad day, and not giving a product the spend it needs to show its result.
  • Judging an interest (a topic people follow, used to pick an audience), ad or ad set before its break-even spend. Break-even spend is the amount spent when the ad has earned back its cost, with zero profit.
  • Reacting to single-day results.
  • Running ads with no measurable goal (a goal you can count), for example mixing multiple platforms into one store.
  • Under-funding (giving too little money to) a test with $20 to $40 per campaign.
  • Scaling (spending more on a winning product) before the threshold of sales is reached. The threshold is the minimum number of sales needed.
  • A mixed approach where results are judged before the ad has spent enough to read a result, while also not stopping when the spend passes break-even with no promising signal.
  • Judging a new product's ads after a day or two, which wastes money on losing ads.
  • Using a product's ads as proof that the product fails in a market when only one ad was run there.

Product-level testing principles

1 min
  • Test, don't overthink. Dropshipping (selling products you do not stock, where the supplier ships them to the customer) is about testing rather than overthinking. Launch the product, gather data and decide based on the results.
  • Profitability (earning more than you spend) cannot be expected immediately.
  • Test a product for enough spend before moving on, and keep a product whose metrics (the numbers that measure results) show that its audience responds.
  • Products are not given a fixed budget in advance.
  • Results from a test can be read by country, age and gender once spend is split.

Before you spend: store and supplier checks

1 min
  • The landing page (the first page a visitor sees after clicking an ad) must deliver what the Facebook ad promises. If a visitor clicks an ad for one product and the homepage (the front page of the store) shows unrelated products, the visitor loses trust and leaves.
  • Cut ad spend on products from a supplier who does not process orders at once. A supplier is the company that sends the products to your customers. Spending to acquire customers for products you cannot ship only wastes budget.

Diagnosis order: ad metrics before store conversion

1 min
  • When checking Meta ads, judge CTR, CPC and CPM first, and only then the store conversion (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Record results daily, including trends (the direction numbers move over time) and patterns by day of week and time of day, even when sales are flat (not changing).

Killing (stopping) underperforming creatives (ad level)

1 min
  1. Compare each ad's CPC and CTR with your account averages (example: average CPC $0.90, average CTR 1.3%). Your account averages are the typical numbers across all your ads.
  2. Wait until the ad has spent about $8 or more.
  3. If the ad shows clearly worse data, such as a CPC of about $3 and a CTR of 0.3% to 0.5%, turn it off.
  4. Keep the other creatives in the ad set running so they get budget.

Killing (stopping) underperforming ad sets

1 min
  • Cut an ad set after two days if it has spent more than the breakeven cost per purchase without a sale, and replace it with a new audience. The breakeven cost per purchase is the most one sale can cost before you lose money.

Testing pitfalls: store review

1 min
  • A typical beginner mistake is launching ads to an unreviewed store and spending money, such as $200 with no sales, before knowing whether the product page (the page that sells one item), offer or checkout (the payment steps) is the cause.

Further details

1 min
  • The testing phase is built to find winners quickly while not wasting much money or time forcing an ad set to work. Clear kill rules (set rules for when to stop an ad), applied on schedule, are the main protection against overspending on weak audiences or creatives.
