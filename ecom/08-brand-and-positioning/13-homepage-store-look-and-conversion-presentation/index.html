Playbook › 8. Brand and positioning

Homepage, store look and conversion presentation

Section 8.13 · 13 of 24 in this chapter · 12 min read

In short

  • Your top line must say what the buyer gets, not just what you sell.
  • Use one look everywhere: same colors, same font, same style.
  • Show trust points near the top: where the product comes from, a guarantee, reviews.
  • Use real customer photos. They match what visitors just saw in short videos.
  • Most visitors use phones and decide fast, so make the top image convince at once.

Homepage headline and hero (headline: the big line of text at the top; hero: the large top section with the main picture)

3 min
  • The first thing a visitor reads should say what the store does for them, or what result they get.
  • It should not only say what the store sells.
  • People buy a product to solve a problem or reach a goal.
  • So the message should name that outcome.
  • The homepage headline should state the business's primary promise.
  • When the wording changes, for example for a new product launch, the core promise stays the same.
  • Headline pattern: name the result the customer wants, then the specific worry the store removes.
  • Keep it to one short line.
  • The visitor sees their own goal first. So the page is about them, not the store.
    • "Keep your gear dry on every trip, with a seal that never leaks."
    • "Sleep through the night on a mattress that keeps your back supported."
  • Show your USP (unique selling proposition: the one thing that makes your store different) in the top line or header (the bar across the top of every page).
  • It should be visible from the first moment.
  • Keep it short and specific.
  • It must set the store apart from competitors (other stores selling the same thing).
  • Use sales language with some personality.
  • Explain how the business serves the customer.
  • A page that only pushes sales and promotions makes the store look like a commodity (a product that looks the same as everyone else's), especially in a competitive market.
  • Match the hero image with a statement of what you help the customer achieve, such as a travel experience.
  • A sale banner placed over an aspirational image (a picture of a dream result) sends a conflicting message.
  • In the first view (the part of the page visible before scrolling), use one of these:
    • a lifestyle image (a photo of people using the product in everyday life)
    • a UGC image (user-generated content: a photo of a person holding the product, taken by a customer)
    • a high-quality product photo
  • Match the visual style to the brand shown in your ad.
  • Visitors who land from TikTok or Facebook then feel they are on a safe, recognisable store.
  • Organic short-video content (free clips, not paid ads) is a main discovery channel, a way people find stores.
  • So many visitors arrive after watching raw, creator-made content (unpolished clips made by creators).
  • Real-customer photos match what they have just seen.
  • Store name and purpose:
    • A store name should signal what the store sells.
    • If the name suggests a gift shop, the homepage, navigation (the menu links) and header must confirm gift buying.
    • Visitors judge the purpose from the name and logo first. (Older source, Source A 2019-2022 range not stated; no year given.)
    • Identify whether a visitor is buying for themselves or for someone else.
    • This changes the homepage structure and header messaging.
    • For gift stores, the recipient (him, her, mother, father, children, grandparents) should be visible at the top of the page.
  • Display the best-selling category first, so visitors immediately see what the store is known for. A category is a group of similar products.
  • Example: a jewelry store whose homepage is filled with bracelets, because bracelets are its top seller.
  • Authority markers (logos of well-known media outlets that show credibility) build trust.
  • They work best high on the homepage, where visitors look early rather than far down the page.
  • Visuals convey ideas and build a mental picture faster than text alone.
  • People process images as one of their primary senses.
  • Use imagery alongside copy (the written words) in ads, product pages and brand assets (logos, fonts and other brand files).
  • If you own a physical store, use a photo of the storefront with its signage as the homepage hero image.
  • Show products with short demo videos.
  • Order of work: focus on the offer and where it is placed, before the look of the site.
  • Improve targeting (who sees your ads) and the offer first.
  • Then adjust the presentation on the website.

USP block and trust claims

1 min
  • USP block pattern: use three to four short, bold lines, each stating one trust point (origin, safe ingredients, guarantee or reviews).
  • Each trust point gets one line of supporting detail.
  • Keep generous white space (empty space) around the block.
  • Visitors scan for a reason to trust the store. A quick scan should answer that without reading.
    • "Made in a small family workshop. Every batch is tested for purity before it ships."
    • "Free shipping on every order. Not happy? We cover the return shipping."
  • Put the trust claims that your specific buyers care about most into the USP block.
  • Examples: cruelty-free, vegan, aluminum-free, safe-ingredient or made-in-a-country statements.
  • Each claim answers an objection (a doubt the buyer has) that the buyer would otherwise have to research on their own.

Brand identity and consistent design

2 min
  • Consistency before decoration:
    • Standardize all badges and logos to one color scheme, one font family (one set of letter shapes) and one visual style before launch.
    • Badges are small trust icons or labels.
    • Even a new store should look like an established brand, not a small improvised shop.
    • Badges, icons and product images pulled from different sources look unprofessional.
    • Mixed colors, fonts and styles also lower trust.
    • Visitors judge this within seconds of arriving.
    • A consistent visual system signals that real money went into the business.
    • Large established retailers with one color scheme and one font system are a practical visual benchmark (a standard to copy).
    • Matching that level of consistency makes a small store look like a serious business from its first visit.
    • Weak branding makes a store look untrustworthy. Examples: an amateur logo, clashing color combinations, mismatched fonts, or an unchanged default theme (the template's built-in look).
    • Inconsistent design across pages signals that the store may not be legitimate.
    • A consistent, distinctive design that competitors do not copy makes a brand recognisable at a glance.
    • That recognisability adds to perceived uniqueness.
    • Choose one visual signature (one look that belongs only to you) and apply it everywhere the buyer sees the brand.
  • Theme and sections:
    • Each homepage section with a Color scheme option takes its colours from the palette (the set of colors) in Theme settings > Colors (the store's color settings menu).
    • Sections with this option are: slideshow (rotating images), collection list (groups of products), featured collection (one highlighted group of products), email signup (a box to join your mailing list), and pre-footer (the strip just above the footer).
    • Changing a section's scheme recolours only that section.
    • This keeps the whole store consistent.
    • Test the logo, fonts and colors on the live product page (the page real buyers see) before finalizing.
    • Brand fonts and colors usually need trial and error.
    • Expect to revisit the branding once the product images and copy are in place.
  • Voice and copy:
    • The hero and feature copy should match the brand's own voice (its tone of speech) and niche (the small market it sells into).
    • It should not copy the template's default category.
    • When a template was written for another niche, rewrite headlines and body copy so each sentence fits the product's actual use.
    • When mocking up a layout (making a rough preview), use neutral placeholder text such as lorem ipsum (dummy filler text).
    • Real words on a theme can push the look of the design toward that topic and distort the choices.
  • Conflict: badge colours:
    • says to standardize all badges to one color scheme.
    • says to use the brand colour and logo consistently across the header, footer and rating badges (review-score labels).
    • But where a third-party badge (a logo from another company) is shown, use that company's official colour so it looks authentic.
    • Apply both: your own badges and logos follow the brand scheme; third-party badges keep their official colour.

Store look: branded versus generic dropshipping (dropshipping: you sell a product, and a supplier ships it to the buyer)

1 min
Signal Generic store Branded store
Overall impression Empty, no brand personality, reads as a quick dropship store Branding, consistent colors and some character added
Niche position Copies a competitor's store; in niches with 20 to 100 or more sellers per product, it cannot stand out Branded landing page and store design beyond the product itself
Packaging and assets Unbranded Branded boxes, a sample product photographed for the site, a custom-branded landing page
Visuals Generic product images Professional branded images, lifestyle photos, custom packaging mockups
  • A product page built with a branded template structure (a ready-made page layout with a consistent design) signals a professional store, even when you use a template.
  • Higher perceived trust raises conversion (the share of visitors who buy) compared with minimal store designs.
  • A simple, low-excitement product can sell at a premium (a higher price) if the product page and visuals look clean and branded.
  • Consistent photos, restrained text, benefit copy (text that explains what the buyer gains) and a small discount or bundle (several items sold together) make the store look trustworthy.
  • Match layout and tone to the price and nature of the product.
  • A premium product needs a refined, confident look and tone.
  • A bargain needs a busy, deal-focused look and tone.
  • For jewelry, keep the design light: few elements, elegant fonts, plenty of space, simple benefit text.
  • Testimonials (customer quotes) and trust signals stay.
  • Features and comparison blocks can be removed if they add no real differentiator (a feature that sets you apart), e.g. anti-scratch or rust-proof material.
  • Invest little money, but make the look distinct.
  • Custom branding raises conversion rates and separates the store from competitors.

Imagery and visuals

1 min
  • Brand images should mix raw images (unposed, unpolished photos), lifestyle images and review visuals showing people who are happy with the product.
  • This gives the impression of a real, trusted company rather than a generic dropshipping store.
  • Sources for lifestyle images (photos of people using the product):
    • Canva (a design tool) and Printful (a print-on-demand company that makes product mockups, or preview images)
    • searches for lifestyle product images
    • freelancers hired on Fiverr (a site for hiring freelancers)
  • Lifestyle images show people holding or using the product.
  • Influencer-style photography (casual photos like social media creators post) tends to devalue a premium brand image.
  • Editorial-style photography (magazine-quality photos) supports higher pricing.
  • The first impression a customer gets of the brand shapes how they see it afterwards.
  • Phone-shot product videos are good enough if the camera is held steady and the lighting is decent.
  • Show how the product works, such as how a lock opens or how doors move.
  • Attaching artisan videos (videos of makers at work) or interviews adds value.
  • They work best after the buyer finds the product.
  • For buyers searching generic product names, optimize product pages and the Merchant Center feed (the product list you send to Google Merchant Center) for those terms first.
  • Then use videos for email or remarketing (showing ads again to people who visited before).
  • Pick the rule that fits your case:
    • Premium pricing: editorial style supports higher prices.
    • Visitors arriving from short-video content: polished studio imagery (perfect-looking professional photos) can feel out of place. Real-customer photos match what they have just seen.
    • Default brand image set: a mix of raw, lifestyle and review visuals.

Product page trust elements

1 min
  • Product pages need more than one kind of trust signal (a sign that makes you believable).
  • Buyers scan images and GIFs (short looping animations) before reading text.
  • Combine visuals, reviews with photos, and direct answers to common concerns in the product description.
  • Product pages should answer the questions customers have before buying.
  • So descriptions go beyond supplier stock text (the generic text the supplier provides).
  • Good product information gives customers the experience that makes them buy from you.
  • Show star ratings such as 4.8 out of 5 near the top of the product page.
  • Visitors look at what other customers say first, so reviews should appear high on the page.
  • Reviews and photos of similar buyers raise the perceived likelihood of achieving the dream outcome (the result the buyer wants).
  • A product page with only a product image and no reviews or wearers (people wearing the product) makes purchase decisions much harder.
  • Place a "guaranteed safe checkout" trust badge directly below the add-to-cart call-to-action button (the button that tells the buyer to act by adding the item to the cart).
  • Custom icons can also work if they are consistent with the brand.
  • Once reviews exist, enable a Shopify review app (a plug-in that collects and shows reviews). Shopify is the online store platform you use.
  • Configure Google Webmaster Tools (Google's free site-check tool, now called Search Console) so Google crawls (checks) your site regularly and detects new reviews.
  • Star ratings then appear in Google search results next to your store name.
  • Advertise on the store which brands you sell for. State the brand names, the way large retailers state the brands they carry.
  • Visitors arrive searching for those products, so the brand names help them decide.
  • Keep the estimate for lead time (how long before the item is sent out) or shipping visible and clear on the product page.
  • Customers usually accept longer delivery times when they are stated openly.

Footer

1 min
  • Make the footer (the bottom section of every page) clean, professional and ready for suppliers.
  • Suppliers and wholesale partners (companies that buy in bulk) evaluate store credibility partly through footer design and completeness.
  • The footer should contain:
    • The logo
    • About Us information
    • contact details
    • policy pages
    • an FAQ (frequently asked questions) or how-to section
    • a short slogan or brand mission
    • a customer support email
    • links to social media profiles
  • Replace the Twitter bird icon with X. The Twitter icon in the Manhattan theme footer (Manhattan is a design template) should be updated to X. (2024.)
  • Do not offer competitor referrals or recommendations on your store before the customer has purchased.
  • Add referral recommendations only after purchase, to encourage repeat visits (return visits).

Cart and checkout

1 min
  • Match the local language of the market in your cart wording.
  • The cart title text (the heading above the cart) is free to edit.
  • So merchants (store owners) selling in the UK can use words like bag or basket instead of cart.
  • The cart icon in Shopify theme settings under Icons can be changed from Bag to Shopping cart.
  • This is a matter of personal preference. The cart icon is often preferred because customers recognise it.
  • Progress bar message pattern (a bar that shows how close the shopper is to a goal): state the remaining amount to the next threshold (the goal amount), then name the reward.
  • This tells shoppers exactly how close they are and what they gain.
    • "Add $25.00 more to unlock a free tote bag."
    • "You are $15.00 away from free shipping on your order."

Mobile

1 min
  • Around 80% to 85% of visitors arrive on mobile (phones), and they have very little time to decide.
  • The hero image takes the most space above the fold (the part of the screen visible before scrolling), so it must convince quickly.
