Brand protection and trademarks

Section 8.22 · 22 of 24 in this chapter · 5 min read

In short

  • A trademark is a legal right to a name or logo. Trademarking is optional for most small stores.
  • Never copy a competitor's logo, text or photos. Make your own.
  • To get a copycat removed, send a complete report with proof of ownership, from a company email.
  • Put your store name on creator videos and product images. This makes copying harder.

Trademark decisions

  • A trademark is a legal right to a name or logo. It stops others from using it.
  • Trademarking your business name is optional for most dropshippers. Dropshippers sell products they do not keep in stock. The supplier ships them.
  • A trademark gives peace of mind. You do not need one to run a store.
  • If you have not put much effort into branding (building your brand's name and look), change a name that is very similar to a competitor's.
  • The risks are:
    • Cease-and-desist letters (formal legal letters telling you to stop).
    • Customer confusion (buyers think two brands are the same).
    • Remarketing ads (ads shown again to people who already visited your store) that mix up the brands.
  • In Shopify (an online store platform), you can change your domain (your web address) easily by linking a new one.
  • A TM or ® mark next to a product name does not mean the trademark is registered. TM means a brand claims a trademark. ® means it is registered.
  • Customers may believe these symbols. Do not use them without a real registration.
  • A unique mechanism (the special way your product works) often carries a trademark. This stops competitors from using the same term.
  • Before you name your own mechanism, check whether the brand already holds a trademark for it.
  • A trademarked logo showed no measurable difference in sales (the conversion rate, meaning how many visitors buy) in one owner's experience.
  • Trademark the brand mainly for protection. Consider it at larger scale.

Registration by jurisdiction

  • A jurisdiction is the country or region whose laws apply.
  • Trademark protection depends on the registration. The trademark must be registered in the jurisdiction where the infringement targets customers before a report can succeed. An infringement is unauthorized use of protected work.
  • Brand names can be registered as trademarks in several regions (for example EU, Australia, Cambodia, US).
  • Check a trademark in every market where the brand sells.
  • Do not send reports for a trademark that is not registered in the jurisdiction where the infringer targets customers.
  • Confirm the registration before you report.

Copyright

  • Copyright is a legal right to original creative work, such as photos.
  • It covers original product photos and images the brand created, such as studio shots with models.
  • Keep the original files and the publication date as proof.
  • A licensed product is made with permission from its owner. For example, a product made under a license from a major media company.
  • State in the report that the product is licensed.
  • You can name the licensor (the company that gave the permission). This supports the claim that the copies are unauthorized.
  • If a book is currently run by a main author, a listing risks a DMCA takedown from that author.
  • DMCA (a US copyright law) takedown means a request to remove content.
  • This can lead to a Meta ad ban (Meta, which runs Facebook and Instagram, stops your ads) or a Shopify DMCA action.
  • Reduce the risk with a custom cover page or an internally customized book.

Copying competitors' assets

  • Assets are a store's brand materials, such as logo, website text and images.
  • Never copy a competitor's unique store assets, such as their logo, custom website copy or custom-made images.
  • Taking these is treated as stealing intellectual property (IP, creative work owned by someone). It is not an acceptable shortcut in dropshipping.
  • Create your own brand assets.
  • Selling the same type of product as other stores is fine. Similar products are not the problem.
  • The line is crossed only when you copy their original creative work, such as brand assets or written and visual content.

Detection

  • Choose the platforms (websites or apps) to monitor based on where the brand's customers and copycats are. A copycat is a store that copies yours.
  • For a brand selling apparel (clothing) and blankets, Facebook and Instagram are the main hotspots.
  • Marketplaces (shopping sites that host many sellers, such as Amazon, eBay and Etsy) and standalone websites (stores on their own site) are also searched.
  • Other brands may find different hotspots based on their products.
  • Detection methods:
    1. Search keywords on each platform.
    2. Search the Meta Ad Library (a public list of all Facebook and Instagram ads) for ads that claim to sell the brand's product or use its name.
    3. Run a general search across platforms to see what appears.

Reporting process

  • Platforms are sometimes reluctant to act. They may suggest doing as little as possible.
  • A complete report gives the platform what it needs to act. It includes proof of ownership, links and a clear legal basis (the law the claim rests on).
  • Keep a detailed log (a written record) of every infringement and report.
  • The process is manual and often slow, from days to months. It involves many back-and-forth exchanges with the platform.
  • The log is also the basis for measuring trends.

Reporting mistakes

Mistake Why it matters Do instead Source
Using a generic or personal email address (e.g. Gmail) Platforms may consider it unauthorized Use a company-domain address (an email ending in your own web address) for reporting
Linking private or unpublished material (e.g. shared cloud folders with raw photos or marketing videos) The accused party receives the full report Link only to published material (material already public, such as live pages)
Reporting a trademark not registered where the infringer targets customers The report cannot succeed Confirm the registration first
  • A repeat infringer is someone who copies again. They may return after a takedown with a new page.
  • Keep the older complaint records and original publication dates. They show prior ownership.
  • Report the reappearing content again.

Defense against copycats

  • Create custom content, such as your own product shoot, creators (people who make social videos) or actors.
  • Give the product a custom name to reduce direct copying by competitors.
  • Use the store or domain name inside custom creator videos, not only the product name.
  • Clips that copy the videos then carry your brand name. This makes them harder for competitors to pass off as their own.
  • Keep subtle watermarks (faint logos over an image) on product page images.
  • Do not put a watermark on the primary variant image (the main picture for one color or size option).
  • Reason: Google Merchant Center (Google's product listing tool) can reject listings with visible watermarks.
  • A watermark is also evidence. Copycat stores often scrape (automatically copy) whole image sets. A watermarked photo on a copied page can support a later takedown.
  • A private brand (a brand you own) is much harder to copy than a dropshipped store, because nobody else owns it.
  • Becoming the brand that suppliers and customers want reduces the risk of being dropped by a supplier.
  • Copying a store is a common worry, but a private brand is the stronger position.
  • Fast fashion brands (cheap clothing that follows trends quickly) can copy a design within about two to three months of its release.
  • Stay ahead by moving on to the next design. Rely on quality, brand identity and new ideas, not on one product.
