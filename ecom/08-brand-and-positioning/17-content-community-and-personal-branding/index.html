Playbook › 8. Brand and positioning

Content, community and personal branding

Section 8.17 · 17 of 24 in this chapter · 14 min read

In short

  • Build trust slowly with helpful content. Let it run in the background, not as a day-one step.
  • Be a visible, helpful expert, not only a seller. Show the real person behind the brand.
  • Do not copy others. Share clear, different opinions.
  • Ask customers for reviews and photos. Use them as proof that you are trusted.
  • Do not argue with online critics. Ignore them and be kind to others.

Building authority and trust through content

2 min
  • Authority (being seen as a trusted expert) is a long-term project.
  • It runs in the background. It is not a day-one step.
  • First, move from silver suppliers to the best ones, the gold suppliers, in your niche. A niche is one narrow group of customers and products. A supplier is a company that provides your products.
  • Then expand into buying guides (articles that help people choose) and tutorials (step-by-step how-to guides).
  • To build authority and trust through content, follow this order:
    1. Write thought-leadership articles (articles that share your clear view on industry trends and best practices).
    2. Share your company values through content, for example sustainability (caring for the environment) or charity donations.
    3. Feature customer testimonials (written or spoken praise from buyers) and case studies (detailed stories of how a customer got results) prominently.
    4. Post guides and tutorials that teach, not only sell.
    5. Become a helpful expert, not just a retailer (a shop that only sells).
  • Before you plan content, define the goal of each content column or pillar (a main content topic).
  • Examples of goals: brand awareness (people know your name), audience sentiment (how people feel about you), trend reach (how many people see your trend content), education or trust.
  • Each video should have one clear job, such as nurturing (keeping people interested), giving value, educating or building trust.
  • Content should offer a unique perspective instead of repeating what everyone in the niche says.
  • Debatable, distinctive viewpoints attract podcast hosts (people who run audio shows) and readers far more than generic posts.
  • Do not copy your niche.
  • Do not write generic content, the kind everyone else publishes.
  • Such content is boring. It will not attract podcast hosts, readers or journalists.
  • Become the recognized expert in your niche before you push sales.
  • Answer questions in Facebook groups, on TikTok (a short-video app) and in forums (online discussion boards). Explain why your product is different.
  • This active presence brings early sales when paid channels (paid ads) are not yet working.
  • Brands that post only helpful, polished content, with no visible person behind it, tend to feel distant to followers.
  • Being human matters as much as being useful.

Sharing process and handling criticism

1 min
  • Sharing your process publicly builds trust and attracts an audience.
  • Process means how you work. It can include works in progress, tips and small glimpses of your daily work.
  • You control what to reveal. You can keep specific details private while still showing progress.
  • Success follows a two-step formula:
    1. Do consistently good work. It takes daily effort and accepts a period of weak results.
    2. Publish that work online.
  • Skipping either step means little visibility (few people see you) or little substance (little real value).
  • Build audience trust by sharing small useful items. Examples: a practical tip, a relevant article link or a book you are reading.
  • Do not only promote products.
  • Reveal one part of your process at a time.
  • Keep specific details private if needed.
  • Do not reply to online attacks.
  • Do not spend time on public disputes. They give critics more visibility.
  • Ignore negative commenters.
  • Be kind and generous toward others. People tend to notice and respond to that.

Showing people behind the brand

1 min
  • Show the people behind the brand.
  • Share behind-the-scenes content (what happens out of view).
  • Audiences connect most with visible human stories.
  • Community, trust, connection and longevity (staying power over the years) are what separate the brands that perform best over time.
  • Personal branding makes the owner the public voice of the business.
  • Channels include a blog, guest posts (articles written for other websites), podcast appearances and a YouTube channel.
  • The more the owner speaks about the business and its industry, the more traffic (website visitors), sales and industry connections it tends to attract.

Customer-generated content and social proof

1 min
  • Customer-generated content (CGC) means content made by your customers.
  • Social proof means signs that other buyers trust you, such as their reviews and photos.
  • Use CGC in this order:
    1. Ask satisfied customers for testimonials, photos or short videos.
    2. Offer coupons, free accessories or discounts in exchange for their content.
    3. Feature their stories prominently on blog and product pages.
    4. Implement Q&A apps (similar to Amazon's format), where customers ask and answer questions about products.
    5. This provides authentic social proof and improves SEO (how well search engines like Google find and rank your pages).
  • Build community by giving customers reasons to post their own photos, videos, recipes or stories.
  • Their posts become content and social proof.
  • Host these contributions in a group where people can easily upload them.
  • Adult coloring books built a brand community. Customers share their finished artwork on Facebook, Instagram and Pinterest.
  • The seller's goal is to make customers spread word of mouth (people telling others about you), so the brand becomes more valuable when sold.
  • Few e-commerce (online selling) stores collect post-purchase feedback (opinions after buying) and then tell customers what changed because of it.
  • Doing this consistently gives a store a differentiator (something that sets it apart) in its market.
  • Customers rarely see this from competitors (other shops selling similar products).

Brand vocabulary and community experience

1 min
  • Own your vocabulary. Name your places, routines and events with your own terms.
  • Repeated use of those terms ties them to your brand, so people think of you when they hear them.
  • Customer experience in a brand community should feel like belonging. Members talk to each other and form friendships.
  • Consistency across locations and touchpoints (every place a customer meets your brand) makes the experience predictable.
  • Predictable experiences build loyalty (customers who keep buying from you).
  • Brand consistency across articles and images matters.
  • A site built over several years may have inconsistent images.
  • Set a brand standard (a fixed rulebook for look and style) and follow it.
  • Keep images consistent from that point forward.
  • A fitness training brand built a distinct identity.
  • Its training locations look like warehouses or old factories, not normal gyms.
  • It uses its own terms for locations, workouts and events.
  • Its marketing is polarizing (it divides people: they either love it or hate it).
  • Members experience a family-like community. They talk and make friends.
  • Consistent experiences across different locations worldwide make members spread the word.
  • When the brand name was searched on a video platform, eight of the top ten recent results came from people other than the brand itself.

Measuring organic conversation

1 min
  • Organic conversation means people talking about your brand for free, without you paying for it.
  • A brand is working when other people talk about it without being asked.
  • They use the hashtag (a word tag that starts with #), post photos, submit videos and mention the brand in their own content.
  • Track these signals as proof that the brand is growing.
  • Word of mouth (people recommending you to others) from friends and family drove unpaid sales.
  • After stock (the products you hold to sell) arrived, around 1,000 USD in revenue (money coming in from sales) came in without ad spend (money paid for ads).
  • To check whether a brand is being talked about on YouTube:
    1. Search the brand name or category term (a general word for your product type) on YouTube.
    2. Filter the results to videos uploaded in the past 24 hours.
    3. Count how many of the top ten results come from people other than the brand itself.
  • If most results are from third-party creators (people not connected to your brand), the brand is generating organic conversation.

Testing interest before a launch

1 min
  • A launch is the day a product first goes on sale.
  • Before a product is for sale, you can post a blurred photo of an unreleased product on Facebook.
  • Add a "guess what I'm selling" question.
  • Wrong guesses still create comments. They show which subjects spark curiosity (interest).
  • This is a cheap way to test interest and start conversation before a launch.

Engaging loyal followers and live communities

1 min
  • Brands that win on Instagram focus on building a community of people invested in the brand's journey (its story over time).
  • They do not focus only on raising follower counts.
  • A strong community turns passive viewers (people who only watch) into repeat visitors who keep coming back.
  • Ways to celebrate your most engaged followers:
    • Shout them out in Stories (short posts that vanish after a day) or comment replies.
    • Repost their DMs (direct messages, private messages) only with their permission.
    • Thank them by name or with a custom voice-note reply.
    • Tag them in posts when they contributed an idea.
  • The most engaged followers can become moderators (people who manage and keep order) in a broadcast channel (a one-way message channel for followers).
  • You can also invite them to a separate group chat for super-fans.
  • This makes top followers feel invested in the brand and rewarded for their activity.
  • Set all brand data and messaging in Livecom (the live-stream setup named here) before a live stream starts.
  • The brand is controlled through this setup, not during the broadcast.
  • The pre-stream channel page (the page shown before the stream starts) is where the stream is promoted and the community is built.
  • Active participation in online e-commerce and dropshipping communities builds a reputation (how others see you).
  • Dropshipping means selling products that a supplier ships directly to buyers, so you hold no stock.
  • Your reputation attracts clients and requests for paid help.
  • Posting about results and lessons learned can lead to inbound demand (people contacting you) before any formal marketing is set up.

Social media as a producer

2 min
  • Use social media as a producer, not a consumer (someone who only watches).
  • Focus on creating business-relevant engagement (interaction that helps your business) and relationships.
  • Do not focus on passive scrolling (endless viewing) of personal content.
  • Reuse the same presenter image, layout, logo placement and video length across every episode of a faceless channel (a channel without the owner's face on screen).
  • A consistent format makes the channel recognizable.
  • It lets each new video be produced faster from a fixed template (a set pattern you copy).
  • Write each video as if speaking to one viewer, not a group, because each person watches alone.
  • Choose the platform based on where your target audience (the people you want to reach) spends time.
  • Each platform has a slightly different tone.
  • Build audience profiles (detailed descriptions of typical buyers, called personas) in this order:
    1. Describe one persona with a daily routine, interests, income situation and life stage.
    2. Write a second persona for another segment (group) of the audience in the same way.
    3. List the search terms each persona would type.
    4. List the video formats each persona would identify with.
    5. Use the personas as the reference when deciding topics and formats.
  • Audience profiles differ by platform and tone.
  • A teenage viewer of makeup tutorials on YouTube, a thirty-something man looking for anti-aging routines (skin care to look younger), and a founder audience on LinkedIn (a professional network) each need a different voice and topic angle.
  • Define the platform, age and interests before writing the script (the spoken text).
  • TikTok statistics cited for branded video (short videos made by a brand):
    • 84% of people said a brand video convinced them to buy a product or service.
    • 67% want varied storytelling from brands.
    • 52% of internet users want brands to be authentic.
  • Once a seller can reliably generate views (how many times videos are watched) on TikTok, the same skill can be applied to a genuinely good product.
  • The goal is a lasting brand, not only short-term viral sales (a sudden wave of sales from a video that spreads fast).
  • Early tests on weak products count as paid practice (practice that costs money) for this skill.

Podcasts and content series

1 min
  • Build a podcast (an audio show) or content series for existing loyal customers.
  • Do not use it as a new-customer acquisition channel (a way to find new buyers).
  • In the example, weekly episodes, around 5,000 listens per episode per month, did not bring a surge (sudden rise) of new customers.
  • But the most loyal buyers (30 to 40 orders) listened to every episode. This made it a retention tool (a way to keep buyers coming back).
  • This model may not fit every industry.

Personal branding

1 min
  • Start a YouTube channel for reach (how many people see you) around your products or industry.
  • Google indexes videos (adds them to its search list) very quickly.
  • Publish consistently on one channel.
  • Do not post only occasionally.
  • Build personal branding in this order:
    1. Start a blog on your own store site. Write guest posts for other industry sites.
    2. Use the unique perspective from those posts to get invited onto other people's podcasts, since debate-driven conversations attract listeners.
    3. Start a YouTube channel about your products or industry. Keep posting on it consistently, because Google indexes videos very quickly.

PR and press releases

1 min
  • PR (public relations, getting media attention) can help your store grow.
  • Issue a press release (an official news announcement sent to the media) when you add a new supplier. Tell that supplier about it.
  • This can bring links back to your site (links from other websites to yours, called backlinks).
  • Publications that feature your store can be listed in a resource section (a page of useful links) on your site.
  • helperreporterout.com connects you with journalists who look for industry experts.
  • Register, and write a bio (a short personal description) with your areas of expertise.
  • Reporters can then contact you for news stories and features (longer articles).
  • A brand-owned store can own its brand name in search (rank first for its own name).
  • But it must create demand (make people want to buy) for its products through PR, brand mentions and content.
  • Without these, product-name searches stay small.

Trade shows and events

1 min
  • Trade show (a large industry fair where companies meet) strategy:
    1. Attend industry trade shows to find suppliers and learn trends.
    2. If you cannot attend, contact suppliers and ask them to take photos of your product or logo at the show.
    3. Post articles featuring your products at the trade show (e.g., "Our Products at [Trade Show Name]").
    4. This creates content, backlinks and authority without direct attendance.

Dream 100 relationship building

1 min
  • A Dream 100 is a list of 100 key people or sites in your niche that you want to build relationships with.
  • Build connections with each Dream 100 contact through the platform where they are active.
    1. Follow them on Instagram, Facebook or other social networks where they post.
    2. Subscribe to or like their YouTube channel, depending on what makes their posts appear in your feed (your personal list of posts).
    3. On LinkedIn, follow them if your business is B2B (business to business, selling to other companies).
    4. Join their Facebook groups and forums.
    5. Subscribe to the email list of anyone who has a website or blog.
  • Join Facebook groups and forums as a personal profile, never as a Facebook page (a business account).
  • Many groups refuse page accounts, and some admins (group managers) block them outright.
  • Relationships are built between people. Operators (the people running the business) should be visible as the real person behind the business.
  • Using a fake name is possible but is considered misleading.
  • Open ownership of the business supports the Dream 100 approach.
  • Use a dedicated email address, one used only for this purpose, for signing up to Dream 100 email lists.
  • Keep it separate from the main business or personal address.
  • A new Gmail or other mail provider account is suitable.
  • Subscribing to a prospect's (a possible customer or partner's) email list shows which topics and content their audience cares about.
  • This can guide your own email and site content.
  • The content should be modelled (used as a pattern), not copied.
  • In outreach emails (messages sent to reach new contacts), describe your site by the problem it solves for the reader, not by the products it sells.
  • For example, say you help people build a dream bathroom rather than saying you sell bathtubs.

Sponsorships and community partnerships

1 min
  • A sponsorship means paying to support an event or group in exchange for visibility.
  • Sponsoring community events is not the only option.
  • Sponsorship and visibility can also come from ads or placements (paid spots) on blog posts run by members of your industry community.
  • Community partnerships cost money. Higher sales usually require investing more in the business.
  • The community is the network of your industry. Being visible in it means people link to you and mention your name in conversations.
  • Set a realistic budget for monthly or yearly sponsorship (a fixed amount of money) before accepting any sponsorship request.
  • Saying yes to every request is a fast way to spend money without results.
  • Once you publicly offer to sponsor events, many organizers and companies will ask you to join.
  • You need a fixed sponsorship budget before you respond.
  • Pick a monthly or yearly amount first. Then choose the opportunities with the largest reach (number of people it reaches) within that budget.
  • Plan community sponsorships in this order:
    1. Decide how much money you will spend each month or year.
    2. Identify the sponsorship or event opportunities in your industry with the largest reach, such as sponsoring events, trade shows or meetups (informal get-togethers), or hosting your own.
    3. Differentiate your sponsorship with a unique format instead of only placing your name on a banner.
  • Hosting your own recurring events (events that repeat), such as dinners or social games for your industry community, as the sole sponsor, sets you apart from banner sponsors.
  • It also builds your name in the community.
  • Do not sponsor events with only your name on a banner.
  • Undifferentiated sponsorships (ones that look like every other sponsor) do little to raise your profile.
  • They cost money without a clear return (a clear gain).
  • No single community or sponsorship tactic works for every industry.
  • Choose tactics based on what your specific niche and customers respond to.
