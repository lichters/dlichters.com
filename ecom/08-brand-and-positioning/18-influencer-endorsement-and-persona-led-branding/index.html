Playbook › 8. Brand and positioning

Influencer, endorsement and persona-led branding

Section 8.18 · 18 of 24 in this chapter · 19 min read

In short

  • Pick your goal and your buyers first. Only then look for an influencer.
  • A big follower count does not mean sales. Check that the followers are the people you want to sell to.
  • Use only real people, real credentials and true stories. Never invent or borrow them.
  • Send real, high-quality product samples. Influencers judge your product by them.
  • Ask who your buyer already trusts. Pick a character that matches that trust, not the product.
More key points
  • Never use a logo or product photo as the profile or cover photo of a character page.
  • A local sports club can give your brand recognition in your home market.

Set the goal and audience before choosing a partner

1 min

Before you search for influencers (people with a large online following who promote products), decide these four things in order:

  1. Brand objective: decide your goal. It can be brand awareness (people knowing your name), sales conversions (people buying), or community engagement (people interacting with you). The goal decides which type of creator fits.
  2. Target audience: study the demographics (age, place and similar facts), interests and behaviours (what people do) of your ideal customers.
  3. Preferred platforms: find out which social apps your audience uses, and pick creators who are active there.
  4. Influencer selection: choose creators whose audience and message match your goal and your audience. Keep the message honest.

Clear goals give influencer marketing a direction. Each partnership should support your overall marketing plan, not stand alone as one post.

Before choosing an influencer, define your target audience in this order:

  1. Define the demographic (age, location and similar traits) of your target customers.
  2. Define their interests.
  3. Define their behaviour.

Use these three points to find the influencer whose audience matches yours most closely.

Match the influencer to the campaign goal

1 min
  • Brand awareness: choose an influencer with broad reach (many people seeing their posts) and a large, mixed audience.
  • Engagement: choose an influencer whose audience is active. Active means people comment, like, share and repost stories. This audience can be smaller than a mass audience and still be worth more.
  • Conversion or sales: choose an influencer with a proven record of changing what people buy. Look for detailed product reviews, real product use, or exclusive discounts for their followers.
  • Product launch: pick an influencer who has a history of introducing new products to their audience. A promotion or giveaway with them is a good launch approach.

A large follower count alone does not make an influencer a good fit. Their audience must match your target buyers, because many followers does not mean those followers will buy.

Influencers must share your brand's values and message style, not only your audience. Their tone and behaviour reflect on you. A family-friendly brand should avoid creators who use swear words or post inappropriate content. Creators whose lifestyle fits your message are better partners than creators who only cover a related topic.

Pitch and work with influencers

1 min
  • Be open about what the collaboration involves from the start. Tell the influencer it is good for both of you. Stay flexible on how the work is done, especially with larger influencers, to build a good working relationship.
  • In your first message, clearly state how the collaboration fits your brand and the influencer's goals. Showing this fit is a core part of a successful pitch.
  • Brand your products heavily when working with influencers. Influencers avoid promoting cheap-looking products. Digital products need mockups (realistic preview images), a polished website and a branded PDF (a document file) with the influencer's photos.
  • For dropshipped products (products the supplier ships straight to the buyer) used in influencer promotions, send at least one branded unit to the influencer. Your terms and conditions can state that branded packaging depends on stock, so unbranded packaging may be sent when stock is missing.
  • Influencers test products before they promote them. Send a high-quality sample with fast delivery. Packaging quality directly affects whether they will promote the product.
  • Partner with people who already have authority (trust and respect) in the community. Do not position your brand as the better choice against them. Working with established voices builds trust faster than claiming to be better.

Affiliate partners

1 min

Older source. An affiliate is a partner who earns a share of each sale they bring you. To set up an affiliate program for your store:

  1. Decide if you will use the Refersion app (paid, not free) to manage it. Refersion is a tool that tracks affiliate sales.
  2. Create a small affiliate page that explains why you have an affiliate program.
  3. Describe the type of business partner you want (niche, audience size, etc.). A niche is a small, specific market.
  4. Include an email address or contact form for interested partners. Do not auto-approve anyone.
  5. Review each applicant by hand. Approve only qualified affiliates who fit your brand.

Authority endorsements and credibility transfer

1 min

Credibility transfer means trust passes from a respected name to your product when that name backs it. Endorsement means a person or group publicly supports your product.

  • Credibility transfers from respected institutions, professional bodies (official groups of experts in a field) and recognised symbols to a product that carries their endorsement. A buyer who respects the endorser often stops checking the claims themselves.
  • Older source. As a new business, aligning with trusted market figures passes some of their credibility to you by association. This builds your own authority much faster than building alone.
  • The strongest endorsement is an official approval from a respected institution. It can be shown with a seal or logo that signals review. If a full endorsement is impossible, a recognisable symbol of authority can still give a similar boost.
  • Health and science products gain credibility from authority cues (signs of expertise), such as a figure in a white lab coat presenting statistics. Use only authority cues that are real and relevant. The trust they carry depends on the reader's respect for that authority.
  • Signed ads, a named spokesperson or a clear personal authority make claims feel more credible than a company voice. Use a real person to represent the brand when the claim depends on personal expertise.

Use authority endorsements in this order:

  1. List the people, groups and institutions in your industry whose respect your target buyers already have.
  2. Ask one of them for a testimonial (a written statement of praise), endorsement or official approval of your product.
  3. Get written permission to use their name and image in your marketing.
  4. Optionally, pay for a short video of them explaining the product for your website or a DVD.
  5. Place a list of verifiable facts, figures and charts next to the endorsement. The authority should support your argument, not replace it.

Identity and group appeals

1 min

  • Buyers often purchase to improve their self-image and to close a gap they feel in their identity. Ads can show the traits the target customer wants to display, such as attractiveness, intelligence, economic success or status, and link them to the product.

  • You can create identity appeal without a persuasive argument. Link the product to respected users in the same field. For example, a martial arts school names well-known fighters who train there. Or a printer offers free work to local public figures and names them afterwards. A security firm can cite the choices of professionals who have stricter needs than the average buyer. In each case, the copy shows the image the buyer wants rather than proving a claim.

  • Groups shape buying decisions in three ways:

    • Aspirational: people the buyer wants to become.
    • Associative: people who share the buyer's values.
    • Dissociative: groups the buyer wants to avoid.

    Link the product to one or more of these. This pushes the buyer toward a decision made on group identity.

  • For an aspirational group appeal, the buyer must be able to recognise and identify with the people shown. Showing people who are too distant, too old or too ordinary for your market weakens the appeal.

  • Associative group appeals speak directly to the attitudes of one segment (a defined part of your market). They can also set the product apart from a rival group. This can make the product feel cooler or more accepted to younger buyers.

  • A brand name or tagline can signal distance from a group the buyer does not want to resemble, such as an older generation. This attracts a younger segment that wants to look different. This dissociative signal works best when the target group clearly sees the contrast and the brand delivers a product that fits it.

Attractive characters

2 min
  • An Attractive Character is a persona (a made-up or chosen public identity that represents you) that draws people to you on any platform, such as email, Facebook or YouTube. It does not have to be physically attractive. What matters is that it gives audiences a reason to follow and trust.
  • Build the character from three parts: elements, identity and storylines. Each part is needed for a character people will like and follow. Start with the elements and identity before writing storylines.
  • Every Attractive Character needs a backstory (the past events that shaped the person) that connects directly to the product you sell. For weight-loss products, use a weight-loss story. For financial advice, use a financial story. The backstory is the hook that makes people willing to follow the path you lay out.
  • If you lack a relevant backstory, you can use a story from a customer, student or client success case and make that person the character. The character does not have to be the founder, but the backstory must be real and relevant. Success stories from others are valid material. (Rewriter's note: get that person's permission, and say the story is theirs.)
  • Use parables (short, memorable stories from your life) to teach the lessons you want customers to learn. People remember stories longer than bare facts. Look through your own experiences for these stories.
  • People value what they pay for more than what they get free, and they act on it more. A small charge can increase follow-through. This is why a parable about paying for a free-looking gift works well when you ask for investment. Use this idea when you want customers to commit.
  • Share real flaws and weaknesses. Perfect characters are hard to relate to. Audiences feel close to imperfect people and trust them more. Hiding every weakness makes you seem untouchable.
  • Take clear positions on hard issues. A polarising character (one that splits opinion) creates supporters, neutral people and opponents. Supporters become loyal fans who share and buy again and again. Staying neutral to please everyone reaches almost no one.
  • Personal life details, such as a family milestone, can open a new segment of buyers who relate to that part of your persona. This new segment often trusts you enough to buy for the first time. Personal stories widen your audience beyond your first niche.

Identities

  • Leader: guides the audience from one place to another. The leader has walked a similar path, knows the hurdles and has achieved the result the audience wants. People follow for help along that path.
  • Adventurer or Crusader: curious. Goes on a journey to discover the truth, then brings back answers to share with the audience.
  • Reporter or Evangelist: interviews many people to learn and share their stories. Interviewing experts is an effective way to build authority in a niche you do not yet know well.
  • Reluctant Hero: humble and does not seek attention, but feels a duty to share important knowledge despite being uncomfortable in the spotlight. This identity suits people who have hard-won secrets and a sense of responsibility to share them. Choose the identity that fits you naturally and build your character around it.

Storyline templates

2 min

Fill in the text in square brackets with your own details.

Loss-and-redemption narrative skeleton: 'I used to have it all and felt like I was at the peak. Life was going well. Then [event] struck. I was forced to find a way out of [situation]. Still, it ended up being a silver lining, since I got through [experience] and came away with [lesson or result]. These days I'm [current position].' The narrative shows how hardship can lead to gain. If you don't have a personal story like this, take one from a follower, a client, or mainstream media.
Before and after storyline template: 'First I was [starting condition]. Now I am [current condition].' Use it to show transformation in any market, for example 'First I was [pain point]. Then I tried [product]. Now I [desired result].' Transformation stories are simple to write, can often be shown with pictures alone, and are strong motivators.
Amazing discovery storyline template, for selling webinars and teleseminars: 'Everyone, wait until you hear about this amazing new thing I just discovered! You will not believe it, but I got results on my very first try. I was not sure it would work, but it is amazing, so you have to try it [specific call to action].' This story helps people believe the answer they have been searching for is finally available.
Secret-driven narrative skeleton: 'I'm keeping a secret. To learn what it is, you have to [specific action].' Intrigue about a concealed method pulls readers deeper into your funnel and higher up your value ladder. A secret-based narrative performs well in most markets when the secret connects to what you sell.
  • Us versus them storylines set your audience against outsiders, such as talkers versus doers. They create a rallying cry (a call to unite) for loyal followers. Use them to split opinion and pull fans closer. Make the choice of identity clear so people decide which side they are on.
  • Third-person testimonials give social proof (evidence that others trust you). Collect as many customer, client and student success stories as you can. Weave them into your messages or use them as standalone case studies. Other people's results often persuade more than your own claims.

Cases

  • Fast-food chain (weight-loss character): a traditional fast-food chain changed its marketing around a customer who had lost weight, from about 425 pounds to about 190 pounds, by eating from the chain twice a day. The company used his before and after story in commercials and on billboards. This set the brand apart from competitors. The positioning moved the chain from generic fast food toward a weight-loss plan. The same character kept bringing in business for more than fifteen years.
  • Course company (student as character): a company built its continuity program (a plan where customers pay regularly) around a student's success story, not its founder's story. The launch sold over 8,000 units and made more than 1,000,000 USD in sales within two weeks. A later workshop for buyers was split about 50/50 between men and women. Usually such workshops were about 90% men. The women said they wanted to be like the featured student.

Persona-led ads and challenge formats

1 min
  • Ads framed as a person recommending something are expected to get higher engagement than brand-voiced ads for the same product and offer. Treat this as a hypothesis (an idea to test). Verify it with your own split tests (two versions shown to split groups of people) before you commit budget.
  • A famous personality can work better as a challenge or opponent than as an endorser. The challenge creates a story, while an endorsement only adds a name. Build the ad around the contest and let the product be the thing that gets challenged.
  • Chess computer campaign (1978): the product was sold on its features and was not selling well against competitors. The ad was reframed as a public challenge to a famous chess champion. It ran as three ads. The first announced the challenge. The second responded to a dispute over permission. The third showed the champion declining to play but endorsing the product. The three ads with different concepts sold more than 20,000 units. Competitors selling only the product did not succeed.

Choosing the archetype

1 min
  • Pick the archetype (the character type your buyer relates to) by asking who the buyer already trusts, such as a coach, a peer, a parent figure or a quiet expert. Match the character to that trust, not to the product itself. Base this on the product and avatar research (your made-up picture of the typical buyer).
  • A quiet expert, a calm person who knows a lot without being famous, does not need medical qualifications to be trusted. For example, a herbal product could be recommended by an older gardener with decades of hands-on experience rather than by a doctor.

Workflow:

  1. Read your product and avatar research: who buys, their age, life stage, self-image and insecurities.
  2. Ask who this buyer already trusts: a peer, a coach, a parent figure or a quiet expert.
  3. Pick the archetype that fits that trust source.
  4. Attach your customer research and product documents to Claude (an AI assistant).
  5. Paste the full generator prompt (a ready-made text that tells the AI what to do) for this topic, and let Claude read the research, pick the archetype and generate the assets.
  6. Check the 3-line research summary Claude prints to confirm it understood the buyer and product correctly.
  7. Copy the outputs: profile photo prompt (the text for the small face picture), cover photo prompt (the text for the wide top banner picture) and page bio (a short text about the character).
  8. Paste the profile and cover photo prompts into ChatGPT, DALL-E or Midjourney (AI tools that make pictures) to generate the images.
  9. Upload the images and the bio to the character page (a page for the fictional character).

Whitelist page results and common mistakes

1 min
  • Starting point: a brand page held a steady ROAS (return on ad spend: money earned back for each unit of money spent on ads) of 1.8 to 1.92. Change: a whitelist page (a page approved to run ads) with a fictional character was introduced for the ads. Result: ROAS rose to about 2.1 to 2.3, a small gain that matters more at scale (when you spend and sell a lot).
  • Common mistakes with character pages:
    • Using a logo (the brand sign) or a product shot (a photo of the product) as profile or cover photo.
    • Using E-commerce or Retail (shop-type labels) as the category.
    • Giving the character several niches as its purpose.
    • Using stock-photo perfection (generic pictures bought from photo sites) or polished corporate headshots (formal business portraits).
    • Impersonating a real person, which means pretending to be one.

Clubs, teams and local partnerships

1 min
  • For a brand focused on local awareness, approach large local sports teams to propose collaborations. These partnerships build brand recognition in a home market. They are aimed at awareness rather than direct sales.
  • A brand can be built around a local market by partnering with local sports teams or clubs for custom-branded merchandise, such as fan hoodies or skiing-related products. Custom logos on hoodies at the supplier (the company that makes the products) required orders of at least 500 pieces.
  • Custom-branded versions of a product can be sold at a higher price than standard designs. Club or partner logos add value and give fans a reason to buy.

Club partnership procedure

4 min
  1. Identify a local football club with passionate fans and a sold-out stadium (all seats sold), relevant to the brand's audience.
  2. Email the club's marketing department with a simple collaboration proposal.
  3. Hold a call with the club representative to discuss terms.
  4. Agree on a one-time payment for one year of logo usage rights (permission to use the club logo), reported at about 10,000 USD.
  5. Produce custom designs with the club logo and sell them to fans at a higher price.

More details

The five essential components every character needs:

Component Rule Example
Name Real first name, casual last name if needed, optional title such as Coach, Doc or Trainer Coach Sam, Doc Ellie, Trainer Mike
Face Clear headshot, direct eye contact, warm expression, AI-generated or licensed stock; the same photo across the page and all ads One consistent portrait
Voice Choose 3 personality traits and keep them in every post and ad; direct, first-person Direct, self-deprecating, practical
Story Personal experience with the exact problem the product solves; the origin story that earns credibility Fixed own posture after 10 years at a desk
Purpose One clear helping goal tied directly to the product category, not multi-niche 'Back pain from desk work', not 'wellness'

  • The character name is a real first name with an optional last name and an optional title such as Coach, Doc or Trainer. Titles signal authority without impersonating anyone. Avoid titles like Doc if the character has no real basis for implying that credential.

  • The character needs one consistent personality with exactly three traits, chosen once and kept in every post and every ad. Mixing tones between posts and ads breaks the illusion that a real person is speaking.

  • The character's purpose must be a single helping goal directly tied to your product category. A multi-niche purpose makes the character read as a brand rather than a person.

  • Setting the page category to E-commerce or Retail makes the page look like a store and breaks the person illusion. Use a person-like category instead, such as Coach, Enthusiast, Blog or Personal Trainer.

  • The character's bio should be 60 to 100 words, written in the first person, opening with a backstory hook tied to the product's problem area and ending with what the character shares or helps with. Use no hashtags, no emojis and no sales language.

  • A whitelist page is a Facebook page you own that presents a fictional character with perceived authority in your niche, instead of your corporate logo. Ads run from that page so the same product and offer look like a recommendation from a person. The page, audience and pixel all stay in your own account.

  • Every whitelist character needs five elements: a name, a face, a voice, a story and a purpose. If one of them is missing, the page reads as a brand and the illusion of a person breaks.

  • Give the character a real-sounding first name, an optional casual last name, and a title such as Coach or Trainer when it fits. Avoid 'Doc' and other medical titles, because they conflict with the medical caution for character pages.

  • Set the page category to a person-like label such as Coach, Enthusiast, Blog or Personal Trainer. Never use E-commerce or Retail as the category.

  • Use a clear headshot with direct eye contact and a warm, friendly expression for the profile photo. It can be AI-generated or licensed stock, but never a logo or a product shot. Use the same photo on the page and in all ads.

  • Use a lifestyle scene from the character's world as the cover photo, such as a desk setup, gym or outdoors. Never use a product shot or a discount graphic as the cover.

  • Choose three personality traits for the character and keep them consistent across every post, primary text and ad. Use them as the framework for how the copy is written.

  • The character's story is a personal experience with the exact problem the product solves, which builds credibility. Pin a personal organic post to the top of the page explaining why the character started recommending the product.

  • Each character needs one clear purpose tied directly to the product category, such as helping with back pain from desk work. Avoid vague purposes like 'wellness' and never cover several niches with one character.

  • The page bio is a short first-person backstory about the character's own experience with the product's problem. It should be 60 to 100 words, sound like a real person, and contain no hashtags, emojis or salesy language.

  • A face instead of a logo does about 80 percent of the work of building trust, even if the character profile is not perfect. This is why the whitelist page is worth starting on day one of testing.
