Playbook › 8. Brand and positioning

Customer research and avatars

Section 8.2 · 2 of 24 in this chapter · 30 min read

In short

  • Know your buyer well before you design the shop or write any sales text.
  • Look past age and income. Find out what they do and why they want it.
  • Write one avatar: a written picture of your ideal buyer, based on research. Update it often.
  • Speak to the deepest need, such as status, control or belonging, but never state it in a crude way.
  • Write down every main problem your product solves. There can be several.
More key points
  • Ask why the buyer wants the product. The deeper problem often matters more than the product.
  • Use the buyer's exact words. Their own phrases make the best headlines and copy.
  • Before you write any ad, fill in the persona brief: a short written profile of your buyer.

Research before the store and the copy

3 min

  • Understanding an audience means looking past demographics. Demographics are facts about a person, such as age, gender and socioeconomic status (their income and social class).

  • Looking past demographics shows you their behaviour and motivations. Motivations are the reasons why people act.

  • This distinction separates effective advertisers from those who work by trial and error. Trial and error means guessing and testing until something works.

  • Before branding, know what the product is, what it does and how it benefits customers.

  • Also know who the customers are, how they behave, and what their needs, goals and motivations are.

  • Branding means the name, look and feel that make your business recognizable.

  • Understand the customer before designing the store.

  • Review a customer Facebook group, the photos members post and what they watch online.

  • This removes guesswork about layout (how pages are arranged), imagery (pictures) and messaging (the words you use).

  • Before writing any copy, define the target market. The target market is the group of buyers you aim at.

  • Define the problems, desires and other facts about prospective customers. Prospective customers are people who may buy from you.

  • Copy means the written words that sell, such as text on a shop page, in an ad or in an email.

  • Copy works as a conversation with those people, so it only persuades when you know them well.

  • Keep the customer avatar sheet as a one-page reference. An avatar is a written picture of one type of buyer.

  • Anyone can check the sheet before writing copy for a single product, email or ad.

  • Customers are the group of people who experience the problem your product solves.

  • Confirm that this group exists through customer research.

  • Knowing the ideal customer in detail is required before you can communicate the problem and solution effectively. The ideal customer is the perfect buyer for your product, described in detail.

  • Empathy means feeling what another person feels. It is a core marketing skill.

  • Put yourself in the customer's position to see your business from their side.

  • The more you do this, the better the marketing becomes, especially in fields that must be estimated by intuition (gut feeling).

  • Review the customer research document at least once a year.

  • Update it whenever you learn something new, because the customer base can shift over time.

  • Completing the avatar profile before writing copy shortens the copywriting process.

  • Treat the avatar as a living document. A living document is updated again and again as you learn more about the market.

  • Audience research should cover four pillars. Use this table:

    Pillar What it describes
    Demographics Who they are
    Psychographics (inner traits: personality, values, beliefs and attitudes) What they value
    Behaviours (how they act) How they act: research habits, loyalty, online communities
    Goals, needs and motivations What they want and what belonging they seek

  • Use these pillars so that all brand messaging speaks to a defined group rather than everyone. Brand messaging means the words and images that represent your brand.

  • Before filling in the audience section of a brand document, prepare two earlier research outputs. A brand document is the file that sets out your brand's rules.

  • The two outputs are a detailed customer research spreadsheet and a shorter summary document.

  • Base demographics, psychographics, behaviours and goals on these sources, not on assumptions.

  • For performance-oriented products (products meant to deliver results, such as sports gear), psychographics typically include a desire for quality and results.

  • Audiences for these products also show a strong interest in the activity the product supports.

  • They also appreciate brand status and reputation.

  • The brand image and product descriptions should reflect these values directly.

  • When defining a brand's target audience, the overall group can be broad. It can span both genders, several age groups and low to high income levels.

  • Name one focused core age range where the product category is most active. For example, 15 to 45 for a sports brand.

  • Let that core guide the messaging.

  • Enter the research findings into the research form before any creative is produced. Creative means the ads, images and videos made to sell.

  • Doing this sharpens your ICP (ideal customer profile, the perfect buyer written down) and your USP (unique selling proposition, the one reason to buy from you instead of others).

The four layers: demographics, psychographics, behaviour and motivation

2 min

  • The four segmentation components are shown in this table. Segmentation means splitting buyers into groups.

    Component What it covers Examples
    Demographics Static attributes of a person Age, gender, income level, education, marital status
    Psychographics Personality, values, beliefs and attitudes Openness, conscientiousness, level of organization, tendency to worry; values such as control or belonging; beliefs about the world, religion, opinions
    Behavior What people do, their context and where the problem fits into their daily life or browsing situation Using a product in a kitchen, scrolling a feed while queuing in a store
    Goals, needs and motivations The underlying why behind what people do Status, power, security, freedom, belonging

  • Demographics are the most surface-level layer. Consider them last when defining a target audience.

  • Demographics matter when they change whether the product fits.

  • Example: a menstruation product. People over 50 generally no longer menstruate, because the average age of stopping is above 50.

  • Demographics alone often fail to explain buying.

  • Example: buyers of one jewelry piece can range from 20 to 50 years old.

  • The underlying need for status is usually a stronger basis for targeting than age or income.

  • Psychographics cover how a person is wired and what they believe.

  • They include personality traits, values such as control or belonging, beliefs about the world, and attitudes and opinions.

  • Use them to choose the tone and angle of the message. Tone is the feeling of your words. Angle is the point of view you take.

  • For a viewer persona (a made-up profile of one viewer), needs and motivations matter more than demographics.

  • Capture what the person desperately wants.

  • Capture what they fear not getting, what stands in their way and what outcome they want.

  • Age, income and education serve only as background.

  • Behavioural segmentation asks what the person is doing and in which context the problem appears.

  • Place the product at the point in the daily routine where the problem or trigger occurs. Do not only place it where they browse.

  • Example: a vegetable slicer's trigger is the moment someone cuts vegetables by hand. They find it tough and annoying, and get an uneven cut.

  • Describe the product through that exact moment so the problem and solution are immediately recognizable.

  • Sharpen the buyer persona with concrete details, such as reading habits, where they shop, income level, occupation and how often they buy.

  • Example: a buyer who purchases four times a year as a gift for friends.

  • These details guide ad and website messaging.

  • Premium beauty-tool example: the buyer invests in appearance and wellness.

  • This buyer buys from Instagram and Facebook ads, and trusts beauty influencers (people with many followers who recommend products) and stylists.

  • Describing the target customer and their psychology this concretely gives the audit enough to write relevant messaging.

  • The persona does not need every field completed.

  • Fill in as much as you can to get a usable picture of the customer.

  • Demographics are static facts. Psychographics cover values and personality.

  • Behaviour covers what they do and the context of their problem. Motivations cover the deeper needs and feelings.

  • Desire, feature and persona together are enough to start writing a decent headline. A headline is the big title line of your ad or page.

The iceberg model

3 min

  • The iceberg model is a way to understand the customer in layers.

  • Demographics (age, gender, location) are the visible surface.

  • Behaviour is what they do.

  • The need is the reason behind the behaviour.

  • The motivation is the deep human driver.

  • Marketing that reaches the deeper layers converts better. Converts means turns viewers into buyers.

  • It also builds a sustainable brand, one that keeps its value over time.

  • Goals, needs and motivations explain why people act.

  • Demographics and behaviour are what you usually observe.

  • The reasons behind them make a message powerful.

  • They usually drive purchases more strongly than demographics or behaviour do.

  • Think of an iceberg. Demographics and behaviour form the visible tip.

  • Needs, tensions, fears and motivations sit underneath, out of sight.

  • Your task is to uncover the layer below what people do.

  • Motivation chain, from visible purchase to underlying need:

    • A commuter buys coffee and a bagel on the way to work. The visible behaviour is the purchase.
    • The need is that they have no time to make breakfast.
    • The fear is being late, which could damage their reputation at work or cost them a promotion.
    • The motivation is avoiding shame, losing control of their life and running short of money.

    Use this chain to move from the visible purchase to the underlying need and motive when building a buyer profile.

  • Status can be a hidden buying driver, even when convenience appears to be the reason.

  • A buyer may choose a premium café because its setting makes them look successful while working. Convenience is not the only reason.

  • Coffee-chain example: people do not buy mainly for the coffee quality.

  • They buy for a pleasant social environment and the status of looking successful while working with a laptop.

  • Proximity (being close by) is secondary.

  • The deeper status and environment need keeps them coming back.

  • Ask why the buyer really buys and how the product fits into their life.

  • The surface action, such as buying a quick snack on the way to work, often hides a deeper motive.

  • Deeper motives include protecting their reputation or sense of control.

  • Deeper motivations matter more than surface demographics.

  • A buyer often wants to avoid a deeper consequence. For example, missing work and losing a promotion.

  • This is not just removing a single pain.

  • Writing copy about that underlying need speaks more intimately.

  • Ask why repeatedly to move from a visible behaviour to the need behind it.

  • Keep asking until you reach a need such as security, status, freedom or belonging.

  • Procedure for finding the real motive behind a purchase. Ask why at each layer:

    1. What does the customer do?
    2. What need does that action meet?
    3. Why does that need matter to them? For example, to protect their reputation or control at work.
    4. What deeper motivation sits underneath, such as identity (who they are), control, belonging or status?

  • Procedure for working the iceberg with an audience:

    1. Record the surface facts about the audience: demographics and observable behaviour.
    2. Ask why they do this behaviour and identify the need it serves.
    3. Ask what they fear will happen if the need is not met.
    4. Ask what deeper motivation drives them, such as control, status, security or growth.
    5. Use the deepest layer to shape the message, without necessarily stating every motive openly in the ad.

  • The deepest motives do not always need to appear in ads.

  • But you must understand the audience closely enough to speak to them at that level.

  • Ads that touch the motive often outperform ads that only describe the behaviour. Outperform means do better.

  • Knowing these motives is what makes a message feel relevant.

Human drivers and motivations

2 min
  • Nine core human drivers push people to buy. A driver is a basic reason that moves people.
    • Power and control over outcomes.
    • Freedom from pain.
    • Conserving or gaining resources (time, effort, money, physical energy).
    • Status and superiority.
    • Connection and belonging.
    • Sexual selection (the wish to attract a partner).
    • Social conformity (keeping up with peers).
    • Personal growth.
    • Providing for loved ones.
  • Map the product to one or more of these drivers.
  • Base human needs to check against an audience include:
    • Power.
    • Control over outcomes.
    • Freedom from pain.
    • Gaining or conserving resources (time, energy, money).
    • Status and superiority.
    • Sexual selection.
    • Survival.
    • Connection.
    • Love and belonging.
    • Keeping up with peers and not falling behind.
    • Growth.
    • Caring for loved ones or having a purpose bigger than oneself.
  • Each need is felt to a different degree depending on the person.
  • Strong messages tie a product to a fundamental human need instead of just describing its function.
  • Common drivers include power, control over outcomes, freedom from pain, and gaining or conserving resources such as time, energy and money.
  • These needs are present in every audience, but weighted differently depending on the person.
  • Other core needs include status and superiority, connection, love and belonging.
  • They also include keeping up with peers, growth, looking after loved ones, and having a purpose bigger than oneself.
  • Buyer motivations often sit in opposing pairs.
  • Examples on the personal side: control versus enjoyment. On the social side: power versus belonging.
  • Decide which pair the product speaks to before writing the message.
  • Opposing motivations create tension that good marketing can address.
  • Identify which side a product serves and speak to that need in the copy.
  • Map both directions for each segment. Buyers move toward a goal they want, or away from a pain they want to escape.
  • Ads and offers work better when they name the specific pain to remove or the specific goal to reach.
  • Negative emotions include fear, shame, guilt, anger, helplessness and grief.
  • Negative emotions narrow attention onto one point. This makes them useful for hooks (the first line or image that grabs attention) and problem agitation (making the problem feel bigger, so the buyer wants a fix).
  • Positive emotions include hope, relief, pride, belonging and love.
  • Positive emotions broaden attention. This makes them useful for presenting the solution and the transformation (the change the buyer gets).

Status, identity and social comparison

1 min
  • People compare themselves to others.
  • They feel envy toward those who have something they lack.
  • They scorn those who lack something they already have.
  • Brands can use this status comparison to position a product as the visible sign of being ahead of others.
  • Status and belonging are powerful because standing within a group affects access to resources and opportunities.
  • Today that means career progression, social acceptance and keeping pace with peers. It does not mean physical survival.
  • Status, attractiveness and social standing are sensitive needs.
  • Phrase them carefully and indirectly, rather than shaming the viewer.
  • The need should be understood without being stated in a crude way.
  • Phrase status, comparison and social claims in a way the customer can accept about themselves.
  • Blunt wording can insult or alienate the audience.
  • Desires can include social status, such as wanting others to see the buyer as cool.
  • Status is a strong angle for copy.
  • Record status next to practical desires.
  • Stopping at the product as the only desire misses the status, identity and pain angles that drive buying.
  • Always list what the customer wants beyond the product.
  • Record the identity the customer wants, meaning who they want to be.
  • For example, a parent may want to be a reliable parent. A host may want to be a relaxed host.
  • A customer who wants to be seen as successful by friends may buy products to project that identity. Project means show to others.
  • A parent buying a new bed for a child may want to be seen as providing the right things and being a good parent.
  • Complete the motivation fields by asking four questions:
    • What do they want to gain?
    • What do they want to be?
    • What do they want to do?
    • What do they want to avoid?
  • The avoid field captures the negative experiences the problem causes. The purchase helps the customer escape them.

Customer avatars: worksheet and fields

6 min

  • Define the ideal customer avatar with a worksheet before setting up the store's structure.

    1. Open a customer avatar worksheet built for your niche. A niche is a small, specific market.
    2. Fill in every field for the person who should land on the site and buy.
    3. Use the answers to decide which visitors to attract and how to convert each of them into a buyer.

  • Define one dream customer with a name, age, relationship status and income level.

  • Choose products that fit her daily life and needs.

  • Check every new product against this customer profile before testing.

  • Example: a dream customer described as a 40-year-old single woman with money.

  • This customer maps to different products by life situation.

    • Home decor for a home-improvement-minded person.
    • Stretch bands for someone who goes to the gym.
    • A relaxed hoodie for evenings at home.

  • Segment customers by use case or buyer type (avatars), not by job title.

  • The best customers may come from very different professions.

  • In the example, the top customers spent over $1,000 per year. They included people from very different fields.

  • Avatars built on use cases were more useful for targeting.

  • Build a bundle or promotion for each main avatar. A bundle is several products sold together in one offer.

  • A business with one customer type may fill in only one avatar column.

  • Keep a separate avatar for each group of buyers if you have different groups, such as B2B (business to business) and B2C (business to consumer).

  • Also keep a separate avatar if customers of different genders have needs that differ greatly.

  • The first pass at the avatar document should contain only what you already reasonably know.

  • Accuracy is not required at this stage. The document is a living resource that grows with each review.

  • Give each customer avatar a name.

  • Team members and agencies can then refer to it quickly. For example, when briefing an ad for a specific avatar.

  • The name can be anything.

  • The customer avatar document is not confidential.

  • Share it with anyone who creates work for you or interacts with your customers.

  • A one-page customer research document can be handed to agencies, freelancers, consultants, and new employees or customer service staff.

  • This way they understand who the customers are without lengthy briefings.

  • Agencies, especially traffic agencies (companies that bring visitors to your ads and pages), will ask about the target market, customer attributes and what customers look like.

  • Use a customer avatar tool, such as a spreadsheet.

    • A Numbers file (Apple's spreadsheet app) or an Excel file (Microsoft's spreadsheet program), on Apple or Windows.
    • A Google Sheets copy (free online spreadsheet from Google) made in your own Google Drive that you can share with a team.

  • Spreadsheets are easier to customize and expand than PDFs. A PDF is a fixed document file, and its formatting is hard to manage.

  • Avatar sheet fields (three avatar slots, one column each):

    Field What to record
    Customer Avatar 1 / 2 / 3 One column per avatar; the sheet has three avatar slots
    Avatar Name Any memorable name, used as shorthand in team discussions
    Age Range Basic demographic data
    Gender Basic demographic data
    Average Income Basic demographic data
    Experts Followed Bloggers, YouTubers, authors, TV personalities, athletes and influencers the customer follows
    Favorite Online Spaces Facebook groups, online forums, Reddit communities, Quora, fan pages (Reddit and Quora are online question and discussion sites)
    Prior Relevant Purchases Other versions of your product, supporting products, accessories, and products bought alongside yours
    Primary Problem(s) The main problems the avatar wants to solve by buying your product; one or several
    Wants / Desires Desires that follow from the primary problems and relate to your product
    Emotions Currently Feeling How the customer feels now about the problem, e.g. frustrated, sad, excited
    Beliefs What they believe will happen if they buy, what they believe about your product, and what they believe about themselves
    What do they want to gain? Outcomes beyond the physical product, such as pleasure or status
    What do they want to be? The identity they aim for
    What do they want to do? The actions they want to take, as specific as possible
    What do they want to avoid (pain)? The pains they are trying to escape

  • Start the profile with basic demographics: age range, gender and average income target.

  • Then move to the deeper fields, such as problems, desires, beliefs and emotions.

  • Record gender separately for each avatar if customer groups differ in ways that matter.

  • Procedure for building or checking avatars:

    1. Check whether you already have customer profiles. If they are current, review them for accuracy and stop there. If not, fill in the full sheet.
    2. Decide how many avatars you need. One avatar is enough if one product solves one problem for one type of buyer. Otherwise make one per distinct buyer type, such as B2B and retail, or different experience levels within retail.
    3. Give each avatar a short, memorable name.
    4. Record age range, gender and average income.
    5. List the experts, bloggers, YouTubers, authors, TV personalities, athletes or influencers this avatar follows.
    6. List prior relevant purchases: other product versions, supporting products, accessories and products bought alongside yours.
    7. List favourite online spaces: Facebook groups, forums, Reddit communities, Quora and fan pages.
    8. Brainstorm the primary problems the avatar wants solved by your product. Several are fine.
    9. Turn each problem into specific wants and desires that relate to your product.
    10. Record the emotions the avatar feels right now.
    11. Record beliefs about what happens after buying, about the product, and about themselves.
    12. Record what they want to gain beyond the physical product.
    13. Record who they want to be.
    14. Record what they want to do, as specifically as possible.
    15. Record what they want to avoid (pain).
    16. Research anything you do not know, and keep the sheet updated as your knowledge grows.

  • Procedure for customer research:

    1. Start with your own knowledge and experience from serving customers, and the signals customers have already given you.
    2. Open the customer avatar spreadsheet and fill in every field you already know or can reasonably estimate. Do not aim for perfect answers.
    3. Give each avatar a name so it can be referenced quickly in team and agency work.
    4. Use Google to research the fields you do not know yet, such as experts, books, magazines, movies and TV, and online spaces.
    5. Fill in the remaining fields: prior purchases, primary problems, wants, emotions, beliefs, and the gain, be, do and avoid questions.
    6. Carry out the exercise within the current month, then keep updating it as you learn more.

  • Record prior relevant purchases, such as other product versions, supporting products and accessories.

  • If your product is an accessory, note which main products your customers already own. For example, which phone models buyers of a phone case use.

  • Fields you cannot answer yet can be left empty and completed later.

  • For passion-based niches, find relevant books and magazines by searching Google for titles about that passion.

  • A half-hearted profile leads to half-hearted marketing and weak results.

  • Treat the exercise as serious research. Look up anything you do not know instead of guessing.

  • Describing the customer as a broad group such as "everyone interested in the activity" is the lazy approach.

  • Dig into sub-groups, since their knowledge, needs and problems usually differ.

Problems, desires, beliefs and motivations in the avatar

2 min

An avatar is a written picture of one type of buyer. Build one for each buyer type.

  • A primary problem is a problem the customer feels that your product or business solves.
  • One avatar can have several primary problems. Write down every one you find, even if the list grows over time.
  • Define primary problems as the main problems the product helps solve.
  • There may be one or several. There is no single right answer.
  • Ask why the customer wants the product. This finds the deeper problem behind the wish.
  • Example: a person who wants a rowing machine may really want to feel fitter after a stressful year.
  • That underlying problem drives the purchase more than the item itself.
  • Wants and needs are different.
  • A customer can want a product without needing it.
  • The want usually comes from an underlying problem, such as a worry about status.
  • Record both the want and the problem behind it.
  • Turn each problem into a desire. A desire is the result the customer wants.
  • Tie the desire to how the product is used, not just to the product.
  • Example: a first-time home cook's problem is making a meal quickly. So the desire is a meal kit that matches their skill level.
  • A belief is something a person holds as true, even without proof.
  • Record beliefs in three areas:
    • What the customer thinks will happen if they buy.
    • What they believe about the product.
    • What they believe about themselves.
  • Customers buy for more than the physical product.
  • List what they want to gain, such as the pleasure of use or social status.
  • Each gain should connect back to their original desires.
  • Record what the customer wants to do.
  • Make it as specific as possible. A precise action, such as mastering one particular technique, gives sharper copy than a vague goal.
  • Core problem digging: for each avatar, collect five kinds of problem. (Digging means searching for the real problem under the surface one.)
    1. Surface problem: how people describe it in public. At least 10 quotes.
    2. Real problem: what they think at 2 a.m. and never say out loud. At least 15 quotes.
    3. Identity problem: the negative labels and self-image the problem created. At least 10 quotes.
    4. Relational problem: strain on partners, family and friends, and social situations they avoid. At least 10 quotes.
    5. Trajectory problem: what they fear becoming, and the worst case they imagine. At least 10 quotes.
  • Target avatar research inputs. Request these before you start:
    1. The brand website or key product information.
    2. Facebook ad account data. (This is the numbers from your Facebook ads.) It includes:
      • Gender split, with spend and performance by gender. (Spend means money spent on ads.)
      • Performance by age cohort. (A cohort is a group of people in the same age range.)
      • Time-of-day and day-of-week spend patterns.
      • Top-performing creative themes. (The main message or look of the best ads.)
      • Geographic concentration, if relevant. (Where buyers mostly live.)

Desires, emotions and distress

2 min

  • A persona is the same as an avatar or an ideal customer profile (ICP). An ICP is a written description of your perfect buyer.

  • The most attributed persona in a competitor's data is the buyer type its ads are credited with reaching most. (Attributed means the ad system assigns sales or results to that group.)

  • Study that persona first.

  • A desire is the result the customer wants, such as fading acne scars, even skin tone or smoother skin texture.

  • Desires work better with an emotion attached.

  • Emotions attached to a desire make the message more effective.

  • Example for an acne product: love or attraction can be the emotion. Acne stops a person from being seen as a partner, and the product is the fix.

  • Broad, non-targeted love messaging can also be strong for acne products.

  • Top emotions in a competitor's data, such as hope and relief, show what to target. They are backed by data.

  • Start with the emotion that has the most attributed ads. Use it as your starting point.

  • Distress means how much pain or stress a person feels. Distress segments are groups sorted by that level.

  • Attention state means how focused and open a person is right now.

  • Message receptivity means how willing a person is to hear a message.

    Segment Intensity range Baseline state Attention Message receptivity
    Low (curious or preventive) 1-4 Mild curiosity, optimization focus Broad, can process positive and negative cues Responds to aspirational and lifestyle messaging
    Moderate (problem-aware) 5-7 Frustration, concern, mixed hope and skepticism Beginning to narrow Needs problem acknowledgment first; wants proof; distrusts overpromising
    High (desperate) 8-10 Chronic shame, fear, desperation, anger, helplessness Severely narrowed Weak to positive cues; needs extensive validation; responds to 'finally, someone who understands'

  • A label is a word a person uses about themselves, such as "I'm lazy." A label-emotion map links each label to a feeling.

  • Make one entry for each of at least 10 significant labels. Each entry has seven parts:

    • The label.
    • The primary emotion (the strongest feeling).
    • The secondary emotions (weaker feelings).
    • The attention state while the label is active.
    • The behaviour it causes: what they do or avoid.
    • Which messages can reach them in that state.
    • The positive label that could replace it.

  • Also map the transition path from each of the top 5 negative labels. A transition path is the route from a negative label to a positive one. Note three things:

    • The trigger (what starts the change).
    • The required proof.
    • The typical duration of the change.

Interviews and surveys

3 min

  • Run customer interviews yourself at the early stage of a new product. Do not hand them to someone else, even if your time is expensive.

  • Raw answers in the customer's own words are the source for headlines and copy.

  • A summary removes the exact phrasing you need.

  • A customer profile is a short record of who the buyer is.

  • Build it with a questionnaire of about 15 questions before any content work. Cover at least these areas:

    1. Demographics: age, sex, race.
    2. What a typical day in their life looks like.
    3. Their biggest pain points.
    4. What they value most.
    5. Their goals.
    6. The shopping experience they expect.
    7. Which magazines and blogs they read.

  • Use the answers for every article, email and image. Phrase content in the customer's own language.

  • Survey procedure for current and future customers. A survey is a set of questions asked to many people.

    1. Ask what they have now and how they use it.
    2. Ask how your product would help them, and what they would need to know before buying.
    3. Run the questions as a survey funnel. (A funnel is a series of steps that narrows people down.) The prospect answers a few questions before getting the offer.
    4. Segment the respondents by their answers. (A segment is a group of people who gave the same kind of answer.) Examples: colour, resistance level, whether they train with a partner.
    5. Only offer a new product or variant to people who already said they want that feature.

  • The 'Ask' approach comes from the book 'Ask' by Ryan Levesque.

  • It means asking customers directly instead of guessing what they want.

  • Perceived value means how valuable the buyer feels something is.

  • Perceived value matters more than the number of extras you add.

  • A message that speaks to the buyer's specific problem makes the same offer feel more valuable.

  • Survey template question. A template is a ready-made sentence to copy and use:

    • "What led you to make a purchase with us?"

  • Customer research for offers:

    1. Survey new customers with the questions in the survey template.
    2. Read the answers. Note what the buyer cared about most when choosing.
    3. Decide what your specific customer would value most.
    4. Write down your own store ideas as you work through the offer steps. Keep the list ready for when you need it.

  • Use the following prompt, unchanged, to pull out the core mass desire. This is the strongest wish many buyers share. The prompt also pulls benefits, pain points, proof and transformation stories from a product description and customer reviews.

    Act as a senior direct-response researcher who receives only two inputs: (1) a product description covering technical details, features and specifications, and (2) customer reviews containing genuine feedback and testimonials. Working exclusively from these two inputs, pull out the core mass desire, the main benefits and features, the customer pain points, the proof points and testimonials, and the transformation stories.

Core mass desire:
1. Study how customers phrase things. Spot emotional themes that recur across the reviews and map the before and after states they describe.
2. From the feedback, answer: What is the deepest emotional driver? Which transformation appears again and again in the reviews? What do customers say they can now do or feel after using the product? Which pain or frustration are they most relieved to be rid of?
3. Phrase the core mass desire in one of these two formats: To [emotional benefit] without [pain point], or To finally [desired outcome] despite [previous obstacle].

Required inputs:
Product description: [insert the complete product description]
Customer reviews: [insert 3-5 detailed customer reviews]

Do not make up any facts that are absent from these inputs.

Identity markers and negative labels

2 min
  • An identity marker is a sign that shows someone belongs to a group. Identity-based products sell to people who share a group identity.
  • Research identity markers in this order:
    1. At least 10 common values of the identity group, with examples.
    2. Visual markers: clothing, accessories, aesthetics.
    3. At least 20 group-specific phrases or terms.
    4. At least 15-20 influencers and authorities the group respects. (An influencer is a person with a large audience.)
    5. At least 10 other brands that market to the identity.
    6. At least 5-7 shared experiences.
    7. The podcasts, YouTube channels and publications the group consumes.
    8. Their aspirations and fears. Also how they signal belonging, which opposing identities they define themselves against, and how the identity has changed over the past 5 years.
  • A negative identity label is a harmful word a person uses about themselves.
  • Collect labels in five categories: body or health, capability or intelligence, relational or social, financial or success, moral or character.
  • At least 10 labels per category.
  • Each label needs:
    • An exact quote.
    • The primary emotion.
    • Intensity, from 1 to 10.
    • Frequency.
    • Origin: self-applied, applied by others, or both.
    • Duration.
    • A source link.
  • An aspirational label is a positive word the person would like to be called.
  • Collect at least 20 aspirational labels. Each needs:
    • The emotion it would trigger.
    • Its distance from the current identity, on a 1-10 scale.
    • What would make them feel they had earned it.
    • What blocks them now.
    • A source link.
  • Avatar research counts. Collect these amounts:
    • At least 20 negative identity labels.
    • 15 aspirational labels.
    • 10 failed solutions, with why they failed and how that felt.
    • 20 failure phrases.
    • 10 skepticism patterns. (Skepticism means doubt.)
    • 10 things the avatar refuses to do, each with the real reason.
    • 25 hurtful quotes across relationship types. That is 5-7 per type: partner, children, parents, friends, coworkers, self.
    • 3-5 secret conversation scenarios. (What they say in private.)
    • 5 quotes in each post-transformation category: supporters, doubters, self, strangers.
    • Top 10 objections, with the real concern under each.
    • 5 label transition paths.
    • 3-5 complete avatars.

Complete avatar profile and transition stories

2 min
  • Write the complete avatar profile in this order. Each section is short.
    1. Demographic snapshot: a short fact sheet with name, age range and gender (both checked against ad data), location and situation, income, and life stage.
    2. Ad data check: share of spend, engagement rate (how many people react, click or comment), conversion rate (how many viewers buy), time patterns, and the creative themes that resonate.
    3. Core problem: surface, real and identity.
    4. Emotional architecture: the layers of feeling. Include:
      • Distress level.
      • Five ranked emotions, each with intensity and quotes.
      • Attention state.
      • Emotional entry point: the feeling where your message can first get in.
      • The emotion that shuts them down.
      • The transition threshold: how far they must move before they change.
      • The permission needed to hope: what they must allow themselves to believe.
    5. Identity profile: primary negative label with duration and origin. Add secondary labels. Add the aspirational label with its distance on a 1-10 scale. End with an identity wound summary in 2-3 sentences. (An identity wound is the hurt tied to how they see themselves.)
    6. Top 5 fears, with quotes.
    7. Relationship impact on partner, children, parents, friends and self. Each needs a hurtful quote.
    8. Failed solution history: 5 items, each with the result and a quote. Refusals: 3 items, each with the real reason and their inner monologue. (Inner monologue means private self-talk.)
    9. The transformation they want:
      • A genie-wish description: 3-5 sentences describing the perfect result, as if a magic wish came true.
      • How their relationships would change.
      • The identity shift.
    10. Top 3 objections, each with the real concern under it. Also list skepticism triggers, and what would make them believe.
    11. Purchase psychology:
      • Emotional trigger.
      • Proof.
      • Risk reversal: a promise that takes the risk off the buyer, such as a money-back promise.
      • Permission.
      • Purchase-trigger emotion: the feeling that makes them buy now.
      • Breaking-point scenario: the moment they can no longer put up with the problem.
    12. Messaging strategy:
      • Emotional sequence: the order of feelings your message creates.
      • Hook approach: the first line that grabs attention. Choose one: problem, mechanism (how it works), identity or aspiration.
      • Tone.
      • Format.
      • Best time to reach them.
    13. A mirror-moment opening line: a first line that shows the buyer their own situation.
    14. A raw monologue: 5-7 sentences of what they would say to a close friend after drinks. Raw means honest and unfiltered.
  • A transition story is a before-and-after story of a person who changed.
  • Follow this procedure:
    1. Write down the negative label the person started with.
    2. Write down the positive label they now claim.
    3. Note what made the change possible.
    4. Record how long the transition took.
    5. Identify the turning point in the story.
    6. Trace the emotional path from the starting state to the end state.
    7. Compare how they describe themselves before and after.
    8. Attach the exact source quote and its link.
  • Collect at least 10 complete transition stories.

Persona brief before scripting an ad

2 min

  • Fill in this persona brief before you write any ad. It is a short profile of your buyer. Psychographics means the buyer's attitudes and inner psychology. Demographics means basic facts like age and income.

    Persona brief to fill in before scripting an ad:
- Demographics: age range, gender mix, income level, education, location.
- Psychographics: attitudes toward life and general psychology.
- Behavior: what the person does in a typical day.
- Goals: what they want more than anything.
- Fears: what they are afraid of not achieving.
- Desires: the outcome or feeling they are after.
- Pain points: the problems blocking that outcome.
- Motivation: the underlying drive behind their behavior.
