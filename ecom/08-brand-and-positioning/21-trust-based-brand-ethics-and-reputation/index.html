Playbook › 8. Brand and positioning

Trust-based brand ethics and reputation

Section 8.21 · 21 of 24 in this chapter · 6 min read

In short

  • Tell the truth in your store. Hidden flaws and misleading claims lose trust fast.
  • Describe products exactly as the supplier defines them, including size and specs.
  • Use only real stories and real reviews. Never make up an origin story or copy reviews from other sites.
  • Protect your reputation. Quick profit is never worth lasting damage.

Honesty in claims and copy

  • Honesty shows in your copy (the words on your store). Readers notice hidden flaws and evasive claims (answers that dodge the question). A candid tone (open, direct, real) about the product builds trust and makes the shopping experience feel real.
  • Describe your business, where it comes from and what you sell in an accurate and open way. When a problem appears, address it honestly instead of hiding it or spinning it (twisting the facts). Trust is the starting point (baseline) for any brand.
  • Do not promote your store with personal or obscure brand claims (claims that are unclear or hard to check). Use direct, factual statements about what the store offers. **
  • Promotional copy (sales text) that claims months of investigation or a secret discovery to justify a set of products reads as fake and wears away trust. Keep product-section headlines literal (plain and exact) and short, and keep them far away from trust elements (things that build confidence, such as charity pledges).
  • Some print-on-demand stores (shops that print a design only after an order comes in) use religious or emotional messaging. Claims of donating 10% to a charity may be false. Check such claims before copying the approach or promoting the brand.

Product accuracy and specifications

  • Misrepresenting specifications (specs: the product's exact size, power and other facts) makes the buyer blame the brand after delivery and harms the supplier's reputation. Example: listing a 48-inch 4K Philips TV as a 50-inch 6K model. (4K and 6K are labels for picture sharpness, called resolution.)
  • Describe every product exactly as the supplier defines it, including size, resolution and other specs. Present the brand as it is.

Sourcing transparency

  • Show full label disclosure (the complete list of what is in the product, printed openly). Explain how to read a label on the site, so customers can check every ingredient and amount themselves. The reasoning is simple: if a customer cannot tell what goes into the body, it may not be a good decision.
  • Start small with transparency steps (openness steps) and increase them as the brand gains confidence. Example sequence from the source:
Stage Action Trigger
1 Publish the label and its contents Start
2 Publish all suppliers After the first roughly 1,000 customers showed demand
  • Transparency about product and sourcing can be used as a long-term defense. Copiers (people who copy your store) may copy formulas (recipes) and marketing, but they usually do not keep up because they are not committed to the same values. Make the brand's authenticity (being real and true to what it stands for) the reason customers stay.

Origin stories and founder claims

  • Do not present an invented origin story (a made-up story of how the product came to be) or a fake inventor in your ads, such as students who supposedly created the product. Claims of this kind damage trust and can get accounts restricted (limited or shut down by the ad platform). Use a true founder story (the real story of the person who started the business) or describe the product's real benefits instead.

  • If you sell someone else's product, use this procedure to get a true backstory (the real history behind the product). **

    1. Look for a backstory on the brand's About Us page (the page that tells who the company is).
    2. If none is published, ask the manufacturer (the company that makes the product) how the product came about and whether they allow you to use the story on your site.
    3. If they agree, put the story on the product page (the page that sells one item) word for word.
    4. Alternatively, ask customers who had a positive experience to share how the product improved their life in a way that matches the main problem of the market.

    The story must be true, but names and personal details may be left out.

Reviews and social proof

  • Only use product reviews (written ratings from buyers) that the supplier gives you permission to use. Never import reviews from other websites or from Amazon. **
  • Product reviews can serve as a source for customer stories, since they show real positive experiences with the product. Stories like these are a form of social proof (signs that other buyers already trust the product). Customers can be asked directly for a story, and it does not need full names as long as it is true. **

Audience and community targeting

  • Running a campaign (a set of paid ads) aimed at a community that does not match the brand's real origin can cause public backlash (strong public anger) and negative reviews. In the documented case, the ads were shut down and the brand kept selling only organically (for free, without paid ads) on Instagram (a photo-sharing app).
  • Customers and critics can often see where a store is actually operated, for example from the visitor's IP address (a number that shows where a connection comes from). If the store's location does not match the community it targets, critics can publicly question its legitimacy (whether it is real and allowed). This can bring negative reviews and force the paid ads to be shut down.
  • Before launching a campaign aimed at a specific group, do the following:
    1. Choose target communities (groups of people you aim at) that you can credibly (truly, believably) serve.
    2. Prepare a response plan (a plan for what you will say and do when criticism comes).
  • Avoid publicly posting store revenue claims (claims about how much money the store earns) or store links for copying in online communities (online groups). People may copy one store without the underlying principles and face risk from that. **

Originality and design

  • Build your store with a unique angle (a distinct way of doing things) rather than copying another store, even when a competitor (rival store) sells in the same niche (a small group of buyers who share one interest). Copying another store's content or layout (how its pages are arranged) is a serious problem. **
  • Copying other brands' designs is a mistake for a clothing brand and should be avoided.
  • Do not display logos (brand symbols) on product images if the physical product does not have them. This creates a trust violation (a broken promise of trust) when customers receive unbranded items. It triggers complaints and chargebacks (when a customer gets their money back through their bank or card company).

Persuasion limits

  • Persuasion techniques (tricks that push people to buy) should only be used for products that reliably deliver the result the copy promises. Psychological triggers (pushes that play on feelings or the mind) can win a first sale. But they cannot keep a product that fails the buyer from being returned (sent back) and reviewed badly.

Business choice and reputation protection

  • Sell only products you believe in, and avoid selling to people who do not need or want them. Trust takes a long time to build and very little time to lose.
  • Short-term profit (money earned quickly) is never worth lasting damage to your reputation (how people see your business). Business made through practices you feel bad about often disappears, because you may not feel you deserve it.
  • A business that creates problems for others, causing as much harm as good or more, is a sign of bad business. Keep the business aimed at solving problems rather than creating them. **
  • The ecommerce (online shopping) dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships directly to the buyer, so you never hold stock) community is very small. A person's reputation travels faster than their ads. Treat every customer as if the whole niche is watching, and never run deceptive or no-ship operations (misleading shops, or shops that take orders but never send the product).
  • Build the brand's network (the people and companies you work with) and reputation through reliability and quality. Be cutting-edge (very new and modern) with designs so they attract attention. Reliable quality is what makes customers and partners return.
