Checking supplier rules before discounting

Section 7.10 · 10 of 28 in this chapter · 12 min read

In short

  • Read each supplier's agreement before you make any discount code.
  • Some suppliers forbid all discounts. Leave their products out of every sale.
  • Show the full price on the product page. Let the customer's code lower it at checkout only.
  • If profit is too thin, skip the discount and offer a free gift or faster shipping instead.
  • The shares below are rough estimates. The brand's own agreement always decides.

Read the supplier agreement before any discount

2 min

Before you create any discount code (a code the customer types in to pay less) or run any promotion, read each supplier's reseller agreement (the contract that lets you sell their products).

  1. Review each supplier's reseller agreement.
  2. Write down which suppliers:
    • prohibit discounts entirely,
    • allow coupon-code discounts (applied after a code is entered),
    • allow no restrictions.
  3. Apply only permitted discounts to products from each supplier.
  • Read each supplier's MAP policy carefully. MAP (minimum advertised price) is the lowest price a brand lets you show for its product. Follow the policy's specific rules before creating store-wide discount codes (codes for the whole store). Some suppliers explicitly prohibit all discount codes in their MAP policy.
  • Check the MAP policy before offering coupons. Many MAP suppliers allow coupons, but some do not. For those products, adjust the exit-intent offer (a pop-up shown when a visitor is about to leave the site). **
  • Whether a discount such as a coupon on a MAP-priced item is allowed depends on each supplier's policy. Read the MAP policy you agreed to with every supplier. **
  • Check each brand's MAP agreement before offering coupons or special discounts. If the agreement forbids coupon codes or special offers on a product, leave those offers off that product. If it is silent, a discount is usually allowed. Still, confirm with the supplier, because they may object later. **
  • Check the policies you signed during approval before running any coupon. **
  • Check the MAP clause in the supplier agreement before offering coupon codes. The agreement states whether coupons are forbidden.
  • Most suppliers allow discounts on MAP products. Confirm this in the authorized retailer agreement (the written approval to sell the brand), because some suppliers forbid discounts in writing.
  • Some supplier agreements forbid coupon codes or discounts entirely. Check the agreement before offering them.
  • Coupon codes are not allowed for a product if the supplier agreement explicitly forbids discounts on it. Check the agreement before offering any discount. **
  • Check each supplier agreement for discount restrictions. Coupon codes must be entered manually by customers (see the section on advertising below).
  • Check the supplier's MAP policy and other costs before launching any bonus or discount on that supplier's products.

Coupon codes: what is normally allowed

2 min
  • Discount codes can be offered to customers unless the supplier agreement specifically forbids them. The discount must not be applied to the advertised price on the website. List the product at MAP. Apply the discount only through a code at checkout (the final payment step).
  • Discount codes are normally permitted under MAP. Example: a 10 percent off code at checkout lowers the price after the discount is applied. This is allowed unless the supplier's MAP agreement says no discounts. In that case, exclude those products from coupons.
  • A coupon applied at checkout is generally fine under MAP, because the advertised price stays at MAP. A few supplier agreements prohibit any discount below MAP. Those products must not be discounted.
  • Most suppliers' MAP policies forbid advertising or showing a product below MAP. A coupon code that lowers the price at checkout does not break this rule, because the advertised price stays at MAP.
  • Discount codes can take the checkout price below MAP unless the supplier prohibits discounts. The advertised price on the product page and in Google Shopping (Google's product listings in search) must stay at MAP.
  • Use promo codes instead of marked-down prices, so the listed price stays at MAP. A coupon discounts the price at checkout unless the supplier agreement specifically forbids coupon codes.
  • MAP is set by the supplier and limits the lowest price at which the product may be advertised. A discount code that lowers the final price is generally not a violation, because the advertised price stays at or above MAP. But read each supplier's agreement, because a few forbid codes. **
  • For a MAP product, run the sale through a coupon code, not by lowering the displayed price on the product. **
  • Usually you cannot offer discounts such as 10 to 15 percent off MAP products. Some supplier MAP agreements forbid coupon codes, which is rare. If the agreement does, exclude those supplier products from your coupon offers. **
  • Discounts that are not advertised are generally allowed under MAP pricing, unless the supplier explicitly forbids them. Advertising a price below MAP is a separate issue and can violate the supplier's policy. **

Conflict on 10-15 percent discounts. The 2019 entry says you usually cannot offer 10 to 15 percent off MAP products. Entries from 2021 to 2025 (,, ) say discount codes are normally permitted as long as the advertised price stays at MAP. Use the later view for checkout codes. The 2019 caution applies only to a supplier whose agreement forbids discounts.

Keep the advertised price at MAP

1 min

Principle. Sell at MAP. Treat any extra offer as a discount off MAP. Do not inflate (raise) the MAP price first to create room for a discount. **

  • List products at MAP. Fund discounts from the margin (the money you keep after costs) that you can afford. Do not list above MAP, because products above MAP lose traction (fewer clicks) on Google Shopping. If a discount is too large, reduce the discount, not the list price. **
  • If the margin cannot cover discounts, list the product at MAP without discount codes for that supplier. Listing a higher price lowers clicks in Google Shopping, because the product appears at the highest price.
  • If you must choose between pricing above MAP with discounts, and pricing at MAP without discounts, choose MAP without discounts. It keeps your prices in line with other sellers on Google Shopping and avoids looking more expensive. **
  • If MAP is the minimum advertised price, discount codes can take the checkout price below MAP. List the product at MAP and apply discount codes to the MAP price. Do not inflate the price and then offer a discount.
  • Under MAP pricing, keep everyday prices at the MAP level. Run promotions as a percentage off that price, such as 5% or 10%. Raising a price and then discounting it back to MAP is an alternative, but it changes the displayed price. **

Conflict on inflating the price. describes raising the price and discounting back to MAP as an alternative., and advise against inflating. Default to listing at MAP. Use the alternative only if you accept that the displayed price changes.

Where prices may appear

1 min
  • Google ads, Facebook ads and strikethrough pricing (an old price crossed out next to the new one) may not show a product below its MAP. Most brands allow discount codes the customer enters at checkout. But these must not be auto-applied (applied by the store without the customer typing) or advertised as the lower price. **
  • Coupon codes must be entered manually by customers. Do not auto-apply discounts at checkout.
  • When a coupon code is allowed, show the full price on the product page. Reveal the discounted price only after the code is entered. **
  • Offer discounts and coupon codes on product pages only where the supplier's MAP agreement permits it. If the supplier forbids discounting below MAP, do not discount those product pages. **
  • For special supplier pricing during a sale, put the discounted products on a separate bundle page (one page that sells several products together). Do not change the price on existing product pages.

Brand-level exclusions

1 min
  • Some suppliers forbid coupon codes that push the price below MAP. If a supplier's agreement says so, exclude that supplier's products from any coupon code that lowers the price. **
  • Do not discount below MAP if the supplier's policy forbids it. **
  • If a supplier forbids discounts or coupons, exclude its products from coupon offers. Then offer a bonus gift instead, so the deal stays attractive without breaking the policy. **
  • Before offering discount codes on a supplier's products, read its MAP agreement. If it forbids discounts, do not discount that supplier's products. Most suppliers, about 90% or more, allow discount codes. **

Store-wide and multi-brand sales

1 min
  • For a store-wide sale, include only brands that agreed to join the promotion. Use the announcement bar's advanced targeting (a thin banner across the top of the site, with rules for which pages show it) to hide it on pages of vendors that are not part of the sale. **
  • Use notification bar promotions (the same kind of top-of-site banner) only for vendors that agreed to the promotion. Show promotions based on conditional rules (if-then rules), not as blanket offers (one offer for everyone, everywhere). **
  • To run promotions across brands with different discount levels: **
    1. Change the promotion banner on each product page to show the offer relevant to that product's brand or collection.
    2. Create a separate coupon code for a brand only if it needs one.
    3. Check the supplier's policy first. Exclude any brand that forbids coupon discounts from the promotion.

Adding value without changing the price

2 min

Principle. When price discounts are not possible, add value instead. Use bonus gifts, extended warranties (a longer guarantee that covers repairs) or expedited shipping (faster delivery). A bonus gift can carry more perceived value (what the customer thinks it is worth) than its cost. Extended warranties can cost little or nothing when arranged with the supplier. **

Principle. Discounts on products that suppliers do not allow to be discounted can be replaced with other value. Examples: expedited shipping, free white glove delivery (workers carry the item in and set it up) or a small bonus gift. Check each option's margin before offering it.

  • If a supplier's MAP policy forbids discount codes, run promotions that do not change the price. Examples: free expedited shipping, or a free bonus gift once a customer spends over $500, $1,000 or $2,000.
  • Bonus gifts, free shipping and upgraded shipping such as white glove delivery do not change the product price, so they do not violate MAP. A brand can still have separate rules outside MAP, so check each supplier's agreement. **
  • A free bonus product from the same supplier can violate MAP. Get written approval from the supplier before running the promotion.
  • For a supplier that forbids discounts, a bonus gift instead of a discount makes the deal more attractive without breaking the policy. **

Check margins before offering a discount

1 min
  • Do not sell at MAP with a 10 percent discount. Net margins (what you keep after all costs) around 15 percent are too low, unless the order value is thousands of dollars. Try a 5 percent discount, a smaller discount, or a bonus gift that costs less than the discount would.
  • Check the margin of each option before offering it.
  • A coupon is paid by the retailer, not the supplier. The discount comes out of the retailer's profit, not out of the wholesale price (what the supplier charges you). The supplier still receives the wholesale price.
  • If the margin cannot cover discounts, list the product at MAP without discount codes for that supplier.

Negotiate supplier-funded promotions

1 min

Principle. If product margins are too thin for discounts or coupons, negotiate with suppliers for promotional support. Propose a promotion where the supplier includes a free item of value, worth $20 to $50 depending on the product price, with customer purchases. **

Rule. When product pricing is fixed and margins are low, build tighter relationships with suppliers. This gives you access to co-promotional opportunities (promotions run together with the supplier). The supplier includes a free premium, upgraded shipping or a discount. You do not have to absorb the promotional costs yourself. **

Example. Buy-one-get-one offers (a customer buys one item and gets a second) were easier to source when suppliers had excess inventory (more stock than they can sell). Today they are mainly worth offering on stock that is overstocked or being discontinued (no longer sold by the supplier), as described in the supplier procedure.

Procedure. Sourcing a supplier-funded deal, such as buy-one-get-one or a deep discount:

  1. Review supplier inventory lists (their stock lists) regularly. Look for SKUs (one product's stock code) with very high stock that are not moving (not selling).
  2. Watch for supplier emails announcing that a SKU is being discontinued.
  3. Ask the supplier which promotional price they can give. For example: a buy-one-get-one price, a 30 to 50 percent discount on the SKU, or a temporary waiver of the MAP restriction for the promotion.
  4. Run the offer only if the numbers show it is profitable for your store.

Competitor offers. Competitor holiday offers (offers from other shops) can be checked with the supplier before you copy them. Send the supplier a screenshot (a picture of the screen) of the competitor's offer. Ask whether it violates policy, or whether the same promotion is available to you.
