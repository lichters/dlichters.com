Scarcity, urgency and price framing

Section 7.19 · 19 of 28 in this chapter · 14 min read

In short

  • Show scarcity (a limited supply) and deadlines only when they are true.
  • Use a real end date. A timer that restarts every day is a trick.
  • Show a higher "was" price next to your price, so the sale looks bigger.
  • Show your price next to a costlier alternative, such as a clinic visit.
  • Never invent stock or deadlines. Buyers stop trusting you, and that trust is hard to win back.

Stock-based scarcity

2 min

Scarcity means a product looks hard to get. Stock means the items you have to sell.

  • Show the limited stock on product pages and in ads (paid promotions). Example: "only 4 units in stock" or "limited supply."
  • Scarcity triggers fear of missing out (FOMO: the worry of missing a good deal). It also raises perceived value (how valuable the buyer thinks it is) and purchase urgency (the feeling they must buy now).
  • Scarcity makes a product look hard to get and more desirable. Phrases such as one-day sale, limited offer, while supplies last, and first come first served signal limited supply.
  • Limited availability also suggests exclusivity (feeling special, as if few people can have it).
  • Scarcity means limiting the quantity of an offer.
  • A fixed stock, such as only five units left, creates pressure.
  • The value disappears once the stock is gone.
  • Anything that looks limited or rare seems more valuable.
  • So limited-stock wording increases demand (how many people want to buy).
  • Show remaining stock next to each variant (a version of a product, such as a color or size) as plain text, such as "only four left."
  • The source recommends this over installing an app for it.
  • State the exact number left, such as 790 coats, 137 sets or 46 sets. Do not just say stock is limited.
  • Specific figures are more believable than vague scarcity claims.
  • Scarcity can be communicated in three ways. State each one plainly on the store:
    1. Limited product supply.
    2. Limited number of bonuses (extra gifts). Example: free shipping only for the next 20 buyers.
    3. A bonus available only until a set date and never again afterwards.

Deadlines and time-limited offers

3 min

  • Urgency (the push to act now) comes from a time limit, not a quantity limit.

  • A set deadline makes customers decide faster. Missing the window means losing the offer.

  • Urgency also comes from a time component (the part of an offer that has a clock). Examples: a limited drop (a short release), a limited edition, or an offer valid only today or this week.

  • The time part triggers FOMO and increases perceived value.

  • Time-limited offers create urgency. Examples: limited-time drops, limited editions, 48-hour sales or deadline-based promotions.

  • The time limits must be clear and genuine.

  • Limited-time offers push customers to buy sooner. This raises the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).

  • Use a clear end date so the urgency is credible.

  • Limiting an offer to a specific time frame or a specific action creates a need to decide now. It beats the habit of delaying.

  • Time-limited offers are used in direct response letters (sales letters that ask for a quick reply) to prompt fast action.

  • Case: one advertiser offered a premium (a free gift) for six packages and proof of purchase, for a single week.

  • Hundreds of thousands of buyers responded. A short, specific deadline drove fast action.

  • Flash sales (very short sales) and limited-time discounts tied to a specific date create urgency. Examples: Black Friday and Cyber Monday (big US shopping days).

  • The discount exists only on that day. Customers feel they cannot buy it earlier or later.

  • Example: a Posture Pro Corrector launch at 20% off the regular $49.99. It is marked as a launch offer with a specific end date ("Offer ends Sunday").

  • The discount is tied to time-limited event positioning (presenting the sale as a timed event). This drives an immediate purchase, rather than comparison shopping (buyers checking other stores first).

  • Show a visible expiry (end date) on the coupon (a discount code), such as "ending soon."

  • Do not offer only an automatic discount. A visible deadline makes buyers act when they see the offer.

  • Use a deadline as the most basic urgency builder, alone or combined with other tactics.

  • Make it visible with a handwritten or rubber-stamped date (a date pressed on with a rubber stamp).

  • On larger print runs, printed or simulated handwriting (a printed font made to look like handwriting) can replace a true stamp.

    • Case: an insurance agent mailed 100 letters a week to cold prospects (people who do not know him yet). He offered a free road atlas (a road map book) for a quote.
    • Adding a rubber-stamped response deadline in red ink raised response (how many people reply) from 1 to 3 percent to 5 to 8 percent.

  • Use time-limited urgency offers such as 5% off, 10% off or free shipping.

  • Show a dynamic countdown (a timer that updates by itself) that ends when the promotion ends.

  • Time-limited offers give buyers a reason to purchase now.

  • A small urgency added to an offer improves conversion. Examples: a limited-time free shipping offer, or a message that the price returns to the usual level within 24 hours.

  • Urgency offer pattern: pair a time-limited perk (free shipping or a discount) with a clear statement. The statement says the regular price or discount returns after a set period, such as 24 hours.

    • Why it works: the deadline pushes hesitant buyers (people who are unsure) to decide now.
    • Examples written for this pattern:
      • "Free express shipping today only, then standard shipping returns tomorrow"
      • "Save 40 percent until midnight, then the usual price applies."

  • Early-bird pricing schedule (a lower price for early sign-ups) for events and courses:

    1. A lower price if enrolled (signed up) by a set date.
    2. A middle price for enrollment after that date but before a later cutoff.
    3. A higher price at the door.

    The same schedule works for advance orders (orders placed before a product ships), new products, event passes, subscriptions (regular paid plans) and renewals (paying again to continue).

  • Example: a 48-hour window, with 20% off shown with a coupon code in the ad. The ad sends the viewer to the product page (the page for one item), where they can buy.

  • The 48-hour window is generally the time the offer runs for that person.

Limited groups, slots and waiting lists

1 min
  • Example: a consultant (an expert you hire for advice) announces a limited number of open client slots (spots for new clients).
  • The consultant warns that the waiting list (the queue of people wanting a spot) will close for years once filled.
  • This combines scarcity with an urgent timing signal, making action feel immediate.

Countdown timers: setup

1 min
  • Show a countdown timer (a clock that counts down to the end) with a message such as "this deal ends in."
  • Activate it by adding a product tag (a label in your store system) that starts with "sale," followed by the end date of the offer.
  • Place it under the buy buttons. Change its background and colors if needed.
  • Add scarcity with a timer in the announcement bar (the thin strip at the top of a website) that shows when the sale ends.
  • A discount with no end point looks less credible.

Countdown timers: keeping them real

1 min
  • Keep the countdown timer and the wording in sync (matching) with the real expiry time.
  • One email showed a 17-hour timer while the text said the offer ends at midnight.
  • The shopper notices this conflict.
  • Countdown timers help conversion only when the deadline is real. Example: a sale that ends at midnight Eastern time (the US East Coast clock) on Black Friday.
  • A timer that resets every day is a gimmick (a trick) and does not help.

Scarcity must be true

1 min
  • Use scarcity and urgency only when they are true.
  • False deadlines or invented shortages destroy trust (the buyer's belief in you). Trust is hard to rebuild.
  • Scarcity claims must be true, because false stock messages damage trust.
  • Mistake: a discount left without a reason or a deadline marks a random dropshipping store (a shop that sells supplier products without holding stock itself).
  • Always name the sale and state when it ends.
  • Mistake: a generic countdown with text such as "ending soon" or "limited supply" adds little scarcity.
  • Tie the urgency to the specific problem the product solves.

Highest number first

1 min
  • When you show numbers, start with the highest figure that is still realistic and defensible (you can prove it).
  • People compare prices automatically. The first number becomes their reference point (the number they judge everything else against). This effect is called anchoring.
  • Example: a consultant once charged $50,000 for a project that generated $7.5 million in sales. He anchored the first quote at $25,000 upfront (paid at the start).
  • The high number was credible because it referred to a real result from a client.

Comparison prices and anchoring

1 min
  • Showing a comparison price (a higher "was" price) next to your price creates a frame (the way information is presented).
  • It makes the discount look bigger and shifts perceived value upward.
  • Framing the price against another price changes how the buyer judges value.
  • Raise the compare-at price (the higher "was" price) to make the discount look larger, as part of a small urgency offer.
  • Pattern: [compare-at price] shown next to [sale price] with a simple discount label.
    • Why it works: the strike-through (a line drawn through the old price) makes the saving obvious at a glance.
    • Examples: "Was 90, now 60: 30 off" and "Was 120, now 60: half off."
  • Compare your price itemized (listed item by item) against what each part would cost separately.
  • Show each item's price and the total. In one set offer, 418 works (items) worth 472.05 dollars at retail (the normal shop price) were sold for 39 dollars, a saving of 433.05 dollars.
  • Formula (a simple calculation): name a higher price the buyer already knows. Show your price below it by a stated amount. Then say why your price is available.
    • Why it works: a concrete comparison to a familiar price is easy to check, so the buyer trusts the saving.
    • Example one: "our 25-pound feed sack costs 6 dollars less than the same sack at the regional farm store, which charges 34 dollars."
    • Example two: "our three-piece tool set costs 45 dollars less than the same set at the hardware chain, which charges 189 dollars."

Price against the alternative

2 min
  • Compare the price to a more expensive alternative that delivers the same result.
  • Do not compare it to a cheaper product in the same category.
  • Example: a $95 recorded program was set against at least $195 for live seminar enrollment (signing up for an in-person event).
  • That cost also includes travel, overnight lodging (a hotel stay) and days away from work. Standard audio albums sold for about $10 per tape.
  • The buyer must see the total cost of the alternative, because that is what makes the price feel small.
  • Show a price against the alternative. A chiropractor (a back and spine care professional) visit at $300 to $500 per month makes a $50 product look like a much better value.
  • Show the cost and time saved, not only the product price.
  • Compared with a monthly chiropractor cost of $300 to $500, a one-time $50 product costs roughly 6 to 10 times less.
  • Showing this gap in the offer makes the value of a low-priced product easier to see.
  • Show the price against professional treatments in the same category. Give a clear price per session (each treatment) or per visit.
  • This makes the product look small next to the alternative. Pair it with the own product price.

  • Price is rarely the real decision factor. But it can stop a sale early.
  • Minimise its impact before the reader sees the number.
  • Decide the price strategy before writing the copy (the sales text).
  • Where price matters a lot, make the comparison work in your favour.
  • Sort offers into monthly payment bands (groups of prices shown as a monthly amount) rather than showing full prices.
  • A car dealer's sale letter grouped vehicles into bands of $199, $249 and $399 per month. The reader compares small monthly amounts, not the total.
  • Use this grouping only when the monthly figures are accurate and the total cost is disclosed (stated) in the offer terms.

Dollar versus percent framing

1 min

The sources disagree on the format of a discount. No unit sets a threshold for choosing between them. So each value is listed with the reason its source gives.

Words used in the table: wholesale price (the price you pay the supplier, before the shop's markup). Retail is the normal shop price.

Format Example wording Reason given When it applies Source
Fixed dollar amount "save 7 dollars with a code" Worked better in the operator's experience. Savings shown as a dollar amount, including codes.
Fixed dollar amount "save $20" Customers can quantify money more easily than a percentage. Savings shown as money.
Round, plain figure "50 off" Avoids making the shopper calculate the saving; calculating adds cognitive load (mental effort). When the discount should be read at a glance.
Exact percentage "50% off" Customers do not want mental math (doing the sum in their head); a clear number removes friction. When a clear percentage is the headline.
Percentage under regular or wholesale price "25 percent under the original price for the same set," or a quarter, a third or half off wholesale Frames the reduction against a known price. Only for reductions you can document.
  • Pattern example for the round form: "Was 90, now 60: 30 off" and "Was 120, now 60: half off."
  • Both values exist in the sources.
  • Choose the format that the buyer can check fastest.
  • Keep the figure consistent across the store and emails.

Per-unit and bulk value

1 min
  • Cost per unit (the price of one item) = album price divided by number of units.
  • Example: a six-cassette album priced at $49.95 to $69.95 works out to about $8.33 to $11.66 per tape.
  • Use the same division to show a buyer that a single item's per-unit price is lower than a bundle's (a set sold together).
  • Show the price as a bulk of value. List how many pieces of information, items or benefits the product holds.
  • Use large, specific numbers such as 1,001 items. The buyer then sees a big bundle for the money.
  • This also applies to physical items. Each nutrient (a substance the body needs) or benefit can be listed.
  • This shows the volume of value packed into a small product.

Cost per day

1 min
  • Express a total price as a small daily cost. Divide it by the number of days the product will be used.
  • Example: a 120-dollar product used for one year costs about 33 cents a day.
  • Keep the period realistic so the figure stays accurate.
  • Some competitor stores frame price as a cost per day. Example: less than 0.60 USD per day, for daily-use products.
  • Use this framing when the product is taken every day and the daily cost is low. (Source does not state a date.)

Quantity saved

1 min
  • For a concentrated product (a stronger product), state the saving in quantity used, not in price per container (the bottle or box).
  • If the product is three times as strong, the buyer needs only one-third of the usual amount.
  • The cost per use falls even if the sticker price (the price tag) is unchanged.

Return on investment

1 min
  • Show return on investment (ROI: money gained compared to money spent).
  • This means presenting the offer as selling money at a discount.
  • Frame the ROI as dollars made. Example: a course that produced net gains (profit after costs) for many users.
  • Or frame it as dollars saved. Example: a newsletter that saves more than its cost in taxes.
  • State the figures so the buyer sees the cost as small.
  • Formula: ROI in a sales letter = gain divided by cost, expressed as a percentage.
  • Example: a $1,000 income gain for a $199 course reads as roughly a 500 percent return.
    • Exaggerate the claim first. Then bring it down to a conservative (careful, lower) figure, such as saying the claim is only half right.
    • This signals honesty.

Free offers paired with the same product

1 min
  • Offers framed as free tend to convert well. Examples: buy-one-get-one or a free gift.
  • This works best when the paired item is the same product as the one being bought.
