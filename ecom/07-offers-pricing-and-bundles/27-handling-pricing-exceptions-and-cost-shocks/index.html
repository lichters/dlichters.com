Handling pricing exceptions and cost shocks

Section 7.27 · 27 of 28 in this chapter · 6 min read

In short

  • Raise your prices when a supplier raises its prices, but only where the margin is too small.
  • Never raise prices above MAP (the lowest price the brand lets you show) to pay for shipping.
  • Never give discounts for late orders. Send a small free gift instead.
  • Do not depend on one supplier. Keep finding more.
  • Test a new country before you sign up for its sales tax (VAT).

Price Changes and Supplier Cost Increases

Tariffs and Wholesale Increases

  • Tariffs (import taxes) can raise two things: the wholesale cost (what you pay the supplier) and the MAP (Minimum Advertised Price, the lowest price the brand lets you show publicly).
  • Your margin (profit per sale) can stay the same.
  • But a sudden big price jump can make customers stop buying. This is called sticker shock (shock at the new price tag).
  • MAP is usually a fixed multiple (a set number times) of the wholesale cost.
  • When wholesale rises, MAP rises too.
  • Then every seller of that product raises prices.
  • Rising wholesale prices also raise MAP.
  • A wider gap (spread) between wholesale and MAP helps cover extra costs.
  • So pass cost increases on to every supplier's products at once. **
  • When supplier prices rise, MAP rises too. This widens your margin.
  • Update your prices right away when a supplier changes them.
  • Push the ad return, meaning try to get more back from your ads.
  • Add paid upsells (extras the customer pays for), such as expedited shipping (faster delivery) or white glove shipping (delivery with setup inside the home).
  • Put more ad spend behind the high-margin products (products that earn the most per sale). **
  • Instead of showing tariffs as a separate line, add a percentage of the expected tariff cost into the sale price.
  • When a supplier publishes a new MAP policy (its rules for the lowest allowed price) with new prices, update your listed prices to match.

Adjusting Only the Affected Suppliers

  1. Before raising prices, find the suppliers where the gap between your price and wholesale is too small to cover shipping.
  2. Change prices only for those suppliers, not every product on the site. Most suppliers leave enough margin.

What Not to Do Under Shipping Cost Pressure

  • Do not respond to shipping cost pressure by doing any of these:
    • Do not raise prices above MAP.
    • Do not add a large shipping fee when competitors do not.
    • Do not drop the brand first.
  • These moves can make the products impossible to sell.
  • Then the money spent on their ads is wasted.

Worked Example: Free Shipping and Margin

  • Free shipping means you pay the higher carrier rates (what shipping companies charge) out of the product margin.
  • So margins shrink, even when the selling price stays the same.
  • Waiting for suppliers to raise their wholesale prices only partly closes the gap.

Supplier Dependence

Concentration Risk (Too Much Depends on One Supplier)

  • One store made about 80 percent of its sales from one main supplier.
  • That supplier raised its sale prices to cover shipping increases.
  • The store's margin fell from about 20 percent to 10 percent.
  • The supplier also started selling directly to customers.
  • The response discussed had three steps:
    1. Find additional suppliers over time.
    2. Lower your Google Ads bids (the price you pay per click) as a short-term step. Still aim for at least break-even (sales that cover all costs).
    3. Build bundles (sets of items sold together) on low-margin products.

Stock Shortages and Substitution

  • When the original item is on backorder (ordered but not in stock yet), offer a similar, cheaper item that is always in stock.
  • Tell the customer about the lower price.
  • This can raise margin when the substitute brand is less known, but customers still accept it.
  • Example: A supplier often had backorders on one item.
  • The store offered a similar item that was always in stock and cost less: about $700 instead of $1,000.
  • Customers who wanted the original got the price difference refunded (about $200 to $300).
  • The substitute shipped in about three days.
  • Many customers accepted the substitute. Its margin was much higher, because the brand was less known.

Mispriced Orders and Supplier Outreach

Handling a Mispriced Order (an Order Placed at the Wrong Price)

  1. Check your supplier's current stock and pricing. Confirm whether the model is discontinued (no longer made). Also check if the stock is a phantom unit (stock the inventory system shows but does not really have).
  2. Email the customer quickly. Explain the stock or price problem, with a clear, polite apology.
  3. Offer either the corrected price or a cancellation with a full refund, based on what your policy allows.
  4. Ask the supplier to renegotiate the price. Use the dealer cost (the supplier's price to you) and the mismatch as the reason.
  5. Keep the full email thread saved. Check the status again until the order is closed.

Customer Email Template (Corrected Price)

Hi {customer_name},

Thank you for your order of {product_name}. We have just discovered that {reason, for example: this model has been discontinued by the manufacturer or the item is no longer in stock}, and the price shown at checkout was entered incorrectly. The correct price is {corrected_price}.

You can either keep the order at this corrected price, or we can cancel it and refund you in full. Please let us know your choice by {deadline_date}.

We apologize for the mistake and appreciate your understanding.

{your_name}
{store_name}

Supplier Email Template (Renegotiation)

Hi {supplier_rep_name},

We have received order {order_number} for {product_name} at {price_paid}. The current price on your list is {list_price}, which means we are losing money on this order. Could you work with us on a revised price of {target_price}?

If we are not able to agree on a new price, we will have to cancel the order, but we would much prefer to keep working with you.

Thank you for your help.

{your_name}
{store_name}

Accepting Losses

  • Sometimes you must take a loss (sell below cost) on a mispriced unit. This is unavoidable.
  • A loss on a discontinued brand can be small, such as about $50.
  • Accept that these cases are part of selling.
  • But try to negotiate first, before you take the loss.

Shipping Delays and Customer Retention

Gifts Instead of Discounts for Delayed Shipments

  • Never offer discount codes for delayed shipments.
  • Instead, put a free product in the parcel. Its real cost is $1-2, but customers see it as worth $15-20.
  • This creates gratitude and repeat purchases (customers buying again).
  • A 10% discount for late orders fails.
  • Customers ask why they would reorder from a late supplier.
  • Free gifts create emotional value (a good feeling) and willingness to buy again.

Concessions for Unhappy Customers

  • Offer a discount or credit (money back on the account) to keep an order when a customer is unhappy.
  • Only do this while the remaining margin stays comfortable.
  • A small concession, such as an extra $50 off or an additional 3%, can be better than losing the sale entirely.
  • A discount that keeps a customer is better than making no money at all.
  • Compare the cost of the concession with the loss of a cancelled sale.
  • Choose a concession that still leaves a healthy profit margin.

Escalating Resolution for a Delayed or Unhappy Order

  1. Offer a first, smaller credit, such as $25, to keep the order.
  2. If the customer still wants to cancel, offer a larger credit, such as $50.
  3. Keep the total concession within a margin you can afford, so the order still makes a profit.

VAT and Market Entry

Testing a Market Before VAT Registration

  • Think about VAT (value added tax, a sales tax in countries such as the UK) before you sell there.
  • With VAT added, customers may face an extra cost at delivery.
  • They may then return the product.
  • Test demand first. Then decide if VAT registration (signing up to collect and pay VAT) is worth it.

Test a market with VAT in this order:

  1. Test demand in the market first, without VAT changes.
  2. Calculate break-even (the sale price where you lose nothing) including the extra VAT, for example 20%.
  3. Run ads only to that market, separately.
  4. If the result does not exceed the higher break-even, stop the ads for that market.

Pricing Checks

Free Products From Missing Prices

  • Shopify (the online store software) lets a product with no price be sold for free.
  • Check every product price against the supplier's pricing.
  • Customers may believe a free order is real and dispute it.
  • Pricing errors also need checking. **
