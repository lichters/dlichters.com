What an offer is and how buyers judge value

Section 7.1 · 1 of 28 in this chapter · 13 min read

In short

  • An offer is the whole deal a shopper sees, not just a discount.
  • Make the offer worth more by raising its benefits and cutting the time and effort the buyer needs.
  • A simple, clear offer sells better than a complicated one.
  • Lowering the price is a weak tool. Add value first.
  • The offer is about 40% of whether a visitor buys.
This section has 7 parts. Tap one to open it.

Definition of an offer

2 min

  • An offer is the full set of what a visitor is shown to convince them to buy. A visitor is a person looking at your shop.

  • A discount may be part of the offer. It is not the whole offer.

  • Anything that makes the buyer more likely to say yes is part of the offer. Examples are a bonus (a free extra), a bundle (several products sold together) and a guarantee (a promise to protect the buyer).

  • An offer is more than a product on sale at a discount.

  • In marketing, an offer is the full package a buyer sees when deciding whether to buy from you. The package has the product, the business behind it, and everything that persuades them.

  • A discount or bundle is only one possible part of that package.

  • A complete offer has nine elements:

    • marketing messages for the product (the words and pictures that sell it)
    • The product itself
    • The product price
    • shipping or delivery
    • a USP package (see below)
    • risk reversal (protection if something goes wrong)
    • customer service
    • trust factors (signs you can be trusted, such as reviews)
    • bonuses with value delivery or perceived value (extras that feel worth something)

  • Resellers (who sell other companies' products) compete mainly on the business. Own-product brands (who make their own product) compete more on the product.

  • A total offer includes:

    • The product and its benefits
    • The marketing message
    • The price
    • The unique selling proposition (USP, the one thing that makes your product different)
    • risk reversal (protection if something goes wrong)
    • shipping or delivery arrangements
    • customer service
    • trust factors such as reviews
    • any special offer, such as a discount, sale price or free gift

    A discount alone is only one small part of the offer.

  • The USP package is how the unique selling proposition is shown on the page. It can overlap with other offer elements, such as risk reversal. It can also be entirely separate.

  • The offer is more than the price. Branding (how your brand looks and feels), the unique selling point, the bundle or BOGO structure (BOGO means buy one, get one), and the perceived value (how valuable it feels) of the whole deal all shape it.

  • Add-to-cart rates depend on these elements. The add-to-cart rate is the share of visitors who click to put an item in their basket.

  • A weak offer is often the first reason a landing page does not convert. A landing page is the single page a visitor arrives on. Converting means a visitor becomes a buyer.

  • Customers buy an offer, not just a product. The same item can look far more valuable with a free bonus, free shipping and quantity discounts. The item and its base price (its normal price) do not change.

  • The offer must be relevant to the buyer's problem. High perceived value does not require adding more items. Speaking directly to the prospect's (possible buyer's) situation raises perceived value more than a generic "buy this" message.

The value equation

3 min
  • Customer perceived value follows a ratio (one number divided by another).
    • The dream outcome and perceived likelihood sit in the numerator (the top number).
    • The time delay and effort and sacrifice sit in the denominator (the bottom number).
    • This ratio determines purchasing decisions. All four components influence whether a customer buys.
  • Sum form (the factors are added up): (Dream outcome + Likelihood of achieving it) / (Time required + Effort and sacrifice)
  • Product form (the factors are multiplied): Value = (Dream outcome × Perceived likelihood of achievement) / (Time delay × Effort and sacrifice). Raise the first two factors and shrink the last two to make an offer more valuable. For a product, show customers how little time and effort they need.
  • The worked example in uses the sum form.
  • The four factors, judged from the buyer's point of view:
    • Dream outcome: what the buyer wants to become.
    • Perceived likelihood: how possible the buyer believes it is to reach that outcome.
    • Time delay: how long it takes.
    • Effort and sacrifice: what the buyer must invest.
  • Dream outcome examples: a necklace promises looking stylish and expressing identity. Fitness promises a great body. Identify your product's dream outcome. Then communicate it throughout the offer structure, naming and product description.
  • Build perceived likelihood through high-quality customer reviews, before-and-after photos, testimonials (written praise from customers) and comparative evidence (proof that shows results).
  • Worked example: a gym membership scores (100) / (50) = 2. Supplements for the same goal score (100) / (10) = 10. The outcome promise is identical. But supplements have 5x the perceived value because they require less time and effort.
  • Gyms and supplements sell the same dream outcome. Supplements win larger demand because they promise faster results with almost no effort.
  • A six-pack through training can take two to three years or more. That makes the time delay and effort very high.
  • Numeric check: a numerator of 10 divided by a denominator of 2 gives 5. Lowering the denominator while keeping the numerator high raises the result.
  • As time and effort approach zero, perceived value approaches infinity (it has no upper limit). Sources differ on how this compares with the other levers:
    • Cutting time and effort matters as much as strengthening the promise.
    • This asymptotic multiplier (a multiplier that keeps growing as time and effort shrink toward zero) drives purchasing decisions more powerfully than improving the dream outcome or the likelihood of success. Products that need minimal time and effort will substantially outperform identical-outcome products with more friction (anything that slows the buyer down).
  • Shorten the time delay by promising one to two weeks of delivery instead of months. Fast competitors such as Amazon can offer same-day shipping, so the gap matters.
  • Sales copy (the words on your page) should shrink the time, effort and sacrifice in the buyer's eyes. For example, show a simple three-step process. This makes a high price feel justified.
  • Buyers pay for the value they expect to get. If the offer delivers little perceived value, selling and convincing people to spend money on the store becomes very difficult.
  • Buyer types differ:
    • Impulse buyers (who buy fast, on the spot) purchase quickly from a clear offer.
    • Comparison buyers check other stores.
    • Facebook and TikTok ads draw attention. Google Ads (paid ads on Google search) and comparison-shopping buyers (who compare prices across shops) look for the best deal.
    • The store offer has to convince both types.
  • Before launching a landing page or offer, ask whether you would buy it yourself at that price with that offer. Ask why someone else should. Add as much value as possible until the answer is yes.
  • Perceived value created through brand, presentation and marketing angles lets a product sell at a much higher price than its cost suggests. Prefer products with high perceived value potential.

Making the offer valuable (CURVE and bonuses)

2 min
  • A CURVE offer stands for Clear, Unique, Risk-free and Valuable. Each letter is a test the offer must pass. Review the offer against all four.
  • Valuable: the customer perceives more value or benefit than from competitors. Improving one element while keeping the others equal can make the whole offer more valuable.
  • Unique: position the total offer so it is perceived as unique within the market (all shops selling this kind of product). Position means where it sits in the buyer's mind. A different offer from competitors raises conversion more than changing a button colour. An average presentation can sell a great offer. But a poor offer cannot be saved by a great presentation.
  • Clear: simple offers convert best. Shoppers spend mental effort understanding complicated deals. A complex offer such as "50% off your third item" loses buyers. A simple, obvious deal makes customers act quickly.
  • An offer works best when the buyer would feel unreasonable refusing it. This usually comes from a clear saving or added value tied to their problem.
    • A bonus that duplicates the main product adds little. A buyer who needs one item does not want a second one.
    • A complementary product (one that works with the main product) solves a related problem and feels more valuable. Example: an organiser for a wallet buyer.
  • Bundles and products with extra value increase the perceived value of the offer. Examples are a case or accessories that help the buyer complete the job the product solves.
  • Perceived value makes a product look more valuable than a competitor's without a large product change. Ways to add it:
    • bonus ebooks
    • a coupon code (a code the buyer types in to get a discount)
    • a lifetime guarantee
    • bundling the product with a smaller item from the supplier
  • Irresistible offers maximise perceived value so the customer feels they are getting a bargain. Tools include discounts, free gifts, free resources, money-back guarantees and bundles that stack value (add up to a bigger total value).
  • Discounts raise conversion rates because a visitor perceives a discount as a valuable offer. This is the underlying reason behind most promotion types used to increase sales.
  • Explain the cost to develop the offer, such as expert teams, lab testing and the number of repetitions (times the product was made or tested). This turns a feature list into a story of value. Identical features and benefits written with development detail sound more valuable than the same product described briefly.

Offer types

3 min
  • Terms used in the offer type tables:
    • Sitewide means across the whole store.
    • A SKU (stock keeping unit) is one exact product version, such as one size and one colour.
    • Tiered means the discount grows as the order grows.
    • BOGO means buy one, get one.
    • A mystery box is a bundle of unknown contents, sold as a "trust us" pack.
    • Upsell means offering a higher-priced version after checkout (the payment step). Cross-sell means offering a related product after checkout.
    • A warranty is a promise to repair or replace the product. A lifetime warranty lasts as long as the buyer owns it.
    • A loyalty program gives points or perks to repeat customers.
    • A cut-off date is the last day a deal or fast shipping is available.
    • White glove delivery is premium delivery service, such as delivery plus setup.

  • The offer must fit the product, the audience and its price point (the price level). For low-ticket items (cheap products), a free second item can be affordable because the extra cost is small. Weak offers such as BOGO at 20 percent off are seen as boring and ineffective.
  • Offers improve ad conversion. A brand running a promotion can afford to spend more on Meta ads (Facebook and Instagram ads) than a competitor selling the same product without one. It can still win the customer.
  • Offers reduce customer acquisition cost (CAC, the cost to win one new customer) and lift revenue (income). They clear slow inventory (stock that sells slowly). They create urgency (pressure to act now) and generate buzz and word of mouth (people talking about it).
  • The main goal is revenue growth. It is driven by scarcity (few units or little time left), urgency, and the confidence buyers feel while a promotion runs.
  • Offers can roughly double revenue and make forecasting (predicting future sales) harder. A well-matched offer can cause a sharp jump in sales.
  • Plan for offers as a regular growth lever (a tool to grow sales), not an occasional event.
  • Strong offers are the primary lever for scaling (growing sales). A well-designed offer combined with existing ad quality and conversion rates can increase ROAS (return on ad spend, money earned per money spent on ads) from 2x to 4x overnight. It can also raise CVR (conversion rate, the share of visitors who buy) from 1.5% to 2.5%.

The no-brainer gap

1 min
  • A no-brainer offer (so good that buying is an easy choice) creates a large gap between perceived value and price. This is achieved by adding a lot of value, lowering the price, or both. Focus on increasing value. A strong offer can make even average copy convert.
  • Value stack (adding up the stated value of every item): total value = sum of the stated value of each item in the offer, shown as value rather than price. The final price is a fraction of that total. The gap between total value and final price makes the offer feel irresistible. Example: items with a stated total value of $12,000 offered for $990 show a gap of about twelve times the price.
  • Bundle build-up: list every component of a bundle with its standalone price (the price when sold alone). Add up the total hard value. Then show a much lower total price. Name, value and state each bonus as free. Example: a training package whose parts were valued at over $5,200 but priced at $3,895 for a 24-month contract, with bonuses listed as free.
  • Anchoring (showing a higher reference price first, so your price looks lower): build perceived value by anchoring a higher price against a comparable product that offers a similar result. Example: a hedge fund strategy costing six figures (a price of $100,000 or more). Your price then looks like a good deal.
  • Scarcity (few units left), by limiting the number of units available, raises perceived value and adds urgency.

Why price alone is a weak lever

1 min
  • Price is part of the offer. But competing only on price is a weak strategy. If the rest of the offer matches competitors, a slightly lower price can make the total offer stronger.
  • Cutting the price to attract buyers makes the product look cheap. Shoppers assume the best products in a category are not the cheapest.
  • A lower price lowers perceived value and can reduce sales. This is why many businesses price themselves out of the market.
  • Never build a store around the lowest price. Raise the price by increasing the value you provide. Higher prices often signal higher quality and build trust.
  • Price should match what the buyer believes the product is worth. Compare against what competitors charge for similar products. Aim to make the buyer feel they get more for a higher price. Products with high perceived value include electronics, advanced products and items made from premium materials.
  • Sources differ on when to price competitively (at or below rivals):
    • Very simple products, such as a basic knife, cannot gain much perceived value. Price them competitively.
    • Products with several features or benefits can justify higher prices.
  • Price shock: a price noticeably higher than competitors', or unfamiliar pricing, can lead to abandonment (the buyer leaves without buying). Check product pricing against the market (what similar shops charge). Make the offer attractive before lowering the price.

Further details

1 min

  • The 40-40-20 principle (credited to Ed Meyer, adapted for e-commerce) splits the chance of conversion this way:

    • 40% from reaching the right audience at the right time (traffic, meaning visitors)
    • 40% from the offer
    • 20% from how the offer is presented (website design)

    Most people focus only on the 20% presentation part.
