Playbook › 7. Offers, pricing and bundles

Unit economics and margin math

Section 7.3 · 3 of 28 in this chapter · 27 min read

In short

  • Add up every cost of a sale (product, shipping, payment fees, ads, fixed costs, refunds) before you set any price.
  • Keep the product cost at or below half of MAP (the minimum advertised price, the lowest price the brand lets you show), and keep ad cost per sale at or below 10% of MAP.
  • Check shipping before you list heavy or bulky items, because freight can wipe out the profit.
  • Keep ROAS above break-even. ROAS (return on ad spend) is the sales money you earn per ad dollar. Below break-even, every sale loses money.
  • Set the selling price so that 70% of the price is left after all costs of the order. This is the main target.
More key points
  • If 70% is not possible, 67% is the lowest acceptable margin.
  • Every discount, free gift or bundle must still make a profit after product cost, shipping and ad spend.
  • Fill every cell in the calculator, even with 0, or the results show errors.
  • Before you list a product, check that net profit (money left after all costs) is above the threshold.

The full cost stack comes before any price

2 min
  • Before you set a price, list every cost for each sale and for each month.
  • The costs are:
    • The total supplier price (product plus shipping),
    • payment fees,
    • customer acquisition cost (what ads cost to win one new customer),
    • fixed costs (costs that stay the same each month),
    • a reserve for returns (money set aside for refunds).
  • Only then do you know two things: the break-even point (the sales level where you neither gain nor lose) and the margin each sale needs. The margin is the money left from a sale after its costs.
  • A price that ignores payment fees, acquisition cost, fixed costs and refunds can turn a sale that looks profitable into a loss.
  • Check the full cost stack before you trust a sticker price (the price tag shown).
  • Acquisition cost matters only for paid traffic, meaning visitors you pay ads to bring.
  • If most customers come through word of mouth, acquisition cost has little effect on pricing.
  • If you buy traffic with paid ads, every price must cover acquisition cost on top of product cost and fees.
  • Example: a product costs $10 from the supplier and sells for $15.
  • That leaves too little room to cover acquisition, fees, returns and profit.
  • With paid ads, a gap that narrow is very hard to make profitable.
  • Use a profit calculator (a tool that works out profit from your numbers) before you create any offer.
  • Use it to:
    • calculate profit margins,
    • set new ROAS and CPA targets (CPA is the ad cost per new customer),
    • check that the offer stays profitable at scale (when you sell a lot).
  • Verify it stays profitable at scale.
  • Reduce unit economics (the money made or lost on one order) aggressively:
    • product cost,
    • operating expenses (running costs),
    • fulfilment cost per order (the cost to pick, pack and ship each order).
  • Every dollar saved per unit becomes budget for advertising, which is the core lever for scaling (growing sales).
  • Wholesale means COGS (cost of goods sold). COGS is only the product price you pay the supplier.
  • Shipping is a separate cost and is not included in wholesale.

Profit per sale formulas

2 min
  • Net profit per sale is what you keep after all costs.
  • Formula: net profit per sale = MAP − COGS − ad budget per sale − shipping cost.
  • Keep COGS at or below 50% of MAP, and the ad budget per sale at or below 10% of MAP.
  • Profit per sale = selling price (at or above MAP) − wholesale cost − shipping cost (if shipping is free) − advertising cost per sale.
  • Keep advertising cost within about 10% of the selling price.
  • Profit per sale = selling price − wholesale price − shipping cost − payment processing fee (the charge the payment service takes) − advertising cost per sale.
  • Heavy items with high shipping costs need this check before the product is listed, because margins can be very thin.
  • Check this before the product is listed.
  • Profit per order = selling price − supplier wholesale cost − card processing fees (about 2.9%) − advertising cost per sale − shipping cost, if you offer free shipping.
  • Older advice (2021).
  • Net profit per sale = MAP − wholesale − average shipping cost − ad cost per sale.
  • Example: MAP $1,000, wholesale $500, shipping $150, ad cost $100.
  • That gives $250 profit, a 25 percent net margin.
  • Profit before shipping per sale = MAP − wholesale − ad spend (up to 10% of MAP) − payment fee (2.9% of MAP).
  • Example: MAP $1,000 and wholesale $500 leave about $371 before shipping.
  • That is after $100 of ad spend and a $29 fee.
  • Profit before ads = sale price − wholesale cost − shipping cost − Amazon fees (including the referral fee, the commission Amazon takes per sale).
  • Example: $19.97 − $3.00 wholesale − $1.50 shipping − $6.40 Amazon fees = $9.07 profit before ads.
  • The Amazon referral fee in this example is 15 percent of the sale price.
  • Net margin = (sale price − wholesale − shipping − ad cost per sale) ÷ sale price.
  • Example: $1,000 − $500 wholesale − $150 shipping − $100 traffic (visitors) leaves $250.
  • That is 25%, below the 30% target used in this handbook.
  • Older advice (2019).
  • Break-even price for a bulky product (large and heavy) sold with free shipping:
    • minimum selling price = COGS + average shipping cost + other per-order costs.
    • Other per-order costs are customer acquisition, card processing fees, traffic and the seller's time.
  • If the price only equals COGS plus shipping, net profit is $0 before those other costs.
  • So every sale loses money once they are added.

Break-even ROAS and CPA

2 min
  • Break-even ad spend per day = number of sales needed × average order value (AOV, the average amount one customer spends).
  • Equivalent: sales needed per day = daily ad spend ÷ average order value.
  • Break-even cost per purchase = selling price − (product cost + shipping cost).
  • Example: a $39.90 product with a $17 fulfilment cost yields a margin of about $22.
  • Check: $39.90 − $17 = $22.90, so the source's $22 is rounded down.
  • With $22.90, the break-even ROAS is 1.74, not 1.77.
  • Break-even ROAS = selling price ÷ margin before ads.
  • Example: $39.90 ÷ $22 gives about 1.81 in the source's own $22 margin. $39.90 ÷ $22.90 gives 1.74. The source states 1.77.
  • Ad campaigns must keep ROAS above break-even to make money. Below it means a loss.
  • Break-even ROAS is the return on ad spend at which ad revenue only covers product cost and fees.
  • Example: product cost $18 and selling price $39.99 give a break-even ROAS of 1.85 in the Dropship Toolkit (a tool).
  • Product cost alone gives $39.99 ÷ $21.99 = 1.82, so the 1.85 depends on the tool's fee assumptions.
  • If actual ROAS is above break-even, the product makes money.
  • If it is below, raise the price or choose a product with a lower cost of goods.
  • Break-even ROAS in the calculator = total revenue ÷ margin $.
  • Buy 1 gives $43 ÷ $32.50 = 1.32.
  • Buy 2, get 1 free gives $83 ÷ $60.30 = 1.38.
  • Target cost per acquisition (CPA) must be below break-even cost per purchase.
  • Example: a product sold at $30 that costs $10.
  • Acquire each customer for under $20. That difference is the margin available for advertising.
  • At the start, the margin of $20 is calculated without advertising expenses.
  • Other costs such as apps, Shopify and hiring are considered later.
  • Once a product has run for more than a month, reserve about 5 to 10 percent of product plus shipping cost for these.
  • For a store with several price points, calculate break-even ROAS from the lowest-priced product at the start.
  • After about 100 orders, switch to the actual average order value and the average cost of product plus shipping.
  • Work out break-even and selling price before setting the campaign structure (how you organize your ads).
  • Example: for a $40 product, the maximum CPA is around $15-$18.
  • Ad budget ceilings: keep ad cost per sale at or below 10% of MAP.
  • The 10% rule is a budget ceiling (a maximum) for net-profit planning.
  • Break-even CPA is the point where a sale stops making money.
  • These answer different questions, so check both.
  • Margin cushion: do not run a store with margins so thin that one day of ad account neglect could lose money.
  • Keep enough room in each product's margin to absorb mistakes and ad fluctuations (ups and downs in ad results).

Gross margin

1 min
  • Markup (the extra added on top of cost) to gross margin:
    • 43% markup on wholesale gives about 30% gross profit.
    • 54% markup on wholesale gives about 35% gross profit.
  • Gross margin per unit = selling price − supplier cost, before transaction fees, shipping and advertising.
  • Example: a product sold at $38.99 with a supplier cost of about $20 leaves roughly $19 per unit before those other costs.
  • Compare products by profit per order, and by how many orders the same total profit requires.
  • Do not compare by margin percentage alone.
  • A $50 product with $20 profit has a 40% margin.
  • A $3,000 product with $900 profit has only a 30% margin, but it earns 45 times more per sale.
  • Older advice (2021-22).
  • A typical dropship net margin is about 30% of total sales revenue.
  • Business margins: about 23% after negotiating volume discounts with suppliers.
  • A margin of 25% or higher is the target for gross margin on products.
  • Products with thinner margins often cannot carry the cost of paid ads.
  • Older advice (2021).
  • Gross margin in the calculator:
    • Margin $ = Total Paid − Total Cost For Us.
    • Total Cost For Us = COGS + Pick N Pack (a fee per order for picking and packing) + Shipping + Gateway.
    • Margin % = Margin $ ÷ Total Paid.
  • The margin is therefore after product, fulfilment, shipping and gateway fees.
  • It is before ad spend.
  • The 70% profit margin threshold for slight price cuts below competitors does not say whether it is gross or net.
  • Calculate it only after COGS and ad spend are subtracted, so the number reflects what you actually keep.

Net margin

1 min
  • Net profit margin after all costs must be at least 20%, and aim for 30%.
  • Costs include cost of goods, shipping, ads, Shopify fees, merchant fees and other expenses.
  • Older advice (2021).
  • Target an average net profit margin of about 30% across the business, after all costs.
  • Do not treat the MAP-to-wholesale spread alone as the margin.
  • Shipping and ads still need to be paid from it.
  • Net profit should aim for 30% of the sale.
  • A supplier that gives only about 15% margin leaves very little room for ad spend.
  • So reaching a 30% net profit target usually requires very low customer acquisition costs.
  • Check this before you build a product list (catalogue) around that supplier.
  • Target profit after shipping is about 25 to 30 percent of the selling price, measured after freight.
  • Older advice (2023 is not flagged).

Pricing from cost: setting the selling price

2 min
  • Selling price = total cost per order ÷ (1 − target margin). Total cost per order means everything it costs you to deliver one order.
  • Total cost per order includes product cost, credit card fees, merchant account fees (bank charges for taking card payments), shipping and fulfilment cost per unit (the cost of packing and sending each item).
  • For a 70% margin, price = total cost ÷ 0.30.
  • Example: a product costs $8 with a 70% margin target. Raise the price until margin reaches 70% after fees, shipping and fulfilment are added.

Procedure: fix a product that shows no profit at MAP with flat-rate shipping (flat-rate shipping means the same shipping price for every order) **

  1. Calculate the profit per sale: selling price minus wholesale cost minus flat-rate shipping cost.
  2. If the profit is zero or negative, ask the supplier's sales rep (the supplier's contact person for sales) whether a different price list applies to your account.
  3. If no different price list exists and competitors sell at break-even (no gain, no loss), do not list the product.
  4. If the product is still worth listing and is not MAP-protected, price it at about three times the wholesale cost. Do not run Google ads on it. Rely on organic sales (sales that come without paid ads).

  1. Compare the MAP price of each product with its wholesale cost.
  2. Ask the supplier for sample shipping costs.
  3. Calculate the expected profit per sale from these numbers.
  4. Base the offer on that profit. Do not choose a discount without these figures.

Margin calculators and sheets

4 min

Calculator procedure (Margins & Offers sheet)

  1. Make your own copy of the shared margin calculator sheet (a spreadsheet for working out margins).
  2. List every offer you run or plan to test, such as Buy 1, Buy 2 get 1 free or Buy 3 get 2 free. The list depends on your product.
  3. For each offer, enter the COGS as the total supplier cost of all units the customer receives in that offer.
  4. Enter the Pick N Pack cost (the fee for a company that picks and packs your orders) and the Shipping cost you pay. If shipping is already included in COGS, leave Shipping at 0.
  5. Check that the Gateway column (the payment processing fee) uses your Shopify Payments rate (Shopify's own payment fee). The standard example is 5.5% of the price plus $0.30.
  6. Enter Price On Site as the total the customer pays for the offer, for example $80 for Buy 2 get 1 free.
  7. If you charge shipping, enter the Shipping Price the customer pays.
  8. Read Total Paid / Total Rev (the total money in from the customer), Margin $ (profit in dollars), Margin % (profit as a share of money in) and BE ROAS (break-even ROAS) for the offer.
  9. Enter Price Before Discounts to see Savings and Savings %.
  10. Read Target CPA for 2+ ROAS and for 2.5+ ROAS.
  11. Confirm that Margin % is at least 70%.
  12. Fill every cell for each offer you run before you use its numbers.

Filtering products by net profit per sale

1 min
  • Start from the spread (the gap between two prices): MAP minus wholesale. Then take out shipping, fees and ads.
  • A product that does not leave enough after these is not a candidate.
  • Keep a product only if its net profit per sale (money left from one sale after all costs) is above roughly 10%.
  • Products below this are not worth listing. Older advice (2019).
  • Net profit per sale check (target 30% net, 20% floor, where the floor is the lowest acceptable level): use the net-profit formula for each candidate.
  • Then compare against the threshold.
  • Compare candidates (products you are considering) by profit per order.
  • Also compare by how many orders the same total profit requires. Do not compare by margin % alone.
  • For a catalogue (your full list of products) with hundreds of products, do not calculate every item.
  • Focus on the best sellers. There are often fewer than 20 out of 500 products, and they give the highest leverage (the biggest effect on results).
  • For large catalogues, group products into price buckets (groups of similar prices), such as $2,000, $1,700, $1,500 and $1,300.
  • Use averaged targets per bucket. Older advice (2021-22).
  • Shipping-heavy items (products where shipping costs a lot) need the net check before listing. Margins can be very thin.
  • Amazon net check: profit before ads = sale price − wholesale − shipping − Amazon fees (the fees Amazon takes from each sale).
  • Products whose net profit is below the threshold, and that show no profit at MAP with flat-rate shipping: see the procedure above.
  • Do not launch a store on a supplier list where most products are around 10% margin before ads.
