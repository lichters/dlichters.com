Pricing products from suppliers without MAP

Section 7.6 · 6 of 28 in this chapter · 12 min read

In short

  • If the supplier sets no minimum price, sell for at least 2 times what you pay them.
  • If the maker sets a minimum advertised price (the lowest price you may show), follow that price.
  • Never win by undercutting rivals. Price wars leave everyone with less profit.
  • Before paying for ads on a product, check the market price. If the profit is thin, skip paid ads.
  • Do not offer price matching for products from suppliers that set no minimum price.

Core floor: 2x wholesale for non-MAP products

1 min

MAP means Minimum Advertised Price, the lowest price the maker lets you show publicly. Non-MAP means no such price is set. Wholesale means the price you pay the supplier. The floor means the lowest price you should go below.

  • Without a MAP, set your selling price at at least 2x wholesale, which means twice what you pay. Competitors should also sell at a minimum of 2x wholesale. For example, a 250 wholesale price implies a competitor price of at least 500.
  • The rule for non-MAP pricing is to set prices at at least 2x wholesale. Research competitor prices, meaning what other sellers charge. If competitors sell above 2x, match or exceed that level rather than undercutting, which means selling a little cheaper than them.
  • Without a MAP or MSRP (the Manufacturer's Suggested Retail Price, the price the maker recommends), the formula is: selling price = wholesale × 2. Example: wholesale 500 gives a selling price of 1,000. [L969.002]
  • Minimum advertised price for suppliers without a stated MAP: MAP = 2 × wholesale cost. Example: wholesale $500 means the selling price should be at least $1,000, which leaves roughly 20 to 30% after expenses.
  • MAP means Minimum Advertised Price.
  • The 2x test: the average competitor selling price should be at least double the wholesale cost. If it is below double, treat it as a red flag (a warning sign) and do not upload the supplier's product, since margins (the money you keep per sale after costs) will not be sustainable.
  • Example with $500 wholesale: an average competitor price of $700 fails the 2x test, so the product should not be listed. An average competitor price of $2,000 passes, leaving about $1,500 per sale before fees.

Alternative markup and where the rule is recorded

1 min
  • Where a manufacturer sets a minimum advertised price, follow the MAP price list. Where none exists, apply a fixed markup (a set amount added on top of cost), such as 20%, to each product and compare it with competitor prices. (Older advice, 2021-22. This 20% fixed markup differs from the 2x floor above. Use the MAP list where one exists, and use the 2x floor for non-MAP products as described above.)
  • Put the pricing rule on the upload card (the form used to add a product to the store) so the person uploading does not have to guess.

Setting up price fields

1 min
  • The compare-at price (the higher, crossed-out price shown beside the sale price) is the supplier's MSRP. The sale price (the price the customer actually pays) is the MAP.
  • Example: wholesale $500, MAP $1,000, MSRP $1,200. Set the compare-at price to $1,200 and the sale price to $1,000.

Pricing against the market

1 min

Procedure: price a non-MAP product by market (2025)

1 min
  1. Search Google for the product from the supplier's brand to see the prices competitors list. [L966.049]
  2. If competitors list it at a price that gives enough margin, list your product at that higher price. [L966.049]
  3. If competitors list it too low for a sufficient margin, keep the product on your store at a markup you accept. [L966.049]
  4. Do not include that product in your paid ad spend, meaning the money you pay for ads. Sales from free traffic (visitors who arrive without paid ads) still earn money without wasting ad budget. [L966.049]

Procedure: start at 2x and adjust to the market

1 min
  1. Set the starting selling price at about double the wholesale cost. [L967.065]
  2. Search for the same brand's products online to see what other retailers charge. [L967.065]
  3. If other retailers sell at a higher multiple of wholesale, which means a higher number of times the cost, match that multiple instead of doubling automatically. [L967.065]

Procedure: check the market before doubling

1 min
  1. Search for the product online. [L853.035]
  2. Note what other sellers charge. [L853.035]
  3. If most sellers charge about 2x or more, the higher price can work. [L853.035]
  4. If sellers charge only about 1.2x, the markup is not worth pursuing. [L853.035]

Procedure: price a non-MAP product, 2019 version

1 min
  1. Search Google for the supplier's product names and note the lowest prices from competitor listings on Google Shopping (Google's product price search).
  2. If competitors sell at a much higher price (for example $1,000 against a $200 wholesale cost), price the product at the market level and run Google Shopping ads for it.
  3. If competitors sell close to the wholesale cost (for example $300 against a $200 wholesale cost), do not run Google ads for that product, because no one will buy at a loss. List it only if needed for catalog completeness, meaning every product you want is listed in the store.
  4. If the market gives no usable price, set the price at three times the wholesale cost, provided shipping does not consume the margin.
  5. Exclude non-MAP brands from the price match policy, which is the store's promise to match a cheaper price found elsewhere.

Three-times formula

1 min
  • Selling price = wholesale cost × 3, as long as shipping cost does not wipe out the margin. Examples: $200 wholesale gives $600, and $500 wholesale gives $1,500.
  • This 3x rule sits alongside the 2x floor. The 2x floor is the newer and more common guidance. Use 3x only when there is no usable market price.

Avoiding price wars

1 min
  • Do not win non-MAP sales by undercutting competitors. Price cuts start a race to the bottom, a cycle where sellers keep cutting prices until no margin is left. Match the average market price when margins allow it, and win customers through conversion optimization (improving the store so more visitors buy) instead.
  • Sell at MAP and do not manipulate feed prices. Feed prices are the prices sent to shopping sites through your product feed, the file listing your products. For non-MAP suppliers, match a low competitor price only when profit remains, because price wars leave everyone with less profit. [L469.071]
  • Pricing far below the market, for example selling a $1,000 market product at $800, starts a price war and gives away profit. Price with the market instead.
  • Using the three-times rule without checking the market can leave money on the table, because the actual market price may be much higher than three times wholesale. Always check competitor prices first.

Selling above MAP with trust

1 min
  • With a strong review history, meaning many good customer ratings, a store can sell above MAP and still win sales. A small discount, such as $50 off a multi-thousand-dollar order, can close the sale when buyers want peace of mind. [L806.055]

Price-match protection for non-MAP suppliers

1 min

Procedure: protect prices when selling a supplier with no MAP policy

1 min
  1. Exclude that supplier's brands from your price match policy by adding a note with an asterisk (*) and an exclusions list naming the brands on the price match page. [L357.039]
  2. Hide or remove the price match icon or guarantee from that supplier's product pages. [L357.039]
  3. Check the supplier's products on Google Shopping before contacting them. [L357.039]

Rules

1 min
  • Exclude suppliers without a MAP shown on the product page from the price match guarantee. Their products should not be covered by that promise. [L247.043]
  • If a supplier has no MAP policy, exclude its brand from the store's price match policy and check competitor prices before running ads on those products.
  • Do not offer a price-match policy for suppliers with no MAP. Check Google Shopping before setting your price. [L468.099]
  • In markets without MAP policies, such as Australia or the UK, do not use price match guarantees. Set the sale price at least at double the wholesale cost. [L967.067]
  • Price-match guarantees must never cover items you sell at a loss because of a supplier with no MAP policy. Otherwise, a customer can later find the product cheaper and demand a refund of the difference. [L357.041]

Deciding whether to sell a product

1 min
  • Sell no-MAP products only when few sellers offer them. If many companies sell the same product, the margin will be hard to achieve. [L357.040]
  • Before contacting a no-MAP supplier, check how many companies sell its popular products on Google Shopping. [L357.040]

Deciding whether to advertise a product

1 min
  • If a supplier has no MAP and no margin, do not run ads to those products. List them at about double wholesale without ads, which keeps the store looking established. [L855.046]
  • Run paid ads only on products where the market price gives enough margin. Otherwise, list the product without ads.

Escaping restrictions with your own brand

1 min
  • Creating your own brand from a no-name supplier product (an unbranded product) lets you avoid pricing and channel restrictions, meaning rules on price and on where you may sell. Once you have a private brand with no restrictions, you can consider other sales channels, but only after the main store is consistently strong. [L357.043] **

Escaping restrictions with bundles

1 min
  • If a supplier does not enforce MAP, combine multiple units into bundles, meaning several items sold together as one, and advertise them as a combo product. Build a separate product page for each bundle. [L852.054] **

Pattern: bundle differentiation

1 min
  • Combine the best-rated components from several suppliers, each already sold separately by its maker, into one starter kit under your own brand. Offer a recurring consumables subscription on top. Consumables are items that get used up and need buying again.
  • Pattern: take the top-liked part from brand A, the kit from brand B and the consumable from brand C. White-label each, which means having each maker's item sold under your brand name, then sell them as one bundle plus a subscription.
  • Example 1: a home brewing store white-labels a fermenter (a vessel where drinks ferment) from one maker, a kit from another and a yeast refill from a third. It sells a starter set with monthly yeast delivery.
  • Example 2: a coffee gear store white-labels a grinder, a brewing dripper (a cone that holds coffee and filter paper) and a filter paper pack into one starter bundle, with a monthly filter subscription.

Case: 3D printer store

1 min
  • A store selling 3D printers ran for about one year. Customer comments showed that buyers liked the extruder (the part that pushes plastic into the printer) from one maker, the kit from another and the filament (the plastic thread the printer melts) from a third.
  • The owner planned to ask each maker to white-label its part, combine the parts into one starter bundle under the store's own brand, and add a filament subscription.
  • The owner sold the store before this plan was carried out.
