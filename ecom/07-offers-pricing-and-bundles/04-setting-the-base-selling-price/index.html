Setting the base selling price

Section 7.4 · 4 of 28 in this chapter · 19 min read

In short

  • Set the price from several inputs, not from product cost alone.
  • Start at about 3x the total cost (2.5x if you are new), then round down to a .99 or .95 ending.
  • Do not undercut the market or copy competitor prices without checking your own margin.
  • Show a crossed-out price only if the product really sold at that price.
  • Each new country needs its own prices, in its own money.
More key points
  • Set prices per country from the real cost of delivering there, not one world price.
  • Show local money at checkout, for example CHF for Switzerland.
  • Keep the 2x rule (sell for at least double the supplier's price) in every currency.
  • Before setting the final price, count ad spend and product cost.

Placeholder prices during the build

1 min
  • Enter a fixed placeholder price (a temporary test price) such as $69 in the Price field (the box where you type the selling price) during the build. Do not leave it empty, so the preview shows a realistic layout. Replace it once the final margin and price are decided.
  • Demo product prices should be realistic, but they do not need to be exact. They are placeholders that get updated when real supplier products are added to the store.
  • Price demo products in line with the niche's actual market prices. A niche is a small market segment, such as one type of product. Example: selling price $799, compare-at price $999 (the higher crossed-out price shown next to the sale price), cost per item $300 (what the item costs you, visible only to you).

Inputs to collect before setting a price

1 min
  • Price every product for the USA market by default, because most customers are there. Use US costs and US fees when filling in the sheet (the pricing spreadsheet).
  • Set the price from several inputs together:
    • supplier cost,
    • The margin needed after all per-order costs,
    • competitor prices,
    • The value the customer perceives (how much the buyer thinks the item is worth).
  • Product cost sets only the minimum. It does not set the final price on its own.
  • Include ad spend (money paid for ads) and COGS (cost of goods sold, the product cost) in the calculation before fixing the final price.
  • Do not decide the price from product cost alone.
  • Base prices on healthy margins (the share of each sale you keep after costs). The margin must cover payment processing fees (the card company's cut), shipping costs, ad spend and profit.
  • Do not base pricing only on competitors' prices. Low-margin competitors usually give poor service and fail within years, so long-term success needs sustainable margins.
  • Calculate the total dropshipping price (the supplier ships each order straight to the customer, so you never hold stock) for the chosen variant (version of the product) and destination country (where the customer lives). Get it from the supplier platform (the supplier's website).
  • Then add these costs:
    • payment fees,
    • The estimated customer acquisition cost (what one new customer costs you, mostly through ads),
    • fixed costs per sale (costs that repeat on every sale),
    • a return allowance (money set aside for refunds).
  • Payment fees are not in the 3x split (see Markup rules).
  • At a $29.99 price, a 1.5% to 3% gateway fee (the fee the payment company keeps on each sale) costs about $0.45 to $0.90. This comes out of the $10 margin. Subtract it before accepting the price.

Full pricing procedure

1 min
  1. Calculate the total dropshipping price for the chosen variant and destination country from the supplier platform.
  2. Add payment fees, an estimated customer acquisition cost, fixed costs per sale and a return allowance.
  3. Research competitor prices for the same or a similar product.
  4. Set a starting price and check it with the 3x rule.
  5. Adjust the price against the market and pick a discount strategy.
  6. Launch with ads and test prices and offers.

Markup rules

6 min

A markup is how many times the product cost you charge as the price. For example, a 3x markup means the price is three times the cost.

2x: the minimum floor

  • The simplest starting rule is to sell at no less than 2x wholesale (the supplier's price to you). Use the supplier's MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price the supplier lets you advertise) as the reference. This leaves room for shipping, ads and fees.
  • A rough check: wholesale cost should be about half the selling price (MAP). The spread must still leave money after expenses.
  • Minimum sale price = 2 x wholesale cost. A product with a wholesale cost of $500 should sell for at least $1,000. In a MAP market (a market where the supplier sets the lowest advertised price), the sale price column is set by MAP instead.
  • Example: a stand-up desk with a wholesale cost of $500 and an MSRP (manufacturer's suggested retail price, the official list price) of $1,200 has a strong margin built in. If competitors sell it for $900, do not list it at $900. The minimum is $1,000 (2x wholesale).
  • If competitors already sell above the 2x minimum, price at or near their level instead of at 2x. Do not undercut the market (price just below others). Example: with a $500 wholesale cost and competitors at $1,200 on Google Shopping (Google's product price search page), sell above the $1,000 minimum.
  • The 2x calculation works in any currency, because only the numbers change. A wholesale cost of 500 in any currency gives a minimum price of 1,000 in that same currency.
  • Conflict with a blanket doubling: do not double wholesale prices automatically. Check the price against market rates (what buyers pay elsewhere).
  • Resolution: the 2x figure is a floor to check against the market. It is not a default for every product.
  • Never set the sale price by copying what competitors charge at the moment. Base it on wholesale cost using the 2x rule and the competitor check.

2.5x: testing and strong-brand starts

  • Start at at least 2.5x if you are new.
  • A markup of about 2.5x is a reasonable starting price when a product has high perceived value (buyers think it is worth a lot) and several marketing angles or benefits.
  • Lower competitor prices do not rule this out if branding (the look and name that make your shop recognizable) is strong.
  • For a first test, start at 2.5x to 3x. Raise prices as branding is added.

3x: the standard target

  • Aim for a markup of at least 3x, for example buying at $10 and selling at $30 or more.
  • Set the selling price at about three times the total dropshipping cost. Then check whether the market accepts it.
  • Example: a $10 total cost gives a $29.99 price. That is $10 for the product, $10 for customer acquisition and $10 as margin.
  • The same formula, stated another way: selling price = 3 times supplier cost. It covers supplier cost, customer acquisition cost and profit margin. Example: supplier costs $10, price $30.
  • Round the 3x result down to a.99 ending. A $10 total dropshipping cost gives 3x = $30, which becomes $29.99.
  • Set the selling price at a minimum of 3 times product cost, for example a $10 product sold at $30. Treat a $20 minimum selling price as the bare minimum (the lowest acceptable).
  • Aim for at least 3x the cost of product plus shipping. If product and shipping cost $10, the price should be at least $30. Otherwise the product is hard to scale profitably (grow sales without losing money).
  • Aim for 3x the cost of product plus shipping. Start at 2.5x if you are new.
  • Keep a minimum margin of $20 when the product price is low.

Organic versus paid traffic

  • Organic traffic means free visitors from search or social posts. Paid traffic means visitors you pay for with ads.
  • Organic sales can usually be priced at two to three times cost. This leaves room to cover taxes and possibly shipping.
  • Paid ad sales need a higher price than organic, because ad costs come on top of the product cost.

$10 $20 Organic Workable, leaves room for taxes and shipping

4x: branded and visual products

  • Fashion products can usually carry a higher markup of around 4x once the store has custom images or videos and branding. Buyers pay for lifestyle and visuals.
  • In a fall sock example, one pack was sourced (bought from the supplier) for about $5 and sold for $20, a 4x markup. This is described as a standard single-item markup.

10x: high perceived value

  • For products with high perceived value, such as jewelry, the selling price can be about 10 times the sourcing cost when buyers believe the item is worth it.
  • A product bought for about $5 can be sold at roughly $50.
  • Perceived value sets the ceiling, not the cost.

Fees, low-priced items and currency gaps

  • Build the currency difference into the price when buying from US suppliers in US dollars and selling in Canadian dollars.
  • Otherwise the exchange rate gap (the change in value between the two currencies) can erase the margin.
  • To offset eBay commission (eBay's cut per sale) and payment processing fees on low-priced items, apply a markup rule to the product feed (the list of products sent to shopping sites). Any product below a set price is increased by a fixed percentage.
  • Example: a fee of about 0.30 GBP per order can make cheap products unprofitable without a markup.
  • A cheap product bought at about $10 or less on AliExpress (an online supplier marketplace) was sold at $29.99, matching competitors.
  • The margin was about 60%, so price matching made it workable for a low-cost product.

Perceived value and premium positioning

  • Premium positioning (pricing above the market as a top brand) allows higher prices when differentiation (standing out from others) exists.
  • Selling supplements at 50% to 100% above competitors is possible when a unique formula or packaging justifies the premium.
  • Perceived value drives willingness to pay.
  • A product with high perceived value can be priced well above its supplier cost. Example: a $23 to $25 supply cost with a potential selling price near $50.
  • Check the supplier cost of the exact variant before assuming the margin.
  • A security camera that works like a light bulb went viral. A video had about 6.7 million views, and a second video was still growing.
  • It sold for $38.99, with a supplier cost estimated at about $20.
  • A similar product on Amazon was listed around $69.
  • The estimated margin was considered good. The perceived value suggested a price up to around $50, although TikTok buyers might not accept that.
  • A product that started at $49.99 was later sold at $89.99 without changing the product itself.
  • The increase came from custom branding, custom packaging, custom content, good images and videos, and a branded website.
  • Strong product images and branding can raise perceived value enough to support a higher price in niches such as jewelry.
  • Example: a necklace that once sold at $12.99 can be sold at $19.99 with good images. Then offer it as BOGO (buy one, get one free).
  • A case with a buy-cost (what you pay to buy) of about $20 and a break-even (the price where you earn nothing) of about $52 leaves room for a launch price (the first price you go live with) of $49 or $59.
  • When a supplier sells versions of one product that differ in a single feature, such as a hood or open feet, the version with the extra feature can be priced higher.
  • The price gap should match a feature the buyer can see on the product page.
  • When a viral product's (one that spreads fast online) selling price feels too high for its perceived value, look for a cheaper variation or another supplier. Bring the price into the target range before running ads.
  • Test only once buyers can see clear value at that price.

Platform differences

  • The same product can carry a different price on a different platform, because each platform's audience accepts a different price.
  • Example: a store running ads only on Pinterest sold the same product at about $38.

Competitor and Google Shopping benchmarking

3 min

Where to look

  • If no MSRP exists, search Google for competitor prices and match them.
  • Compare competitor prices using the item price shown for the same product on Google Shopping. This is the price to benchmark against (use as the reference). (2024)
  • Do not base the selling price on Amazon prices, which are usually lower.
  • Benchmark against competitors selling the same product on Facebook, since that is the platform used.
  • Ignore very low prices from competitor stores that look like low-effort tests. Example: a product crossed out at 40 and listed at 20.
  • These prices are not a reliable benchmark.
  • Compare your prices with competitors to decide whether to match, undercut or charge more.
  • A higher price is defensible only when the customer perceives more value in your store.
  • A common starting benchmark is a retail price of about double the product cost.
  • Verify this against competitor prices on Facebook before setting the final price.
  • Use the Pricing Research columns of the sheet: MSRP or RRP (recommended retail price, similar to MSRP), MAP and competitor prices. Also check whether competitors charge for shipping.
  • Use them to compare your price against the market. Move the list price toward the best balance of sales and profit.

Shipping in competitor averages

  • When estimating a competitor average price, include shipping if it appears on most Google Shopping listings for that product. Otherwise, average only the product prices.
  • Do not inflate the sale price to include shipping.
  • Instead, set the MAP high enough that the markup covers shipping, customer acquisition and profit margin.

Competitor research procedure

  1. Find several competitor listings for the same or a very similar product.
  2. Note the one-time purchase price (buying once) and the subscription price (a repeat-delivery plan) for each listing.
  3. Note the spread (the gap between the lowest and highest price) and any subscription discount shown.
  4. Choose your price inside that range and set it in the Pricing section of the product.
  • Example: for a similar toothpaste tablet product, competitor listings ranged from $32 for a one-time purchase up to $48, depending on the purchase option.
  • One listing offered a subscription at $28.80 (10% off a one-time $32 price). Another advertised Save 33% on a 4-month supply.
  • A demo price of $35 was chosen inside the $32 to $48 range.
  • The demo was only an example. The final price should come from your own margin calculation, not from the competitor range alone.
  • A single-book example: the standard price in the Buy 1 tier (the one-book option) is $41.95. The lowest acceptable price for that tier is $39.99.

Positioning against competitors

  • Set the base price close to competitors' prices.
  • If the profit margin is at least 70%, you can price slightly below them to undercut.
  • If margins are strong but tighter, price slightly above them to protect profitability.
  • Price a product at roughly the same level as direct competitors. Use the net margin (what you keep after ad spend and COGS) as the deciding factor.
  • If that net margin is at least 70%, price slightly below competitors to undercut them.
  • If the margin is strong but tighter, price slightly above them to protect profitability.
  • This text gives no numeric boundary between "at least 70%" and "strong but tighter".
  • Below 70%, only the direction is given: price slightly higher to protect profitability.
  • Set the exact adjustment from your own margin calculation.
  • Matching a competitor's price, especially when they offer free shipping, skips the profit check. This happens when you do not first factor in ad spend and COGS.
  • Price decisions need the full cost picture.
  • Premium positioning of 50% to 100% above competitors is possible with differentiation.
  • Check the price after rounding. The final price must still fall within the target margin range (see Psychological price endings).

Avoiding price wars

1 min
  • Do not start a price war (competitors keep cutting prices against each other). It rarely works in this kind of business.
  • Check competitor pricing for similar products before setting your own price.
  • Do not undercut the market. Undercutting starts a price race to the bottom (prices keep falling).
  • Matching a competitor's price can leave only about 5% to 7% profit.
  • That is a red flag for paid traffic. It is also a reason to negotiate supplier pricing before advertising.

Psychological price endings

1 min
  • Psychological pricing means ending prices just below a round number, such as.99 or.95. Buyers perceive these as lower.
  • This makes the same product look more attractive than a whole-dollar price.
  • The 3x rounding step uses a .99 ending. Round the result down, so $30 becomes $29.99.
  • Final prices are rounded to a .95 ending, such as $45.95 or $64.95.
  • After rounding, check that the margin still falls within the 67% to 70% range.
  • Both endings are in use. The Source C rule uses.99 and the Source D rule uses.95.
  • The rules do not say which ending to pick for a given product, so test per market.
  • Price-ending tests matter. A price like 49.90 instead of 50 in the local currency can lift conversion (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Test this per market. Do not assume one rounding rule works everywhere.
  • In a new market such as the UK, do not use an automatic currency conversion price (a price changed by a currency tool).
  • Use a price ending like 24.90 or 26.90 pounds instead of 27 pounds. The source called 27 pounds unattractive.

Anchoring

1 min

Reference points before the price

  • Anchoring means setting a reference point (a number the reader compares against) before the reader judges a price.
  • For example, compare the product with hours of cleaning each month, or with the much higher cost of an alternative. The real price then looks small.
  • Stacking bonuses with individual values and a total value, then showing a much lower price, uses the same effect.
  • Example copy: "Compared to the hours spent scrubbing each month, this brush is a small one-time cost."

Price anchors for thin margins

  • When profit margins are tight and large discounts would wear away profit, create a price anchor (a higher starting price that makes a discount look bigger).
  • To do this, duplicate the product, raise its listed price by 15% to 30%, then discount from that higher price.
  • Customers cannot easily work out the true savings. They lack a reference point for the actual product cost, which creates perceived value.
  • Example: original product cost $100. Duplicate the product and raise the price 20% to $120.
  • Offer 30% off this higher price: $120 × 0.70 = $84 final price.
  • The customer perceives $36 in savings. The actual discount from original cost is only $16 (16%), which preserves margin.
  • Apply the price anchor strategy only when margins are low.
  • With strong margins, offer straight discounts without the duplicate-and-inflate technique.

Buy-one-get-two-free anchor

  • Set a deliberately high price for one unit. Then offer buy one get two free.
  • Buyers compare the offer with the inflated single price. They feel they receive the extra units free, and they can picture giving them to relatives or friends.
  • Example 1: a $25 phone stand shown at $75 with buy one get two free.
  • Example 2: an $18 pet brush shown at $54 under the same offer.
  • Raise the product price, then offer buy-one-get-one-free on top. The offer still looks large, while the margin stays intact.

Compare-at and strikethrough pricing

3 min

Shopify fields

  • In Shopify (an online store platform), the Price and Compare at price fields use the store's currency. The currency is shown as a prefix in the field, for example A$ in an Australian store.
  • Enter all prices in that currency.
  • The compare-at price (the higher crossed-out price) must be higher than the price to show a sale.
  • Enter the selling price in the Price field. Keep "Charge tax on this product" (a box that adds sales tax to the price) ticked by default.
  • Cost per item is visible only to you. It is used for the margin and profit display.

Core rules

  • Do not use a compare-at price as the sale price.
  • The actual selling price must match the product feed. Compare-at is only an additional display.
  • Do not show every product as a discount.
  • Use a strikethrough price (an old price with a line through it) only when the original price is legitimately higher. This maintains credibility.
  • Only display a compare-at price that the product has really sold at. An inflated reference price is a deceptive pricing claim.
  • Competitor pages show a sale price next to a struck-through original and a percentage saving. Example: 59.99 against 119.99 with 50% saved.
  • Display a sale price next to a higher compare-at price with a percentage savings label. Example: $19.99 next to a struck-through $39.99 with a SAVE 50% badge.
  • This makes the saving visible on the product page.
  • Show a higher regular price crossed out next to a lower sale price, so the discount is visible at a glance.
  • The higher reference price makes the sale price look like a real deal.
  • Examples in the same pattern: a regular price of $30 crossed out next to a sale price of $19.99. Also a regular price of $12 crossed out next to a sale price of $7.49.
  • The example given was a regular price of $4.10 reduced to $1.49.

MSRP and MAP as strikethrough and sale price

  • List the MSRP as the strikethrough price and sell the product at MAP.
  • Example: wholesale $500, MAP $1,000 and MSRP $1,250. This gives $1,250 struck through and a sale at $1,000.
  • Competitor sites can be checked for the strikethrough price. It is the MSRP about 95% of the time.
  • If a supplier does not send an MSRP, create an everyday price to use as the strikethrough price.
  • A missing MSRP happens roughly 10% to 20% of the time and is not unusual.
  • Show MAP as the main price on the product page. Show MSRP as the crossed-out compare-at price, which matches typical retail presentation.
  • List products on the store at MAP, the minimum advertised price set by the supplier. (2024)
  • Use MSRP as the compare-at price with a strikethrough so the discount is visible. (2024)
  • When a supplier also publishes an MSRP, set the MAP as the sale price. Set the MSRP as the compare-at price shown with a strikethrough. (2023)
  • Example: MAP 1000 and MSRP 1200 gives a 1200 compare-at price. (2023)
  • When MSRP equals MAP, either set the compare-at price about 20% above MAP or leave it blank.
  • Match the approach used by the top competitors.

Volume discounts

  • A volume discount is a discount for buying more units. For a 50% off volume discount, set the compare-at price to about double the sale price.
  • Example: compare-at $79.99 and sale price $39.99.
  • For volume discounts, set the compare-at price to about double the sale price. Example: compare-at 79.99 and sale price 39.99.
  • Show the sale price in a larger font. Add 50% off.
  • Optionally, add a time-limited or event-based scarcity message (a note that the offer ends soon or at an event).
  • Use a theme (the design template of the store) or a page builder (a tool to design pages without code), such as PageFly or GemPages, to highlight the compare-at price.

Multi-offer display

  • Example structure of a Posture Pro Corrector offer:
    • regular price $49.99,
    • a 20% off offer (implied price $39.99),
    • a risk-free trial (the buyer can try it with no risk) also at $39.99,
    • a free gift (Posture Fix Guide) shown as a $19.99 value.

Multi-currency and multi-market pricing

1 min
  • Each extra market (a country you sell in) needs its own prices in its own currency (the local money). With many markets, you must convert prices by hand. This is easy to forget and can leave products underpriced (priced too low to earn enough). Reducing to a few free markets was seen as simpler. Showing local currency at checkout (the payment step) is worth doing for the UK and similar markets.
  • A store for one country should show prices in that country's currency, such as CHF for Switzerland, not USD (US dollars). Local currency makes the offer easier to understand. It also fits local payment methods (the ways people there usually pay).
  • Convert price ranges to local currency. Keep lower-priced niches (narrow product groups) around $200 from dropping too low, such as to 150 pounds in the UK. Higher-priced niches can be less strict.
  • Set prices per country based on local landed cost (the full cost of getting the product to that country) and shipping cost, not one global price. Example: dropshipping (a supplier ships the product straight to the customer) cost $16 for Australia and $19 for the UK. The UK price was set higher (54.90 AUD, as written in the source; check this currency) to protect margin (the profit left after costs). Australia stayed lower because its shipping was cheaper.
  • Test price endings (the last digits of a price, such as.99) per market. See Psychological price endings.
  • In new markets, avoid automatic conversion prices (prices the shop calculates from another currency). See Psychological price endings.
  • The 2x wholesale minimum (the lowest you may sell for, measured against the wholesale price, which is the supplier's price) applies in any currency. See Markup rules.
