MAP, MSRP and wholesale: reading supplier pricing

Section 7.5 · 5 of 28 in this chapter · 14 min read

In short

  • Your cost is wholesale. Your selling price is MAP (the lowest price you may show buyers). Never show a price below MAP.
  • Follow the 2x rule: MAP should be at least 2 times wholesale.
  • Aim for a 50% spread (the gap between your cost and your selling price) before you run ads for a product.
  • If a seller goes below MAP, send the supplier a screenshot of the lower price right away.

Supplier price lists: wholesale, MAP and MSRP

2 min
  • A supplier price list gives three prices.
    • Wholesale is your cost: what you pay the supplier.
    • MAP (minimum advertised price) is the lowest price you may show buyers. It is your selling price.
    • MSRP (manufacturer's suggested retail price) is the full list price. You show it crossed out as the compare-at price (the old price shown next to your sale price).
  • Example: wholesale $500, MAP $1,000, MSRP $1,200.
  • Example of a supplier's price sheet after approval (the list you get once the supplier accepts you), usually one spreadsheet with three rows:
Price Role for your store Example (USD)
Wholesale cost you pay the supplier 1,000
MAP the price you sell at 2,000
MSRP the crossed-out compare-at price 3,000

Older (2019).

  • Definitions:
    • MSRP is the manufacturer's suggested retail price.
    • MAP is the minimum advertised price you may display.
    • Wholesale is what you pay the supplier. Older (2019).
  • For decisions, wholesale and MAP matter most. MSRP matters less. Older (2019).
  • MSRP is a reference point in the price list, not the price to aim for, because most retailers sell below it.
  • MSRP is always higher than MAP.
  • MSRP and MAP example: MSRP 400, MAP 350, wholesale 175. MAP is shown as the sale price. MSRP is shown struck through (crossed out). Older (2022).
  • Example: a studio microphone with MSRP $300 and MAP $250. If the brand lets resellers advertise at MAP, sell it for $250. Older (2019).
  • Use the supplier's price list. Upload each product at the prices the supplier specifies instead of choosing your own.
  • Use the supplier's MAP as the listed price on the product page (the page a buyer sees for one item). Calculate margin (profit left after costs) from that same price.
  • Use MAP controls. Pick suppliers that set a minimum price, so competitors cannot undercut each other to zero margin (no profit left). Set your prices from the supplier's list.
  • Price lists may show net price (a price term on the list), MAP or IMAP (another minimum-price code), and wholesale. MAP is the sell price and wholesale is your cost. To confirm MAP, search the product name on Google and check competitor listings (other sellers' product pages).
  • If a supplier gives neither MAP nor MSRP, you set your own selling price and check it against competitor prices. Older (2022).
  • Record MSRP/RRP and MAP together with competitor prices, and whether competitors charge for shipping. This shows where your list price sits. RRP (recommended retail price) is another name for MSRP. Older (2021-22).

MAP policy in three forms

1 min
  • A supplier's MAP policy (its minimum-price rule) can appear in three forms:
    1. a MAP column in the price list,
    2. a multiple of wholesale, such as 2x (twice the wholesale price),
    3. a maximum discount below max retail price (the highest full price). Treat each form as the lowest price you may advertise. Older (2018).
  • Formula: MAP = wholesale × 2 when the supplier gives a multiplier (a number you multiply by). Example: $500 wholesale gives MAP $1,000. Older (2018).
  • Example: max retail price $148 with a 20% discount cap (the most a supplier lets you discount) sets the lowest advertised price at $112. Older (2018).
    • Check: 20% below $148 is $118.40, not $112. Confirm the exact figure with the supplier before using either number.
  • Some lower-tier suppliers (suppliers ranked lower) do not enforce MAP (make sellers follow it). They still cap discounts at 20% below max retail price. Treat that cap as a MAP rule. Older (2018).

What MAP allows and forbids

1 min

Definition and minimum

1 min
  • MAP is the lowest price a retailer may advertise or sell a product for, set by the supplier. Example: a $1,000 MAP on a standing desk means no retailer may list it below $1,000. This removes price competition between sellers.
  • MAP is the lowest price you may show in your store and in your ads. It is the default price on the product page (the price shown first) and the price people see in an ad. Older? No (2025).
  • MAP sets only a minimum. A product can be listed above MAP but not below it. Older (2020).
  • MAP sets the lowest listed price. If MAP is $1,000, your listed price must not go below $1,000, the same as every other seller. Older (2020).
  • Older (2022). MAP is the lowest price a product may be advertised at. The product page price must be at least MAP.
  • The price shown on a product page cannot be below MAP, because the "A" in MAP stands for advertised.
  • MAP is the lowest price a supplier lets retailers advertise.

Allowed

1 min
  • MAP has no maximum. You may list above MAP. You may never advertise or display below MAP on the site or in ads.
  • Selling above MAP is permitted. Example: MAP $2,000, sold at $5,000, is allowed under the MAP policy. Older (2020).
  • Pricing above MAP is rare. It works only where few retailers sell the brand and competitors also price above MAP. You can see this on Google Shopping (Google's product price search). Older (2020).
  • Competitors should not undercut your prices, because suppliers enforce pricing policies. Work with brands where the margin is locked in (fixed and protected from price cuts). Older (2020).
  • "Add to Cart to See Price" (a button that hides the price until the buyer adds the item to the cart) may replace the price. The lower price must be revealed only after the buyer adds the item to the cart. Older (2020).
  • Lower prices with discount codes (codes typed at checkout, the last payment step) are a practical option. This is true unless the supplier agreement says otherwise.
  • A price-match guarantee (a page promising to match a cheaper price) matters only where the products come from MAP-enforcing suppliers. If the suppliers are not MAP suppliers, remove the price-match page from the store menu so shoppers do not see it. Older (2022).

Forbidden

1 min
  • Never list a product below MAP on the store, because it is advertised below the minimum advertised price.
  • Coupon codes do not change the rule. The advertised price is what draws clicks. Older (2018).
  • Bonuses count as a price (a bonus is a free extra item). If the bonus is advertised below MAP, it breaks the policy. Example: a $100 MAP paddle with a bonus paddle advertised at $50 breaks MAP. Older (2021).
  • A MAP agreement, signed during supplier approval, means you may not advertise or sell below MAP. Every seller of that product must show it at or above MAP. Older (2019).
  • Once a supplier approves you, do not undercut other sellers. If a competitor's best price is $1,500, sell at $1,500. Price cutting eventually leaves no one with a profit. Older (2020).

Why MAP exists

1 min
  • MAP enforcement (making every seller follow the rule) stops competitors from undercutting your price. Without it, competitors selling below your cost can drain your margin and sales. Older (2019).
  • Suppliers without MAP let competitors undercut you, so avoid their products unless the margin is good.
  • Silver suppliers (a supplier tier) enforce MAP. This means you compete only on service and brand, not price. Check that a supplier enforces MAP before you commit to its products.
  • MAP creates a level playing field (equal rules for everyone) among retailers. Older (2021-22).

The 2x spread rule

1 min
  • Spread is the gap between your cost and your selling price, shown as a share of the selling price. Spread is the key number for deciding whether a product is worth selling.

Core rule

1 min
  • MAP must be at least 2 times wholesale. Example: $500 wholesale needs a MAP of at least $1,000. Wholesale can be no more than 50% of MAP. Older (2019-2021).
  • The 2x is a rough rule for typical items. MAP should be about 2x wholesale to keep healthy margins. Older? No (2025).
  • For products that will receive ad spend (money paid for ads), MAP should be at least 2x wholesale. Example: $500 wholesale implies $1,000 MAP.
  • List at 2x wholesale as long as the price covers all expenses. Example: $1,500 wholesale listed at $3,000.
  • Minimum threshold: MAP must be at least 2x wholesale. If margins are insufficient, negotiate wholesale down. Do not sell above MAP instead, because customers will buy elsewhere at lower prices.
  • Minimum rule example: MAP $575 on a $500 product is a 15% markup (extra percent added on top of cost). This is not worth pursuing. Older (2019).
  • Example: if MAP is 500, wholesale should be no more than 250. If wholesale is 400, MAP should be at least 800.
  • Minimum spread used to decide whether a product is worth carrying: wholesale no more than 50% of MAP.
  • Target a MAP about twice the wholesale price. That is a 100% gap between wholesale and MAP. Older (2019).
  • Wholesale should be no more than half of MAP. Suppliers above that leave too little margin. Older (2022).

Spread target and formula

1 min
  • Spread = (MAP − wholesale) ÷ MAP. The target is about 50%.
  • For advertised products, aim for a minimum 50% spread between wholesale and MAP. This leaves room for up to 10% of the price in ad spend, a 2.9% transaction fee (the card or payment cost per sale), and shipping.
  • Example: wholesale $161, MAP $230 gives a spread of about 30%, which is too low.
  • Example: wholesale $1,200, MAP $2,000 gives a spread of 40%. It leaves $800 profit per sale, which can be acceptable.
  • The 50% target has one accepted exception: a 40% spread on a $2,000 MAP product with a $1,200 wholesale price. The $800 dollar profit still makes the sale worthwhile.
  • Minimum listed price formula: minimum listed price = 2 × wholesale cost (the MAP). Example: 500 wholesale must be listed at no less than 1,000. Older (2021).
  • The 2x rule is calculated on product cost only, excluding shipping. Example: wholesale $500, MAP $1,000.
  • A wholesale of $700 on a $1,000 MAP product is too thin for ads. Rely on free organic traffic (free visits from search) and free Google Shopping traffic instead.
  • First step when reviewing a product's margin: check the wholesale-to-MAP multiplier. Older (2021).
  • The ratio can narrow somewhat at higher prices. A solid spread is still needed to cover shipping, ads and card fees. Older (2019).

Multiplier below 2x

1 min
  • A multiplier of about 1.8 can occasionally be acceptable. Older (2021).

Exclusivity, direct sales and channels

1 min
  • A channel is a route you sell through, such as your own website or Amazon.
  • With true exclusivity (only you may sell the product), you set the selling price yourself. MAP does not limit it. Confirm exclusivity in writing before relying on it. Older (2018).
  • A brand that sells directly to the public (direct-to-consumer, or DTC) is fine for a reseller. This is true as long as the brand lets you advertise at its MAP. Older (2019).
  • Suppliers may sell direct to the public at MSRP while retailers advertise at MAP. Check the supplier's MSRP and MAP before deciding whether the brand's margin works. Older (2019).
  • A supplier's own website may sell at MSRP while your store sells at MAP. That is acceptable because you set your own price. Check the supplier's retail price before listing, so your store is not underpriced by mistake. Older (2020).
  • A direct-to-consumer supplier can be carried only if it sells at MSRP and lets retailers sell at MAP. The other option is a weak direct sales effort, for example no ads and an outdated website.
  • A supplier lists a product below MAP on its own website while others sell at MAP:
    • If your MAP is lower than their website price, the product may still be worth selling.
    • If they sell below the MAP you intend to use, skip the product entirely.
  • Some brands sell directly to consumers and to online retailers at different prices. Brands may give online retailers incentives (bonuses or perks), so verify pricing with the supplier.
  • Online retailers often receive better wholesale prices than large box retail chains (big physical store chains). This makes online sellers a preferred channel when comparing supplier pricing.
  • A brand selling on Amazon does not disqualify it for your store. This is true as long as your listed prices follow the supplier's price sheet and pricing policy. Older (2021).

MAP outside the United States

1 min
  • MAP policies cannot be legally enforced in the EU, Australia or New Zealand. Brands there often still require retailers to sign an agreement not to list below a set price. They enforce it through the account relationship. Older (2021).

Supplier pricing with little or no margin

1 min

Warning signs

1 min
  • Avoid suppliers offering only 15% off MAP. After transaction fees (2.9%), shipping and ad spend, these are nearly impossible to sell profitably. Older (2022).
  • A 20% supplier discount on a $1,000 product makes MAP $1,000 with insufficient margin. Older (2020).
  • A supplier offering 50% off MSRP while MAP is 25% off MSRP leaves too little margin. The deal works only if competitors sell at MSRP. Older (2021).
  • A supplier whose wholesale is above half of MAP leaves too little room for margin. Older (2022).
  • A 20 percent commission (a fee the supplier takes from each sale) is a bad deal. Older (2019).

Options when margin is thin

1 min
  1. Check the multiplier and spread against the thresholds above.
  2. If the spread is 40% and the dollar profit is strong, the exception may apply.
  3. If margin is insufficient, negotiate wholesale down. Do not sell above MAP to compensate, because customers will buy elsewhere at lower prices.
  4. If the product is listed above MAP, it must go without paid traffic (ads that bring visitors). List at a higher price and use organic traffic or remarketing (ads shown again to people who already visited) only. Example: a $1,000 product with only a 20% discount is listed at $1,600 with no paid traffic. Older (2020).
  5. Above-MAP pricing works where few retailers sell the brand and competitors also price above MAP. Check Google Shopping before you rely on it. Older (2020).
  6. Without MAP, the retailer sets its own price and must check it against competitor prices.
  7. Products without MAP enforcement are avoided unless the margin is good.

Negotiation email

1 min

Use this template when competitors are undercutting you and the supplier's terms leave too little margin:

We noticed that [competitor name] is selling [product name] at [competitor price]. At that price, there is not enough margin for us to drive traffic profitably. Do you offer different pricing tiers, incentives or wholesale discounts for a retailer in our position?

Competitors selling below MAP

1 min

Monitoring

1 min
  • Monitor competitors for MAP compliance (following the rule):
    1. Check their current discounts for MAP violations.
    2. Report any violation as soon as you find it.
  • Monitor for prices below your MAP, including product-specific discounts that can push a competitor under MAP. Report violations quickly. A competitor who undercuts for a long time can build a war chest (saved cash for ads) and outbid you in paid advertising.
  • Minimum advertised price policies protect your margin. Monitor competitor prices for your products. Report violations to the manufacturer to enforce the policy.

Checking whether the supplier enforces MAP

1 min
  1. Pick a specific product from the supplier.
  2. Search its name on Google and open the Google Shopping results.
  3. Compare prices across sellers and confirm the listings are the identical product.
  4. If the same product sells much cheaper at some sellers, MAP is likely not enforced.

Older (2021).

Do not guess: ask the supplier

1 min
  • When a competitor sells a supplier's products below the MAP price sheet, ask the supplier's sales rep rather than guessing. A sales rep is the supplier's salesperson. Send a screenshot of the competitor's listing and the supplier's price list. Ask whether the price list is wrong. Older (2021).
  • Email to a supplier sales rep:
Email to a supplier sales rep about a competitor selling below MAP:

Hi [sales rep name], I am seeing [competitor store name] selling [product name] for [competitor price], which is below the MAP price on your current price list. I have attached a screenshot of their listing and a screenshot of your price list. Do I have an incorrect price list, and could you confirm the correct pricing for this product?

Older (2021).

Reporting a violation

1 min

  • Report a MAP violation to the supplier immediately. Email your contact with a screenshot (a picture of the screen) of the violating price. Older (2021).

  • Full sequence when a supplier breaks MAP:

    1. Take a screenshot of the Google Shopping result showing the lower price.
    2. Take a screenshot of their website showing the price.
    3. Email the supplier with the proof.
    4. Ask them to reach out to the retailer to adjust price per policy.
    5. Give the retailer a few days to a week to fix it.
    6. If they don't comply, the supplier should remove them from the approval list (the list of approved sellers).

    Older (2022).

  • Shorter sequence for reporting a competitor who violates MAP:

    1. Email your sales rep or supplier contact right away.
    2. Include a screenshot of the violation and the link to the page showing the lower price.
    3. Ask the supplier to act on the violation.

    Older (2019).

  • Another sequence:

    1. Take a screenshot of the competitor listing that sells below MAP.
    2. Send it to your supplier contact, naming the competitor's domain and stating it violates MAP policy.
    3. Ask the supplier to have it resolved.

    Older (2021).

Google Shopping and price gaps

1 min
  • Do not raise bids (what you pay per click) to fight a large price gap. MAP exists to level the field. Lowering your bids cannot close a price gap in Google Shopping, a price comparison engine. Older (2021-22).
  • Competing for top positions with a large gap wastes money. Shoppers still see the cheaper price and buy elsewhere. Clicks come from people checking the full price, but few convert (actually buy). Older (2021-22).
