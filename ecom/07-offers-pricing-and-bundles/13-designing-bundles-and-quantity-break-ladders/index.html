Playbook › 7. Offers, pricing and bundles

Designing bundles and quantity-break ladders

Section 7.13 · 13 of 28 in this chapter · 45 min read

In short

  • A bundle is one deal that packs several products together. Buying more often means a lower price per item.
  • Check your margin first. After all costs, you must still keep enough money from every sale.
  • Start with one product. Add bundles once that product sells.
  • Test which offers work. Keep the one that sells and still makes profit.
  • Badge the bundle that tests best for margin (money you keep) or volume (number sold), not the one you want to push by default.
More key points
  • Price each bundle from its own supplier cost, then check that healthy margin is left after fees and shipping.
  • Put free shipping on the top bundle tier, not the lowest, in most cases.
  • Name tiers by benefit or by supply length (such as "30-day"), not "Bundle 2".
  • Add a cheap accessory to your main product and sell them together as one bundle.
  • Show the bundle with a clear saving of about 15%.
  • Test each offer and keep the one that earns the most per sale.
  • Do not use buy-one-get-one or free items for products that need explaining, or for single items.
  • Check that each bundle price covers product cost, ad cost and fees, or you lose money.

Bundle types and definitions

1 min
  • A bundle combines more than one product into one offer. Example: "buy this and this item and get an extra 20% off." A quantity break is a bundle with several units of the same product. A unit is one item. Example: "buy one and get 20% off, buy two and get 30% off."
  • Bundle pricing sells two or more units for less than the sum of the single prices. The customer gets an automatic saving. AOV (average order value, the average amount one order brings in) goes up. The bundle price must still leave a margin (the money you keep from each sale after costs) that covers all costs.
  • Quantity-break offers raise AOV. Customers who buy more get a bigger discount, and the store earns more per order.
  • Automatic quantity discounts raise AOV and conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy). The total looks lower when someone buys several units. A short urgency line, such as "only 5 left in stock," can make this stronger. Urgency means a message that pushes the buyer to act now.
  • Bulk pricing or a discounted second unit raises AOV. Buyers who want more of the same product are easier to convert than new buyers.
  • Bundles raise AOV. A customer who buys two items plus a free third spends more per order than someone buying one item. Use bundles to make the store more profitable per visitor, not only to win the first sale.
  • Bundles and volume discounts increase the number of items per order. A free gift option can lower the perceived price without a full discount. Test which offer type fits the margin of your products.

Bundle types in use

5 min

Quantity breaks and buy-X-get-Y

  • Buy two, get a percentage off the second unit or the whole order: buy two and get an extra 30% off raises AOV and margin per order. It suits customers who want more units of the same item.
  • Buy one get one free (BOGO): this works strongly with customers, because people respond to a free item.
  • Buy two, get the third free: a typical bundle offer. Choose the version by testing which sells best while still leaving a good margin.
  • Buy two, get the third free, for products customers already buy in stores: buy two, get a third free raises order size and margin. Buy two with free shipping also raises both.
  • Buy any two, get the third 50% off: this encourages multiple items per order while keeping profit per unit.
  • Buy-X-get-Y offers: customers buy more items to qualify for the free item. They work best when the product is logically bought in multiples, such as home decor.
  • Tiered BOGO-style ladders: Buy 1 Get 1 Free, Buy 2 Get 1 Free, Buy 3 Get 2 Free. Show the savings amount under each tier. A tier is one level of an offer. A ladder is a row of tiers from small to big. Keep the layout simple so customers are not overwhelmed.
  • Common offers for low-priced products: buy two get one free, and buy three get two free. A store running several of these is usually testing which quantity customers accept.
  • Buy two, buy three or buy four and get one free: this works well for small, cheap products. Test several and keep the one that suits the margin.
  • Buy-two offer with a second item at a percentage off: a version made for gifts, used with free US shipping.
  • Buy one at 20% off, with a second item at an extra 15% off: one of the offer structures that work on TikTok (a short-video app).
  • Buy one and get the second at 50% off, with free shipping above a threshold: a bundle structure. A threshold is the order value needed to unlock the offer. Here, the threshold is about 55 dollars.
  • Buy two get 50% off or 30% off: tested structures among several others (see the list below).

Complementary bundles

  • A complementary product solves a related problem with the main product. A complementary bundle pairs two different products. Replace a second identical item with a complementary product. Examples: a sleep-related item with pyjamas, or an organiser with a wallet. A second identical item has little value to a buyer who needs only one.
  • Complementary items work better than pushing customers to buy more of the same product to reach a free-shipping threshold.
  • Complementary bundles work well when the add-on solves a related need and is cheap to ship. Shipping both items in one parcel is cheaper than two separate orders to the same address.
  • Complementary-product bundles can convert better than standard discounts. A bundle with a complementary item, made with the Bundle Bear app (an app that builds bundles in a Shopify store), roughly doubled the conversion rate. This was compared with buy 2 get 20% off. Results showed a 40 to 50 percent bundle take rate (the share of buyers who pick the bundle) and about $40 extra profit per order.
  • Complementary items are added when customers rarely buy more than one of the same product.
  • Offer a complementary item when it complements the solution to the customer's problem or makes the product more attractive. For example, a candle seller can give two free when a customer buys four.
  • For a cheap product that complements a main product, test buy two get two free instead of a small percentage discount. This can raise AOV.
  • Pair a main product with a part it needs from the same list. Examples: Fish Tanks with Aquarium Stands, Koi Ponds & Filtration Systems, and Above Ground Pools with Pool Filtration Systems and Pool Vacuums. Selling them together raises order value and saves the buyer a separate search.
  • Bundle a product with a complementary product the same buyer needs. Example: a neck pillow for someone with neck pain who has trouble sleeping.

Build-your-own, sets, avatar and family bundles

  • Build-your-own: up to 15% off, free shipping, and any combination of products the customer chooses. Show it as a pop-up offer (a box that appears over the page) that splits the screen so the product choices stay visible.
  • Sets (sold together, older advice, Source A 2021): matching sofas and love seats should be sold together with the recliners they match, because customers buy complete sets. Keep unrelated products out of the store, with set-based exceptions.
  • Sets, rule: sell bundles only when the product naturally comes in sets. Example: dining chairs sold in packs of four at about $200. Do not turn single items priced around $50 into bundles just to reach a higher price.
  • Avatar bundles: packages designed around one specific type of buyer, such as entrepreneur, traveler, biohacker, essential and longevity. Use them in promotions to match the offer to each customer type.
  • Family packs: bundles such as a family pack of 3 to 4 products work well. They can be promoted on a specific collection page (a page that groups related products).
  • Two-family bundle: instead of $70 for one unit, offer two units for $60 total. This appeals to families buying gifts for multiple households.
  • Multi-pair bundles for shoes: buyers often buy several pairs in different colors or variants at the same time. Offer multi-pair bundles next to the single-pair option. A variant is another version of the same product, such as another size or color.
  • Subscriptions as a bundle layer: offers can scale with quantity and subscription. A subscription is a recurring delivery the customer signs up for. Options include bigger discounts for more units, extra savings for a monthly subscription, and a "frequently bought together" add-on at a discount.
  • Buy More Save More: this suits direct-to-consumer brands (brands that sell straight to the buyer) of consumables such as food, drinks and makeup. It can also lead first-time buyers into a subscription, because they try an offer before deciding to subscribe.

Bundling with an upgrade

  • Bundling combines a main product with an add-on you already sell as an upgrade, and gives it one bundle price. Example: a $400 golf bag and a $100 trolley sold together for $450, promoted as a single package for a few days.
  • A bundle works as a re-engagement tool (a way to win back people who looked but did not buy), because it is a new offer the customer has not seen before. Its price should be close to the cost of the main product plus the upgrade. Add low-cost accessories so the margin stays acceptable.
  • Case: a golf bag sold for $400 and a carrier upgrade for $100. For one month, the store offered the bag, the carrier, 24 golf balls, a glove and a cleaning kit together for $500. The extra cost to the store was only slightly higher than the bag and carrier alone. The package gave a reason to contact old leads (people who viewed the bag but did not buy).

Choosing bundles by stage and starting point

4 min
  • Start with one product. Add a bundle or a second product once the first one shows demand. Selling several products can also build trust, depending on the niche. A niche is a small market segment.
  • When starting out, focus on one product at a time rather than a bundle. Bundles are harder to sell and harder for a beginner to test.
  • Start with simple bundles that make sense for the product. Do not add complex bundle structures until the store is scaling. Scaling means growing sales.
  • Keep one clear offer per store rather than several different offers for different categories. This avoids confusing customers, unless the store is large enough to manage it. The overall goal is to raise AOV.
  • Replace standard time-limited percentage sales with bundle offers, such as buy two get 20% off plus a free gift, or buy one and get a complementary product free. In one store, this increased conversions and AOV.
  • Changing an offer from 50% off plus free shipping to buy one get one free or buy two get two free made a major difference in one seller's sales.
  • Bundles at the first step of the shop raise AOV. A product priced at $39 can be sold as two or three units to bring the order value to $70 to $80. A bundle raises margin per order without raising the ad cost per click (what you pay each time someone clicks your ad).
  • Bundles plus post-purchase upsells (an extra offer shown after the first purchase) are a way to increase margin when a single product is priced too low. Reported results: about 70 percent of customers chose a bundle, and AOV rose from $39 to $110 across the store.
  • Bundle at the first step, then upsell. A single-product store that averaged $40 per order introduced buy two, get an extra 20% off. Two items cost about $70. Shipping per unit fell when two units shipped together. AOV rose from $40 to $70, with higher profit per order.
  • Expanding a single-product store into a multi-product store with bundles and extra items raises AOV. Multi-buy offers encouraged customers to buy more.
  • Bundles raise AOV for the store. A store with higher AOV can justify a higher cost per acquisition (CPA, what it costs to win one new customer) when scaling. Increasing AOV through bundles, upsells, higher-priced offers or a higher price raises profit per order.
  • Quantity bundles aim to raise average order value, revenue per customer and profit per order at the same time. They work as a conversion lever (something that pushes more buyers to buy), because each order contains more units.
  • Bundle structure matters more than the single-unit price. A visible quantity ladder with a clear saving per tier pushes multi-unit orders. Keep the ladder short, so a buyer can compare the tiers in a few seconds.
  • Bundles raise AOV and also take up more space in Google Shopping ads (product ads shown in Google search results), because each bundle is a different product listing. Bundles can be given their own UPC code (a barcode number that identifies a product), which allows listing them on eBay.
  • A bundle is made by building one product page that includes several products, with the bundle price above the individual sum. No app is needed for a basic bundle.

Bundle strategy by price point

  • Low-priced products (about $5 to $10) in paid ads: a bundle can raise AOV to a level that covers ad costs. The bundle must still leave a margin of about $20 to $25 per customer.
  • Low-priced products in general: bundle offers such as buy two, buy three or buy four and get one free work well for small, cheap products. Test several and keep the one that makes sense for the margin.
  • Low-priced kitchen products: quantity discounts can encourage customers who want multiples to buy more. Test quantity offers with the product's natural use cases.
  • Competitor example at low price: a competitor sold a cheap item at $24.99 for one unit, $29.99 for two units, and a larger pack at a higher price. It used bundling to raise margin per order. The product was judged a potential winner because the competitor ran 280 active ads for it.
  • Cheap add-on check: if a cheap add-on of about 2 to 3 dollars shows a good take rate of around 40 to 50 percent, a bundle with a total price up to about $40 can make sense. The extra margin from the add-on can justify the bundle.
  • Mid-priced products: a bundle offer for two items is typically priced about 20% below the single price. The second item can be a different variant of the same product, for example another size or color.
  • Higher-priced products: a seller of a $79 device has a bundle example below. Price the bundles so the best-selling option is the obvious deal.
  • Thin-margin products (cheap items): see the pricing procedure below.

Designing the tier ladder

9 min

Tier structures

  • Create tiered offers with a simple layout. Buy 1, Buy 2 and Buy 3 quantity tiers with progressive discounts (discounts that grow with each tier) are used for a single-product store.
  • Multi-tier discounts, such as "buy any 2 pieces, get the 3rd one 50% off," encourage multiple items per cart order while keeping profit per unit. A cart is the list of items a buyer has chosen.
  • Pre-selected starter kits: show the six most popular combinations with the items included. This is better than making the customer pick from a long list. Customers who face around 70 stone options and 11 setting options cannot judge which choice is best.
  • Replace a custom bundle builder with ready-made bundles and an extra 10% off the pre-created bundle. Fewer decisions and clicks can help lift AOV.
  • Keep the single-unit price low for entry. Then make the larger quantity or the subscription the default that drives value. Visitors are more likely to accept a bigger option when it is preselected.
  • Price a single unit intentionally higher than the cost structure suggests (for example $25). Then the bundle offer (for example 2 units for $30) looks like an exceptional value. This raises the bundle attachment rate (the share of buyers who add the bundle), even for low-margin products.

Necklace tier ladder (case; prices as stated, store prices in EUR, costs in USD):

Tier Offer Notes

2 Gift One necklace at 29.90 EUR plus a free complementary second necklace (valued at 29.90 EUR) Raised conversion rate; the price increase was needed to fund the gift
3 Buy one, get two Buy one at 29.90 EUR and get two additional, different necklaces free; all items ship in one package Cost of goods about 13 USD for the bundle, margin about 17
4 Big Boss 29.90 EUR for one necklace, plus a second necklace, a jewelry box (about 1 USD cost), a free ebook or lookbook, and free shipping Maximum perceived value; the highest-value offer in this text

Case behind the ladder: an African-map necklace was sold in a Shopify store at 19.90 EUR. It was aimed at people with an African background who want to show their origin. The dream outcome was to look good in it and show love for one's roots. The basic single item was offered first. The price was raised to 29.90 EUR, and a complementary second necklace valued at 29.90 EUR was added as a free gift. This raised conversion. The next tested offer was buy one for 29.90 EUR and get two more different necklaces free, shipped in one package. Its cost of goods was about 13 USD. The final Big Boss offer added a jewelry box (about 1 USD), a free ebook or lookbook (a photo guide for styling and care), and free shipping. Each stage raised conversion. The bundle kept a margin of about 17 at 29.90 EUR. When cost rises, the price must rise too.

Necklace variant: the necklace sold for 29.99 with a free choice of country. Bundles were offered in which customers who bought four items received two for free.

Badges and labels

1 min
  • Competitor stores mark their best-value bundle with a Most Popular badge (a small label on the product).
  • Use the badge to guide buyers toward the bundle with the highest margin or volume, once you know which one it is.
  • Label the 2-pack as "Most Popular" and the 3-pack as "Best Value".
  • Add small icons, such as a gift and a truck, to make the tiers easier to scan (quick to read at a glance).
  • Label one bundle as "most popular" and highlight it visually to steer shoppers toward the higher-value option.
  • A clearly marked default reduces choice effort (how hard it is to pick) and lifts AOV (average order value, how much one buyer spends per order) more than an unmarked list of equal options.
  • Highlight the middle bundle tier with a most popular label and add free shipping to it.
  • This steers most buyers toward the bundle with the higher order value and better margin for the store.
  • Badge sets seen in the sources:
    • Most Popular on the 2-pack, Best Value on the 3-pack.
    • Most Popular on Buy 2, Best Offer on Buy 3, with Buy 2 at Save 50% and Buy 3 at Save 55%.
    • Best Offer on the top bundle at $89.99.
    • Best Seller on the two-pack in the sock ladder (a set of tiers that step up in size).
    • Most Popular on the 60-day tier and Best Deal on the 90-day tier.
  • A badge sits on the bundle that tests as best (margin or volume), not on the bundle you want to push by default.
  • Competitors' badge placement is a signal, not a rule.
  • Show the regular price next to the bundle price to make the saving clear.
  • Show the saving on each tier so the customer can compare them in a few seconds.

Naming tiers and bundles

1 min
  • Creative names create psychological appeal (a feeling that makes the offer attractive) and justify premium pricing (a higher price).
  • Examples: "Elite Growth Kit," "Stylist Bundle," "The Complete Acne Killer," "Twin Pack."
  • Generic names such as "Bundle 2" leave perceived value (how valuable the buyer thinks it is) on the table.
  • Example: a luxury hair laser at $1,999 alone can be repackaged as an "Elite Growth Kit" with a 5-month supply and extended warranty, justifying approximately $300 additional cost through distinctive naming.
  • Generic bundle names fail to create perceived value or justify premium pricing, compared with names that suggest benefits, status or use cases.
  • Name bundle tiers to reflect shared use cases or paired scenarios, such as "Twin Pack," "Room for Two," "Apartment Bundle," "Partner Kit."
  • This appeals to customers buying for multiple users and increases AOV through contextual appeal (a name that fits the buyer's situation).
  • Give each bundle a name that describes the outcome, such as a "sleep bundle" or "back and neck bundle."
  • Combined bundles with a shared problem (for example neck and back pain) sell well.
  • Sleep bundle example: priced at $79.95 against a regular combined price of $126, shown clearly on the landing page (the page a visitor arrives on from an ad).
  • Label each bundle by the length of supply it covers, such as 10-day, 30-day, 60-day, 120-day or one-year supply, not only by quantity.
  • This lets buyers compare the cost per day across tiers.
  • Tier labels for a three-tier pack: 30-day, 60-day, 90-day supply.

Pricing each tier from its own cost

5 min

Rules

  • Before launching a bundled offer, calculate the per-unit costs for all items combined.
  • Verify that the bundled price still allows healthy margins.
  • Bundling power collapses if unit economics (the money made or lost on each unit) do not support profitability.
  • Set bundle pricing from cost of goods (what the product costs you to buy, called COGS for short) and margin.
  • First calculate cost and margin for each option.
  • Then ensure the best bundle still gives a healthy margin even when a second unit is sold at 50 percent off.
  • Each bundle quantity must be priced on its own supplier cost, not as a simple multiple of the single-unit price.
  • A two-piece bundle is priced from the two-piece cost, which is why $45.95 for two is not double the single price.
  • Use a fixed bundle total (one set price for the whole bundle) instead of a percentage discount.
  • This shows the customer a clear per-unit price and the seller avoids discount math.
  • Pick a total where the per-unit price drops clearly from the single price to make the saving obvious.
  • Check the bundle saving against the sum of the single prices.
  • Saving percentage = (combined value minus bundle price) divided by bundle price.
  • Example: a bundle priced at 500 with a combined value of 680 gives (680 minus 500) divided by 500 = 36 percent.
  • A bundle at 29.99 for two items against 59.99 full price gives a $10 saving on the pair, about 16.7% off the full price.
  • Example: a single item at $29.99 and two items for $49.99 instead of $59.99.
  • Before launching a bundle like this, check that the bundle price still covers product cost, acquisition cost (what it costs to win one customer) and fees.
  • A bundle with a total price up to about 40 dollars can make sense if a cheap add-on has a 40 to 50 percent take rate (the share of buyers who add the extra item).
  • Profit check: a buy-4-get-4-free offer on a home decor product left about $30 margin per order for one student.
  • If the profit is too thin at the current price, improve the offer rather than only changing ads.
  • For example, give two items at a discounted total price while keeping a larger margin.
  • The offer change must still leave enough margin after the cost of the extra item.

Pricing examples

  • Thin-margin procedure: set the single unit at 25 dollars (or 21, or 19.99), and pre-select a 2-unit bundle at 30 dollars.
  • Mark the bundle with labels such as "lowest price" or "most popular" and add value indicators.
  • Customers often purchase bundles because the offer feels compelling. Test with bundle apps (add-on tools that build bundles in the shop) such as Catching Bundle, Bundle Bear or White Bundle.
  • Two-item bundle priced about 20% below the single price (see the mid-priced section).
  • A single bottle at $19.95 paired with a Buy Two fixed total of $29.99, which works out to $14.98 per bottle and saves about $10 against two singles.
  • A Buy Three fixed total of $38.95 brings it to $12.98 per bottle, and the saving against a $60.00 compare-at price (the higher "old" price shown crossed out) shows as $21.05.
  • Book pricing formulas (standard price 41.95 USD):
    • Bundle price per tier = unit price × quantity × (1 − discount rate).
    • Worked example: with a 39.99 unit price, a 15% discount on two units gives 67.98 and a 25% discount on three gives 89.98.
    • The displayed prices are rounded to 67.99 and 89.99 so they end in.99.
    • Divide the bundle total by the quantity to see the effective price per unit (about 33.99 for the 2-pack and 30.00 for the 3-pack).
