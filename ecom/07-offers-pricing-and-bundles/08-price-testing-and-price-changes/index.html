Price testing and price changes

Section 7.8 · 8 of 28 in this chapter · 14 min read

In short

  • Test the price both ways, using real sales numbers and not guesses.
  • Raise the price in small steps and watch whether buyers keep buying.
  • Keep the price that gives the best profit and the most sales.
  • A price that is too low can make a product look cheap.
This section has 14 parts. Tap one to open it.

Starting prices and launch

1 min
  • Launch low, at a price such as $49 or $59, to collect pixel data (tracking records of visits and sales). Raise the price once the data gives a clear answer.
  • A low launch price can make a product look cheap. How price affects the offer depends on the product.
  • Test a higher price before you decide that a product does not work.
  • If a price does not feel uncomfortable, it is probably too low. Raise it and measure the response before deciding otherwise.
  • A price that feels uncomfortably low can mean the offer is far below the value it gives. A deliberately low entry price can be a good choice when the value is clearly higher.
  • Review prices at least once a year. Raise them as results improve. Keeping the same price for a year is already too long.
  • Start with a small discount. Raise prices step by step as reviews grow. This protects margin (the profit left after costs) while building trust.
  • When competitors are much cheaper and you cannot match them, start with a competitive price to get sales. After you confirm profitable sales, invest in custom videos, images and testimonials (customer quotes). Then raise the price.
  • If competitors list a product above your price, you may raise your price to match. Test the change for about one week. Watch how conversions (sales from visits) respond before keeping it.

Price validation phase

1 min

Procedure: pricing for a product validation test (a short test to see if a product sells at all).

  1. Set the selling price close to the competitor price, for example 29.99 dollars, even if the margin is only about 10 dollars.
  2. Use this low-margin phase only to check whether the product sells at all.
  3. Once the product works, raise the price or lower costs until the margin after ad spend (money paid for ads), taxes and transaction fees (charges from the payment company) is about 20 dollars per sale.

Testing prices down when nothing sells

1 min
  • Example: a product with fulfillment cost (the cost to pack and ship an order) of about 20 USD was first sold at 39.99 USD without sales. The price was lowered to 34.99 USD to find out whether price was the blocker.

Procedure: price testing sequence.

  1. Lower the price and check whether sales start. This tests whether price is the blocker.
  2. If sales start at the lower price, lower the fulfillment cost so the product still earns a good margin at that price.
  3. If there are still no sales at the lower price, the problem is more likely the landing page (the page buyers see after clicking an ad), the theme (the store's design template) or another factor, not price.
  4. If sales start, raise the price step by step (for example 34.99, 39.99, 44.99). Watch the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) and margin.
  5. Keep the price that gives a good margin and the highest conversion rate.
  • Price tests should show whether price makes a difference. The best price is the one with a good margin and the highest conversion rate.

Testing prices up

3 min
  • Test prices in both directions, including prices well above the current one. Price is one of the first things to test on any offer, even when changing it looks inconvenient.
  • Example: a product sold at $47 was changed to $97 with the same sales page and copy. Conversion stayed the same, so revenue doubled and profit rose sharply.
  • When conversion rate is good during ad runs, test higher prices. Upsells (extra offers after a purchase) and bundles (several items sold together for one price) are where most profit is made.
  • Raising the price can increase profit without losing sales. The perceived value (how valuable buyers think a product is) can rise with the price.
  • In one price test, a $15 increase produced a large improvement in return on ad spend (ROAS, sales made per dollar spent on ads) and profit. A $5 step had little effect. Larger steps worked where small ones did not. Results differ by product and audience.
  • Raising the price by a larger amount may be needed to see a difference. A small increase of about $5 may show no difference, so test about $15.
  • Raising prices can increase profit even if some customers leave. The extra margin on the remaining sales can be larger than the lost volume (number of sales). Test with an acceptable loss of volume in mind. Do not assume the market will reject the increase.
  • Do not raise the selling price while leaving ad spend unchanged. This usually fails. The ads keep spending while sales drop, especially when many competitors sell the same product. First, find other ways to add revenue, such as cross-sells (a related product offered at checkout), upgrades or geo-targeting (showing offers by country or region). Only then try a blanket (across-the-board) price increase.
  • When inventory (the stock you hold) runs low and demand is high, raise the price to slow sales. This keeps stock until the reorder (the next order from your supplier) arrives. It keeps momentum and profit without a stockout (running out of stock).
  • Price each design by its demand. Test prices upward until sales slow. Then keep the highest price that still sells well for each design.
  • Example: a blanket design was tested from 50 to 60 to 70 and settled at about 70 to 80. Designs that sold well stayed at the higher price. Slower designs were priced lower and not reordered.
  • Test pricing per product, not always by lowering it.
  • Example: a home decor product sold at $19.90 had weak sales. Buyers who would have bought it judged it too cheap. After the price was raised to at least $30, sales started to come in.
  • Example: a home decor product at 19.90 USD drew visits but almost no sales. After the price was raised to 31.90 USD, sales started with a conversion rate of about 3 to 4 percent.
  • Value through branding (the name and look of a brand), support and a guarantee can justify a higher price. Price testing matters because a low price can make a product look cheap or less valuable to buyers.
  • Example: a product started at $49 and was later sold at $90 by adding better branding and custom content. It was the same underlying item.
  • Example: a product started at $49.95 and was later sold at $89.95 with no drop in sales pace. More money went into custom content, lifestyle images and the landing page. Raising the price this way depends on perceived value, not on undercutting competitors.
  • Example: a supplement launched at $22.95 and was increased to $28.95 after reviews had built up. The first increase, made when review count was low, caused a conversion drop. The same increase later succeeded once social proof (signs that other buyers trust it, such as reviews) was higher. Higher prices work when customer trust, shown by reviews, supports them.

Step sizes and increment rules

3 min

Procedure: price optimization on a performing offer (an offer that already sells well).

  1. Confirm the offer is performing. It needs a high ad CTR (click-through rate, the share of people who click an ad) and a high store conversion rate, with an acceptable profit margin.
  2. Increase the product price by €2 every 2 days.
  3. Track conversion rate and profit margin closely after each price increase.
  4. Continue daily price increases (€2 every 2 days) until conversion rate drops noticeably (typically a 10-20% decline).
  5. Stop increasing immediately when the conversion decline appears.
  6. Return to the previous price point that achieved both the highest conversion rate and the highest profit margin.
  7. Lock in that price as the baseline until offer components or market conditions change.

Procedure: stepwise increase on a working offer.

  1. Once the offer, the ads and the traffic (visitors) all perform, raise the price in steps of 2 currency units (for example from 29.90 to 31.90).
  2. Watch the conversion rate at each step.
  3. Keep raising the price until buyers stop buying.
  4. Return to the last price that gave the best combination of conversion rate, CTR and margin.
  5. Price increases do not work for every product. For products where a higher price signals higher quality, it can raise conversion.
  • Case: a shoe product was tested last year with a starting price of 29.90. The price was increased in steps of about 2 until buyers stopped buying at 49.90, then lowered back. 44.90 gave the best conversion rate, the best CTR and the highest margin, about 15 more per sale than the starting price.

Procedure: price increase testing method.

  1. Raise the price by $5 each time you add value, such as custom videos or better product images.
  2. Make price increases about every two weeks.
  3. Test each new price for about five days.
  4. If sales stay consistent, keep the higher price.
  5. If sales dip consistently after about one week, revert (go back) to the previous price. Work on branding before raising prices again.

Procedure: test an increase on a product that is already selling.

  1. Test a price increase of about $5 to $10.
  2. Wait about 3 to 4 days and check whether sales continue at a consistent rate.
  3. If sales hold, keep the new price. If they drop, test again at a smaller step or improve the value first.
  4. Repeat price testing as the product's branding and proof improve.

Price and offer A/B tests

1 min
  • An A/B test shows two versions to different visitors to see which sells better. Test price changes this way. Price tests should show whether price makes a difference. Read conversion rate and margin clearly for each price.
  • Test price early. Price is one of the first elements to test on any offer.
  • Value changes, such as custom content or better images, can come before a price rise. Test each change against the price it supports.

Per-market price tests

1 min
  • Run the price A/B test separately for each market: UK, Australia and US. It is not run as one global test.
  • Example: a product auto-converted to 50 AUD (Australian dollars, converted by the store on its own) converted poorly. After the Australian price was set to 49.90 AUD, the conversion rate went up. Set a separate price for every market. Automatic conversion often produces a round number that does not convert well.
  • Lomo (also written Lo Meo) is a paid tool for dynamic price testing, where prices change on their own during the test. It changes prices per country and currency. It offers a seven-day free trial, which allows a seven-day price test across markets.
  • Google Optimize (a Google tool for testing page versions) probably cannot change prices dynamically. A fixed price test would be needed for each country.
  • Google Optimize cannot change prices dynamically per currency. It is better for testing headlines or static elements (parts of a page that do not change).

Pricing automation

1 min

Procedure: setting an automatic pricing rule in AutoDS (a tool that changes product prices by rule).

  1. Open Automations at the top and click Add automation.
  2. Name it automatic pricing and click Next.
  3. Keep 'raise price by sales performance' on.
  4. Set the condition: number of sales higher than 20 per week, then increase the price by $2.
  5. Add the mirror rule (a second rule that works in reverse): number of sales lower than 20 per week, then decrease the price by $2.
  6. Click Next, select the product, and click Finish.
  • The aim is to find the highest price where sales start to fall, since that price gives the best margin.
  • Dynamic pricing through AutoDS is meant to find the price point where sales start to fall. That way, the highest price that still sells can be identified. The 20-orders-per-week threshold (the sales count that triggers a change) and the $2 step are the starting test values shown here. They are not universal rules.

Price changes visible to customers

1 min
  • A rich product pin (a type of shared post that shows live product details) pulls the price dynamically from the store. Lowering the price, for example during a discount, sends a price-change notification to every user who pinned that product. The store owner does not need to do anything extra to trigger it.
  • Prices update only when a supplier changes its MAP policy (the minimum advertised price, the lowest price a seller may advertise). Store prices are not reviewed continuously against the market. Discounts do not change this rule.
  • Small price changes, such as $19.97 to $21.97, may not change buyer behavior but still improve profit. Check the margin effect of each price step before changing a price.

Announcing price rises

1 min
  • Announce a fixed cut-off date. State that orders placed before the date get the current price, and later orders pay the new price. Give both figures in the letter so the reader can compare them.
  • Announce a known future cost increase with its reason and date. For example, paper, printing and binding costs had more than doubled. Buyers respond to a believable cost reason more readily than to an unexplained price rise.
  • When announcing a price rise to existing customers, hold one or two units at the old price for them. State the exact percentage increase. Explain the reason for the rise so the exception looks believable. A specific limited hold works better than a general loyalty claim.
  • Before a known supplier price increase, announce a dated last-chance promotion. Run it together with an email campaign, social posts and a countdown on product pages until the new prices take effect.

Protecting buyers after a price drop

1 min
  • Avoid making buyers feel that a product they just bought is now cheaper. After a large order, if the product goes on sale within one or two weeks, send the buyer a free product instead of a discount. This protects trust and repeat purchases.

Raising prices after validation

1 min
  • Raising the product price by $5 (from $44 to $49) during the scaling phase (growing ads and sales after a test) lifted the margin of the product.
  • A product price was raised by $5, from $44 to $49. The store's margin improved while sales continued. Price changes combined with post-purchase extras (extra offers after buying) were one of the steps used to improve profitability.
  • Check the margin of each price step before and after the change, not only the sales count.
  • Review prices at least once a year and raise them as results improve.

Relaunching after a failed increase

1 min

Procedure: if raising the price causes sales to drop sharply.

  1. Relaunch the product with new campaigns, a new ad account or a new pixel (tracking code) before deciding.
  2. If it still does not sell profitably at the higher price, stop selling it.
  3. Do not keep a product running at a loss in the hope that the price can be raised later.
  • The first increase in the supplement example failed at low review count. The same increase later succeeded once social proof was higher. Timing the increase against trust matters.

Sanity-checking a price with outsiders

1 min
  • Ask a few people outside your team whether a price feels credible for the product. Their reaction is a cheap first signal before running paid tests.
