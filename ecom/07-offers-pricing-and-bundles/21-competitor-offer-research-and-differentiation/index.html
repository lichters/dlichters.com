Competitor offer research and differentiation

Section 7.21 · 21 of 28 in this chapter · 14 min read

In short

  • Study competitors first. Look at what other shops sell to your buyers. Without that, you cannot tell if your offer is different.
  • Judge your offer by the buyer's eyes. Buyers compare shops side by side. Your offer must look better, not just feel good to you.
  • Do not win on price alone. Add value: bonus items, guarantees, faster delivery, and extras.
  • Test before you roll out. Measure any new offer on a small scale before using it everywhere.

Why competitor research comes first

1 min
  • Skipping competitor research leads to guessing. A competitor is another shop that sells to your buyers. Without research, you cannot judge whether your offer is different. **
  • A quality offer is one buyers see as unique, or at least partly unique, next to what competitors sell in the same market. "Offer" means everything the buyer gets for their money: product, price, bonuses, delivery and guarantee. What counts is how buyers see it, not your own view of the product. **
  • The offer does not have to be the best in the world. It must be better than competitor offers, ideally clearly better. Buyers can open several competitor tabs at once. A weak offer is easy to spot. **
  • Competitors selling the same product should not stop you. You win with a stronger offer structure: bundled bonuses (extra items packed in with the main product), guarantees, and benefit-focused names (names that say what the buyer gains). You also win with fast fulfillment (packing and shipping orders). You do not need exclusive rights to the product. A strong offer and fast operations beat competitors with identical products and weak offers.

Identifying real competitors

1 min
  • Do not count products from low-volume one-off sellers as direct competitors. These are sellers who list a few items and sell rarely, such as many Etsy shops (a marketplace for small handmade or vintage shops). They usually hold no large inventory (stock kept on hand). **
  • If competitors sell near Amazon prices (a giant online marketplace), they are often Chinese sellers. Avoid very large brands. Small competitors whose ads get about 500-3,000 likes or engagements (likes, comments and shares) can be out-marketed.
  • When competitors buy cheap and ship through AliExpress (a large marketplace for low-cost goods from Asian suppliers), they keep very low margins (the profit left after costs). The same product can often sell at a higher price. Their short-lived stores build no brand, which leaves room for a stronger store.

Building the competitor offer map

1 min

Core rule and procedure

1 min
  • Compare your offer with competitor offers, using the same offer elements (the same parts of the deal, such as price, bonuses and delivery). This shows where you stand. Where you are behind, improve the offer first. Record the changes in the curve offer builder (the offer-building tool named here). **

Procedure **:

  1. Open the competitor map you built earlier. It lists each competitor's offer and USP (unique selling proposition, the one clear reason a buyer should pick you). Review it. If it does not exist yet, first run a competitor-research process and fill it in.
  2. Compare your current offer with competitor offers, using the same offer elements. Judge where you stand in your market.
  3. Where you are behind, improve your offer toward the strongest curve offer you can. Record the changes in the curve offer builder.

Comparing offer elements

1 min

Multi-buy tiers

1 min
  • A competitor used tiered offers (deals with steps, each giving more). Buy 2 get 1 free was for visible improvement, with free shipping. Buy 3 get 2 free was for real results, with a free post-serum jelly. A serum is a liquid skincare product. Free shipping applied to orders over $40.
  • Competitor stores often use multi-buy offers (the buyer gets extra units for buying more). Examples are buy 2 get 1 free and buy 3 get 2 free. For longer supplies, they use buy 2 get 2 free and buy 3 get 3 free. Test which tier structure sells best before you expand it.
  • Bundle offers (several items sold together) seen in the field include:
    • buy 1 get 1 free (2 bottles in total)
    • buy 2 get 2 free (4 bottles in total)
    • buy 3 get 3 free (6 bottles in total)
    • a two-bottle pack with free bonus champagne
  • Buy-one-get-one-free offers appear on competitor stores. The fashion niche (a narrow market of clothing and style) uses them as a visible hook. You can test a similar form.
  • Common competitor offers include buy one get one free. A stronger differentiator (a feature that sets you apart) is a free ebook or guide given with the purchase. Pair offers with an authority signal (proof that makes you look trustworthy, such as reviews or press mentions). This makes the store look more credible.

Subscriptions

1 min
  • Competitor stores offer an automatic refill (the order repeats on a schedule without new action from the buyer) or subscription, with a saving of around 20 to 25 percent. Show the saving next to the subscription option, so it is clearly compared to the one-time price.
  • A competitor's serum page combined multi-unit tiers (Buy 2 Get 1 FREE, Buy 3 Get 2 FREE) with an Auto-Refill subscription that saves 25 percent and a 90-day guarantee (a promise to make things right if the buyer is not happy, here for 90 days). Multi-unit tiers plus a subscription discount are a common structure to model when pricing a bundle.
  • The same competitor's tiered offer also included a monthly auto-refill at 25% off with cancel anytime.

Shipping thresholds and delivery

1 min
  • Competitors often advertise free shipping only above a threshold (a minimum order amount), such as $49. If you offer free shipping on all orders, make that the main message. Place an expiring coupon code (a discount code that stops working after a set date) in the header bar (the strip across the top of the website) above it.
  • The estimated ship date (the expected day the order leaves) and the delivery window (the expected arrival time range) are easy to add to product pages. They can separate you from a competitor that never mentions arrival time. **
  • A competitor charged $150 for white glove delivery (the item is carried inside, unpacked and set up) and $99 for Room and Choice service (a named add-on delivery service). Basic threshold delivery was included, meaning the item was dropped off the truck at the curb or door.
  • A $1,500 chandelier with free white glove delivery beats a competitor that adds $150 for the same service. Shoppers comparing both sites will choose the store with the free option.
  • If margins allow, include white glove delivery free on selected products. Otherwise, sell it as a paid premium upgrade (an extra the buyer pays for).

Guarantees and warranties

1 min
  • A competitor's serum page put a visible 90-day guarantee and a free-shipping message right next to the add-to-cart button (the button that puts an item in the shopping basket) and the price. A risk reversal (a promise that moves the buying risk off the buyer) placed next to the buy button is worth copying when you test a product page.
  • If a competitor mentions a one-year warranty (a promise to repair or replace a broken item) but does not emphasize it, highlight the warranty prominently. **
  • Guarantees are part of a superior offer structure that competitors selling identical products often lack.

Bonuses, accessories and gifts

1 min
  • Include accessories such as light bulbs as a free gift when the supplier (the company that sends you the goods) provides them at a good price per order. Free inclusion beats a competitor that says the item is not included.
  • Bonus items such as wrist rest pads with desk purchases are a way to add value. **
  • If a competitor's top sellers in Google Shopping (product ads inside Google search) have similar photos, plain websites, a bundle and a pop-up (a box that appears over a page), you can beat them. Offer something they lack, such as a free extra accessory or a free gift on the first order.

Price, total cost and margin

1 min
  • A competitor's low item price may be offset by a separate shipping charge. Compare the total cost at checkout (the final payment step), not the displayed product price. Otherwise, your margin estimate for a cheap product can be wrong.
  • A competitor listed a product below the MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price a brand lets sellers show publicly). Compare the full price a customer would pay. That includes sales tax (tax added at checkout), shipping and any discount the competitor adds. A lower listed price can still end up higher than yours at checkout. That changes whether the listing is a real threat.

Discounts

1 min
  • When a competitor runs a discount on the same product, beat it with a stronger offer. For example, move from 50 percent off to 70 percent off. Or raise the compare-at price (the crossed-out higher price shown beside the sale price). Or add a buy-one-get-one-free deal. Keep the ad copy (the words in your ad) the same while you improve the offer.
  • If a competitor offers 50 percent off, beat it with 70 percent off, buy-one-get-one-free, or a higher compare-at price, so the offer looks larger.
  • In the most competitive product brands, find out which discounts your top competitors give. Be ready to match them. Experts disagree on best practice for discounts, so the right level depends on your specific competition.

Customization

1 min
  • Products with customization options (the buyer can personalize them) can sell at higher prices. Competitors often do not build the full options. This can be a differentiator for a store. **

Verifying competitor prices

1 min
  • The same product showed different prices across one brand's pages and ads. Check the price on each page and each ad before you rely on it. A two-formulation bundle (two versions of the product sold together) under $40 was promoted with free shipping on TikTok. The bundle price was a repeated USP.
  • One source recalled a starting price of about $48 for a blender. They were unsure whether the price had gone up or down as the product became popular. Verify the exact price history before you rely on it.
  • Price points from research, as examples:
    • a nostalgia gift toy (a toy that recalls childhood memories) at $39.99 USD or CHF 34.99 (Swiss francs), with a buy-one-get-one-50%-off offer
    • a customized sister jewelry gift at $19.99, with 148 active ads (ads running now) in Canada, Australia and the US

Finding gaps

1 min

Feature gaps

1 min

Pattern **:

  • Feature-gap differentiation (standing out by solving a need no one else solves): find a need that repeats in customer feedback and that no competitor addresses. Then build the product around solving it.
  • Pattern: find the single complaint or request mentioned repeatedly that the only available option does not solve. Make the solution the core of your version.
  • Example 1: a camping stove seller notices customers complain about bulk (too large to carry). They launch a compact version (a smaller version) that packs into a single pouch.
  • Example 2: a kitchen scale seller hears that users want to weigh liquids without a cup. They launch a scale with a tare-friendly (able to reset to zero with a container on it), removable bowl.

Competitor weak spots

1 min
  • Competitors without promotions (sales offers), urgency (a reason to buy now), bundles or bonus offers leave room for you to win.
  • When a competitor offers free shipping on heavy or large items, check how they make money on it. Review their individual reviews, since some competitors lose money without knowing it. **
  • Read competitor reviews (customer ratings and comments) to see why a competitor's price is low.

Creating a unique offer

1 min

Improve the offer, not only the price

1 min
  • Differentiate from competitors by improving their offer. You can offer lower pricing, bundle multiple units, or reframe the product message for new use cases (new ways people can use it).
  • To beat a competitor in a crowded niche (a narrow market with many sellers), improve the offer rather than only the price. Keep the same product. Add value, such as a free gift, or a lower price with an extra item.
  • Example: a competitor sells a necklace at 40 USD. A competitor-beating offer might be 30 USD plus a free ring.
  • Improve the offer to compete in a new niche, such as jewelry, instead of copying competitors. Offer more value for the same or less money. For example, a lower price with a free extra item.
  • A competitor charges 40 USD for a necklace. Undercutting (selling cheaper than a rival) to 30 USD and adding a free ring is an offer that positions the store above the competition.
  • Check what competitors offer before setting up your own bundles. Then your offers are not weaker than the market standard (what most sellers already offer).

Brand value

1 min
  • If the same product sells cheaper on Amazon, a brand-owned store can still charge more. Add complementary items (items that go well with the product) or a stronger offer. One seller reported pricing 15 USD above the Amazon average and still selling well. Perceived value (how valuable buyers think it is) and branding are the levers, not matching the lowest price.

Tested differentiation

1 min
  • Customized offers that competitors do not make stand out more than free shipping, free white-glove delivery, bonus gifts or 10% off. Test and measure them before a wider rollout. **

Avoiding easily copied terms

1 min
  • Avoid offers that rivals can copy, such as matching guarantees or undercutting. Once rivals adopt them, your advantage disappears. Find an offer structure that is hard to match.
  • Free shipping, free white-glove delivery, bonus gifts or 10% off are easy for competitors to copy. They do not create separation. **
  • Shoppers often value a strong bonus offer more than a competitor's longer history. Compete on the offer rather than on store age.
  • When supplier copies happen (other sellers get the same goods from your supplier), run the same offer with suppliers that do not sell direct (sell straight to the public). Or build a more premium bundle with extras that the supplier does not offer. Offers cannot be copyrighted, so differentiate with better bundles.

Keeping the map current

1 min
  • Expect competitors to answer a successful store with bundles, packages or a redesigned site. Plan periodic reviews (regular checks) of offers and design. Then the store does not lose ground while it is being run.

Responding when competitors sell at MAP or below

1 min

Competitors sell at the same MAP price

1 min
  • When competitors all sell at the same MAP price, price cannot be the reason a shopper chooses you. Win on services, shipping, included accessories, bonuses and clear product information instead.
  • When selling at MAP, stand out through bonus offers and expiring coupon codes. These create differentiation without breaking the MAP policy (the brand's minimum price rule). **
  • When a competitor sells the same product at the same price, add a bonus offer that the shopper values highly to win the sale.

Competitors sell below MAP or non-MAP, and you are on MAP

1 min
  • If a competitor sells a product below the MAP price, or at a non-MAP price (a price from a seller not bound by the minimum price rule), do not match them on price. Matching leads to a race to the bottom (prices falling again and again). Revisit your own numbers for room to offer a special deal. Research the competitor's reviews to see why their price is low. **
  • If a non-MAP competitor sells the same product for less, do not start a price war (sellers cutting prices against each other). Run paid traffic (visitors from paid ads) only on products where the margin remains healthy. Send free traffic (visitors you get without paying) to the products where competitors undercut you.
  • When a competitor sells below your landed cost (the full cost to get the item to the buyer, including shipping and fees), their per-sale costs are usually higher than your margin. Per-sale costs include card processing fees, paid traffic, hosting, and staff for live chat, phone and email. Their price most likely reflects cheaper sourcing or shipping. Ask your supplier about better pricing before you react with a price cut. **

Retailer breaks MAP and the manufacturer does not enforce it

1 min

Options **:

  1. Document that you have followed the MAP policy and raised the issue with the manufacturer on several occasions.
  2. Remove the product from your store. Sell alternatives from other suppliers with better margins and support, if the gap is too large to compete with.
  3. Or match the lower price to stay on a level playing field (equal footing), accepting that the retailer may drop you from its supply.
  4. Or, instead of bidding harder in Google Shopping (paying more for ad spots), drive more organic or other traffic to that product page.
