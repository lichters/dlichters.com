Playbook › 7. Offers, pricing and bundles

Digital products, services, private label and direct-response offers

Section 7.26 · 26 of 28 in this chapter · 17 min read

In short

  • Pick one way to earn money first, then test it before you spend big.
  • Get a real sale before you finish a digital product, such as an ebook.
  • Check that each sale still leaves profit after ad costs.
  • Extra offers, such as bundles, upsells and memberships, earn more from the same buyer.
This section has 12 parts. Tap one to open it.

Choosing a monetization model

1 min

Monetization means the ways you earn money. Pick one model to start.

  1. Traditional dropshipping (a supplier ships each order, so you hold no stock).
    • Use a direct-response approach (ads that push people to buy right away).
  2. Private label products (products made by someone else and sold under your own brand), sold at average prices.
    • Ship them from a fulfillment center (a warehouse that packs and sends orders for you).
    • Use a different brand name.
    • Sell them through your own site and through other online stores.
  3. Custom recipes or subscription boxes (a box sent on a schedule), if your niche (the specific group of buyers you serve) supports them.
  • Set a steady sales baseline (your normal minimum of sales) that runs 7 days per week.
  • Weekly revenue (money in from sales) of $500 with 20 to 30 orders shows you are ready to invest in private labeling.

Digital products: validation and build

1 min
  • Validate demand (check that people actually want it) before you write the product.
    • Get a sale first.
    • Create only the cover and a landing page (a single web page that shows the offer).
    • Run ads.
    • If a sale comes in, tell the buyer the delivery window (the time until delivery) is 3 to 4 days.
    • Then write the ebook (an electronic book, usually a file such as a PDF).
  • Host the store on Shopify (an online store platform).
  • Build the landing page with a page builder tool such as PageFly.
  • Include the pricing and the offer on the page before the ebook exists.
  • Ebook length depends on the niche.
    • Check how long competing ebooks are.
    • Aim for maximum value with minimum effort.
    • A three-page ebook is probably too short for most niches.
    • There is no fixed page count, and 500 pages is not needed.
  • Test a digital product yourself before you sell it, because the file may contain errors.
  • A free ebook from a PLR library (a set of ready-made content you may rebrand; PLR stands for private label rights) can be given to buyers.
    • Use it as a bonus (an extra gift) or to build goodwill (good feeling and trust).
    • It adds perceived value (how valuable buyers think it is) without production cost.
    • Confirm the license allows giving it away first.
  • Planners, ebooks, and health or diet plans sell well around January, when people set resolutions (yearly goals).
    • Production cost is almost zero.
    • Add a logo through Fiverr (a marketplace for hiring freelancers).

Digital products: pricing and margins

1 min
  • Digital products have very high margins (the share of each sale kept as profit).
    • There is no cost of goods (what the item itself costs you).
    • One seller reports a profit margin of around 90 percent on a digital product.
    • Physical clothing products have lower margins.
  • Research the competitor price (what rival sellers charge) for the same type of digital product.
    • Set a similar price, then add more value so the offer looks better for the same money.
    • Check that the margin still covers the cost of acquiring one customer through ads (what one buyer costs you in ads) before you finalize the price.
  • Digital add-ons, such as an ebook, need no shipping.
    • The PDF (a file format that looks the same on any device) already exists, so they can be sold at low cost.
    • They work best when the ebook has real page count and visible value (value the customer can see) that is worth the add-on price.
  • Quantity tiers (price levels for buying more copies) on an ebook product page (example from 2026):

Bundles, upsells and cross-sells

2 min

An upsell is an extra offer to raise the order total. A cross-sell is an offer for a related product.

  • Example of an ebook bundle (a set of products sold together):
    • A single recipe ebook sells for 19 euros.
    • The bundle of all four problem ebooks costs 40 euros, instead of 80 euros when bought separately.
    • The bundle is the upsell used to raise average order value (the average amount one order brings in).
  • At the end of the ebook, offer a paid one-to-one call (a private session with you).
    • Price example: 100 to 200 dollars, booked through a booking link (a web link for picking a time).
    • Use it for personal questions the ebook cannot answer.
    • This turns a digital product buyer into a coaching client (someone who pays for personal guidance).
  • Match information products (digital goods that teach or inform) to the physical product a customer buys.
    • A power tool buyer can be offered a woodworking plans ebook (step-by-step building guides).
    • A drum set buyer can be offered an online drum course.
    • A gun safe buyer can be offered a paid community membership (a paid group).
    • A high-end wallpaper site can sell listings (entries that show a business to buyers) to local interior designers.
  • A pool table store found local partners who could level the table and glue down the felt (the cloth on the table).
    • The store offered a paid assembly service at checkout (the final payment step).
    • Partners are paid a fixed amount per job.
    • The store keeps the margin.
  • Top rung example (the highest-priced offer in a range):
    • A premium (high-priced) done-for-you service (a service the seller carries out for the client) was priced as a 100,000 USD fee plus 10% of the client's profits.
    • The total was capped (limited to a maximum) at 1,000,000 USD.
    • A top rung can combine a fixed fee with a share of the results it produces.

Memberships and subscriptions

1 min
  • A paid community membership (a regular payment to join a group) at about 30 dollars per month works.
    • It works when customers pay to belong to a community they already engage with.
    • Customers pay for belonging, not for a product box alone.
    • Its value comes from the content and people it connects.
  • A weekly digital subscription (a plan that delivers online files on a schedule) can deliver 5 to 20 new coloring designs each week.
    • The price is about $7 per month.
    • The price was still being tested between $5 and $7.
    • Digital files can be sold worldwide, so the offer works in countries where the physical product cannot be shipped yet.
  • Digital products that give ongoing value to a niche, such as product research tools, newsletters or advice, can be sold as a monthly subscription.
    • Subscriptions suit recurring content (content that keeps coming back) for a specific audience.
  • A membership trial can remove the standard obligation to buy more after the first purchase.
    • State this plainly. Example: choose any 4 books for $1 each with no obligation to buy anything else.
    • Describe it as an experiment to see whether it attracts readers who value the membership itself.
    • This lowers the risk of the first step.

Services: yield and pricing

1 min
  • Hourly yield (the money earned per hour) equals price per unit multiplied by units completed per hour.
  • Example: a service charged at $25 per unit (one piece of work) and finished at 2 units per hour earns $50 per hour.
  • Compare this with market rates (typical prices in the market) of $12 to $15 per unit.

Private label and customization

1 min
  • A low-margin accessory (a small add-on with thin profit) that customers buy repeatedly can stay in the store as a loss leader (a product sold cheaply to bring buyers in).
    • Use it to sell other products to the same buyer.
    • Alternatively, produce it yourself so the margin works.
    • Older example, from 2021-22.
  • Worked margin example (a sample profit calculation; older example from 2021-22):
    • A manufacturer (the company that makes the product) sells it at about $21.
    • It costs them roughly $10, or possibly around $7.
    • If you can source it made for about $7 (have a supplier make it for you) and sell it at $30, each sale earns roughly $10 to $15 more than the dropship version.
  • Customizable products (items the buyer can change) can let customers configure them on the product page and buy directly.
    • Customers accept longer shipping times for custom products.
    • Older example, from 2021.

Amazon and eBay pricing

1 min
  • The ideal Amazon FBA price range (FBA means Fulfillment by Amazon, where Amazon stores and ships your products) is $20 to $50.
  • With recent Amazon changes, about $25 to $60 can work.
  • The price must be high enough to earn profit.
  • It must also be low enough for an impulse buy (a quick purchase without much thought).
  • A price of about $20 or more gives enough margin to outbid (beat rivals by bidding more for ad spots) established competitors with Amazon paid ads.
  • Products priced far above $50, such as $200, make buyers research more.
  • More research lowers impulse conversion (the share of visitors who buy quickly).

Free offers and trials

3 min
  • Use a free consultation as the first step.
    • The prospect (a person who may buy) receives something valuable at no cost.
    • Then the prospect is converted (turned into a paying customer) to a paid service.
    • The formula is to communicate a dream outcome (the best result the buyer wants) with minimal effort and time, which makes the value clear.
    • Example A: a free trial month for a fitness coaching service.
    • Example B: a free photo session for a real-estate listing (a home for sale) before a paid package.
  • Make free offers look valuable by charging a token amount (a small symbolic price) for trial packages.
    • A fully free item is often seen as cheap.
    • A trial product the maker is willing to sell at retail (the normal shop price) signals real quality.
  • Giving a product away free can damage its perceived value (how valuable buyers think it is).
    • It can make the product harder to sell later.
    • People resist paying for something they once got for nothing.
    • Charging the normal price for a trial, even a small one, protects the product's value.
  • A trial package priced at a low fraction of retail (the normal price), such as 15 cents, kept the product's credibility (trust in it) better than a free package.
    • Customers then saw the product as worth buying.
  • Charging a price to request a sample lowers the response rate sharply (the share of people who reply).
    • Asking customers to split the cost also lowers it.
    • It can cost $0.40 to $1.00 more per reply.
    • Keep sample requests free, and state that they are free.
  • Partial-price offers (discounts that ask prospects to pay part of the cost) or buy-one-get-one-type offers that ask prospects to share in the cost of trying the product usually fail in direct response testing.
    • Before a prospect has bought, getting them to accept half-price is nearly as hard as selling full-price.
  • Use the word "free" in ads when the offer is genuinely free.
    • It attracts far more readers and responses.
    • A paid trial offer will generally reduce the number of readers who respond more than it saves.
  • Trial wording (the words "trial offer" and a money-back promise) belongs on cheap front-end offers (the first, low-priced offer) priced around $49.
    • Leave that wording off high-priced back-end offers (the larger offers sold later), roughly $2,000 and up.
    • Why: a buyer could download the full content and then request a refund (money back).
    • Back-end offers usually skip the trial framing.
    • Back-end offers often offer no refund at all.
  • Service pattern (a repeatable sales structure) built on a result guarantee (a promise of a visible result).
    • It includes a pay-as-you-approve trial (you pay only for the work you approve).
    • It includes a bonus unit (one extra free piece of work) for paid work in a set window (a fixed time period).
    1. Guarantee a visible result or charge nothing for the service.
    2. Offer a free consultation.
    3. Let the buyer judge each unit of work as it is finished and pay only for approved work.
    4. Give one free unit for every set number of paid units within a fixed number of days, such as 21 days.
    5. Close with a numbered list of what the buyer does next.
    6. Add a short testimonial (a customer's written praise) that describes a specific bad situation and the fast result.
    • Example 1, window cleaning: We guarantee sparkling windows or the job is free. You approve each floor as we finish it and pay only for the floors you approve, and for every three floors you pay for within 14 days, the fourth is free.
    • Example 2, pool service: We guarantee crystal-clear water after one visit or we return the fee. You inspect the first lane before we treat the rest, and every third visit you book in the next month is on us.

Premiums, personalization and exclusivity

2 min

Premiums are free gifts given with a purchase.

  • Premiums often beat discounts for immediate response (a fast reaction).
    • According to the author's experience, a strong premium can change response by as much as 50 percent.
    • Use a premium when the basic offer alone is not strong enough to make people respond immediately.
  • Case: a casino operator (the person who runs the casino) had sold a package of lodging, drinks, show and gambling to repeat customers.
    • Response stopped coming in by the usual deadline.
    • He sent a test letter offering a free six-day, five-night Hawaiian vacation for two.
    • The vacation was paid for in advance through a bonded travel agency (an agency with a financial guarantee) with over 1,000 packages.
    • The letter went only to past guests who returned to the offer.
    • The offer had a deadline and a limit on the number of vacations.
    • The offer combined a premium with limited availability and a deadline.
  • Test a double premium (two gifts) after a single premium proves successful.
    • Examples: one item for an order of a set size, or two items for a larger order.
    • In one test, the overall response rate (the share of people who responded) stayed about the same.
    • The average order size rose by nearly 30 percent.
  • Attach a free premium with a stated retail value and a stated limited quantity to a main purchase.
    • In one test, a $5,000 equipment buyer was offered a TV and VCR (a video recorder) that would cost $499 to $899 in a store.
    • Only 250 units were available from a special purchase with no reorders (no repeat stock).
    • The retail value acts as an anchor (a reference price that makes the gift look worth more).
    • The limit justifies immediate response.
  • Limit an offer to a clear and specific group (for example members of an organization, veterans or executives).
    • People act to keep an advantage reserved for their group.
    • Narrow group offers often beat general ones.
  • A personalized item (made for one buyer), such as a product with the buyer's name on it, adds perceived value.
    • It can increase purchases.
    • People respond strongly to things that belong to them.
  • Example: adding a buyer's name in gold lettering on each book, with the same ads, sold hundreds of thousands of books.
    • The personalization added value without changing the core offer (the main product).

Direct mail and historical cases

3 min
  • Selling direct by mail (sending offers by post) cuts out middlemen (go-between sellers) and selling expense (the cost of selling).
    • This lets a manufacturer offer near-dealer prices (prices close to what dealers charge).
    • Show a saving such as 25 percent on the same tires.
    • Test a direct offer with a small group of buyers first to confirm demand.
  • Case (historical, older): a publisher had contracted for 100,000 sets of a newly revised four-volume history at a low price.
    • Its earlier one-volume edition had sold more than 150,000 copies.
    • The author cut his royalty (the share paid per book) for the large order.
    • The offer was the four library-size volumes, illustrated with 100 paintings, at 25 percent less than the original two-volume price.
    • It was bundled with a one-year subscription to a monthly review magazine.
    • Buyers sent a reply card (a mail-back form) without money within ten days.
    • They received the set for one week of free examination (a trial period to look it over).
    • They paid 12 dollars in 1 dollar monthly payments or 10.50 dollars cash.
    • The letters reported that 95,000 of the 100,000 sets had sold.
    • Fewer than 5,000 remained.
    • Orders had reached about 500 a day the previous season.
    • So the low price could not be repeated.
    • Buyers who returned the set within the week paid 25 cents for the magazine copy delivered.
    • The seller claimed that scarcely one reader in twenty would part with a set once opened.
  • Case: a publisher ran a series of mailings (letters sent by post) for a 50-volume classic reading set that salesmen had sold earlier.
    • Six mail-order attempts from 1910 to 1913 had failed.
    • One of them spent 25,000 dollars and sold 18 sets.
    • In August 1913 the first mailing of 10,000 illustrated four-page letters went out.
    • It had no enclosure, but a stamped post card (a card the buyer returns, with postage paid).
    • It went to advertising leads (names of people who showed interest) one to two years old.
    • Salesmen had turned these leads down as unsalable (not worth selling to).
    • It cost about 4 cents per piece, including postage.
    • The first letter pulled 4.5 percent orders (the share of people who ordered) on a 39-dollar set.
    • That meant under 1 dollar per order.
    • The first six months showed a 34.5 percent profit.
    • Follow-ups to the same leads produced these results:
Mailing Response Note
First letter 4.5% 39-dollar set, under $1 per order
Second letter 2.5% Enclosed circular (a printed flyer)
Third letter 1.5%
Fourth letter 1% Large folder with only a post card
Final mop-up letter (a last letter to leads who had not answered) 2% Four-page letter, pulled after shorter pieces had stopped working
  • Later efforts beyond that were nearly fruitless, so the remaining leads were filed.
  • The offer was the full set of 50 volumes, with 418 works by about 300 authors and a 76,000-entry index (a list for finding entries quickly).
  • It was sent for a week of free examination.
  • Payment could be as low as 2 dollars a month.
  • The price was 39 dollars, against 472.05 dollars for the same works in separate editions.
  • That is a saving of 433.05 dollars.
  • Later letters itemized retail prices (normal shop prices) volume by volume.
  • They offered a silk cloth edition at one-eighth of the original sets' price.
  • 100 sets were set aside for free examination.
  • They warned that prices would rise because paper, ink and binding had more than doubled in cost.
  • The mailings sold many hundreds of thousands of dollars of books over the next five years.

Sweepstakes, giveaways and draws

2 min

A sweepstakes is a prize draw. A giveaway is a free prize.

  • The time between paid ad exposure (people seeing your paid ad) and purchase is a risk.
    • The ad spend is already committed (the money is already spent).
    • Every day without a purchase lets competitors win the same visitor.
    • Convert the visitor (turn them into a buyer) as fast as possible.
  • Setup of an instant giveaway funnel (the path from ad to sale):
    1. Build an instant giveaway landing page with a tool such as WooRise (an instant giveaway tool) or another instant giveaway tool.
    2. Run Meta campaigns (ad campaigns on Facebook and Instagram) with a purchase conversion objective (an ad goal of getting sales) pointed at that funnel.
    3. Show every entrant an immediate result.
    4. Nurture the leads (people who gave their contact details) by email and convert them over time.
    5. Optimize the landing page continuously toward return on ad spend (the revenue earned for each ad dollar spent).
  • Entrant flow on the giveaway landing page (an entrant is a person who enters the draw):
    1. The visitor sees a headline with the prize and the win odds (the chance to win), such as Win 1 of 10.
    2. A live countdown shows when entries close.
    3. The visitor enters first name, last name and contact details.
    4. The visitor accepts the terms and conditions (the rules).
    5. The page instantly shows whether the entrant has lost or won.
      • A loser sees a discount code or a direct purchase offer.
  • Losers should be told immediately that they lost.
    • They should receive a discount code or purchase offer.
    • If they must wait for a later giveaway result, they hold off buying.
    • Reason: a free product might come later.
  • The example giveaway ad set (one group of ads sharing one audience and budget) reached an average order value of $129.20 from its purchases.
    • Use this as a price anchor (a reference price) when setting the offer or discount that losing entrants are shown.
  • Enter-to-win offers (free draws to win a prize) suit niches with an event, conference or similar draw, such as a trip or ticket.
    • The prize has to be relevant to the audience to make sense.
  • An enter-to-win draw can give each buyer who meets a set spend threshold (a minimum amount spent) during the promotion window (a set time period) one entry.
    • The winner is picked by random draw (chosen by chance).
    • Tie the prize to an event or trip the audience cares about.
    • Run it only when business profit can cover the prize.
  • Sweepstakes and contests create response through entry deadlines (the last day to enter).
    • They are expensive.
    • They can repay the cost through higher response.
    • Use them when the budget supports the prize and the response lift (the extra response).
    • State the entry deadline and the prize in the letter.
  • Purchase-to-enter draw example: a customer who buys a mystery box (a box with hidden contents) within 24 hours is entered to win a prize, an iPhone in the example.
    • It creates scarcity (a sense that the item is in short supply).
    • It appeals to gambling-minded buyers (buyers who like taking a chance).
    • It must match the brand and its audience.
