Playbook › 6. Conversion optimization

Testing methodology and the CRO cycle

Section 6.19 · 19 of 22 in this chapter · 30 min read

In short

  • Do not test when your store has too few visitors. Fix obvious problems first. A "test" means showing two versions to split visitors and seeing which one sells more.
  • Test one change at a time, with a written guess (a hypothesis) made before the test starts.
  • Wait for 1,000 samples per version (one sample = one visitor counted in the test) before you trust a winner.
  • Trust only results that are statistically significant, meaning probably real and not luck. Changes of 1 or 2 percent are too small to trust.
  • A realistic pace is about 8 tests per year, and about 3 of them improve the store.
More key points
  • Change one thing at a time so you can tell what caused a result.
  • Write down your guess and your baseline (the normal result before the change) before you test.
  • Wait for enough visitors before you decide; small numbers give false winners.
  • Pick the winner by the money it earns, not by how many people sign up.

When to test and what to expect

2 min
  • Do not recommend A/B testing (showing two versions to different visitors to see which sells more) by default when the brand likely lacks enough traffic (visitors who come to the store). Label obvious, low-risk changes as a "fix" rather than a "test".
  • In the first audit output, hero positioning (the main message at the top of a page), risk reversal (a promise that removes the buyer's risk, such as free returns) and comparison sections should each be labeled a test only when traffic supports it. Otherwise, label them fixes.
  • Low-traffic stores can run fewer tests at once.
  • Do not run A/B tests with very small traffic. Splitting 20 sales into 10 and 10 gives results that are not statistically reliable (you can trust them, they are not luck).
  • Beginners should skip formal testing at this stage and fix obvious problems first.
  • Results from low-traffic stores have low statistical significance (a measure of how likely a result is real) and should be treated with caution.
  • Large platforms such as Amazon test nearly everything. A small store should test as often as its traffic allows and should not overread small differences.
  • Conversion rate optimization (CRO, making more visitors buy) tests need enough traffic for a statistically reliable sample.
  • With low traffic, such as outside peak season, a clear winner can still appear. But small differences are not trustworthy and should not be acted on.
  • A large change, such as 40 percent, can be acted on even with limited data.
  • A 2 percent change is too small to trust.
  • Low daily spend (money spent on ads each day) produces unreliable A/B results.
  • With low traffic, duplicate the product page and product, then send separate traffic to each.
  • Social proof (signs that other people trust you, such as reviews) and other conversion tactics should be tested once steady traffic arrives, so the page can be measured accurately.
  • Avoid site-wide changes such as theme swaps (changing the store's design template) during the first two to four weeks of running ads.
  • Wait until a product page has a few hundred unique visitors (different people, each counted once) before optimizing it. This lets you tell what caused a change in conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Do not change a checkout that still produces a good return on ad spend (ROAS, money back for each ad dollar).
  • If a test is needed, change one element at a time.
  • Conversion testing is a long-term process that needs time and learning. It is not a single app install.
  • A small gain, such as moving from about 3% to 3.5% conversion, can represent a meaningful amount of money.
  • A realistic yield is about 8 tests per year.
  • Of those, typically around 3 are confirmed as improvements. In the other cases, the current version was already the best option.
  • Best practices such as button color are not universal. The right choice depends on the business, audience and website.
  • Test the option in your own store rather than copying others.
  • Conversion tests on real results can show gains of about 0.5 to 1 percent across more than 50 test variations (a variant is one version in a test).

Research and hypotheses

2 min
  • Good hypotheses come from real signals. Examples are customer feedback about something missing on a page, or a comparison of your unique selling proposition (USP, what makes you different) against competitors.
  • Each hypothesis should name the exact problem it addresses.
  • A hypothesis (a clear guess that you check with the test) should be based on data or customer feedback collected so far.
  • Test a specific hypothesis about one change. For example: "a bigger add-to-cart button will increase sales" (the add-to-cart button is the button that puts an item in the basket).
  • Do not test a broad goal such as "more sales".
  • Use this template to write each hypothesis:
Hypothesis: If I change [specific element on a specific page], then [named metric, such as conversion rate or add-to-cart clicks] will [increase or decrease] because [customer reason based on feedback or data]. Write it down before the test starts so it can be checked against the result afterwards.

  • Each test needs a reason, so do not test changes without a basis.

  • If you already have the lowest price in your market, testing a further price cut has little reason.

  • Testing a price increase that keeps conversion stable can be worthwhile.

  • Research step before a test:

    1. Collect the customer feedback behind the hypothesis.
    2. List the options for fixing the gap.
    3. Pull the current performance of the page for the same time period the test will run.
    4. Break that data down by traffic type (where visitors come from), device type (phone or computer) and demographics (age, gender and similar) in Google Analytics (Google's free tool that counts visits and sales).

  • Session recordings (saved replays of one visit, from tools such as Lucky Orange) show where customers drop off (leave the page) and what changes are needed.

  • Turn every insight from a page review into a conversion test.

  • Change the flagged element, run the test and check whether results improve.

  • Keep the six-factor checklist next to the workspace and run it for each page reviewed.

The CRO cycle

1 min

  • Run the CRO cycle in this order:

    1. Form a hypothesis.
    2. Research it using customer feedback and current performance data.
    3. Document the planned test.
    4. Run the test.
    5. Document the results using the same metrics as the baseline (the starting numbers you compare against).
    6. Analyze the results and write down the insight.
    7. Use the insight to form the next hypothesis and repeat the cycle.

  • Write down ideas for future conversion tests in a tracking system (a list or spreadsheet) before running any test.

  • A tracking system keeps testing organized and shows what is planned, running and finished.

Choosing the test type

2 min
  • A/B test: Change one element and split traffic 50/50 between version A and version B. Then implement the better version.
  • Split test: Compares two fundamentally different designs or page structures, instead of one small detail.
  • Split test for solutions: Compares two distinct solutions, such as two brand or product options, to see which performs better [L423.008].
  • A/B testing (concept): Tests one element with two versions to check one specific hypothesis. Example: whether moving the add-to-cart button higher on the product page increases conversions [L423.007].
  • ABC test: Checks one hypothesis against three potential solutions, with traffic split across all variants. Example: which of three positions for a call-to-action button (the button that asks for the sale) converts best [L390.009, L423.009].
  • Multivariate test: Combines several elements at once. Example: 3 button sizes and 3 copy options give 9 combinations (every mix of the options). It needs a much larger sample size and is the most complicated method [L390.009, L423.010].
  • Note on the multivariate example: the example given shows 9 combinations for 3 options and 2 variants. The stated rule (options multiplied by variants) gives 6 for those numbers. Check the count before using it [L423.010].
  • Simultaneous split (A/B), preferred: Show both versions at the same time to split traffic evenly.
  • Comparing a page before and after a change can give false conclusions, because of differences in weekday mix and traffic sources.
  • Consecutive test: Compares a fixed period before a change with a period after it, such as two four-week blocks.
  • A consecutive test is simpler to run. But it is more exposed to other changes, so it is weaker than a split test. It is still better than no testing.
  • Consecutive procedure:
    1. Pick one element to test, such as a bonus offer or the button text.
    2. Run version A for one week with enough traffic.
    3. Run version B the following week and compare the results. [L356.042]
  • Use this procedure only when enough visitors come in, because time-based testing is less reliable [L356.042].
  • Page-builder split test: A page builder is a tool for building pages without coding. This test changes the content on the same URL and shows each variation to about 50 percent of visitors, instead of splitting URLs [L356.043].
  • Typical test items are offers, bonus items and button text [L356.043].
  • Setting up a simultaneous A/B test:
    1. Create two identical versions of the page.
    2. Make only the planned change in one version.
    3. Use a tool that alternates visitors evenly between the two versions.
    4. Collect and compare the results for both versions at the end.
  • Use the tool to test a new Shopify theme (Shopify is an online store platform; a theme is its design template) against the current one before all traffic switches over. This shows whether the new theme converts better or worse first.

Keeping variables isolated

2 min
  • Test one single change at a time on a page. Then any difference in results can be traced to that change.
  • Testing several changes together makes results confusing.
  • Test one variable at a time. Example: test an offer by keeping the same creative (ad image or clip) and audience, then compare the conversion rate to the existing one.
  • When the target audience and angle are known, test one page element at a time [L387.014].
  • Example order: test the headline first for about five days, then the product pictures, then the brag bar (a strip of trust signs such as awards or sales numbers). Changing several elements at once makes results hard to attribute [L387.014].
  • Test several different marketing angles (the reason or message you lead with) together only when the audiences and angles are truly different [L387.013, L387.014].
  • Example: gaming, home decor and a therapeutic angle for the same product, to show which audience converts best without separate ads [L387.013, L387.014].
  • Group small changes into one concept when they belong to the same part of a page, such as the area around the add-to-cart button.
  • Testing every combination of small elements one by one can take six months or more, which is too slow.
  • Example: If a product page has four elements around the add-to-cart button that may distract buyers, remove them together as one concept test.
  • If the concept wins, refine it later rather than testing each element alone.
  • Never run two concept tests on the same page at the same time. Example: a change to the add-to-cart area and a change to the product description.
  • Concepts can run in parallel on different pages.
  • Stores with many product pages can run many tests at once, one per page.
  • Stores selling one or two products have fewer pages to test in parallel, so testing is slower.
  • Verify that each variant is delivered correctly before reading the data. Split testing is technically demanding, and setup errors can easily invalidate results [L423.013].
  • Changing the price of the live store in Shopify is less reliable than split testing. It compares two periods, not two groups at the same time [L999.067].
  • Use split testing for prices [L999.067].

Setting up split tests and tools

2 min

  • Google Optimize (a Google testing tool, now shut down) turned one landing page URL into a single link. A landing page is the page ads send people to. The link distributed visitors across several landing page variants according to set weights. Google reported how each variant performed [L387.010].

  • Weights (the share of visitors each version gets): with three variants, use 33 percent each. With two variants, use 50 percent each [L387.011].

  • Any uneven split can be set to favor one variant [L387.011].

  • Google Optimize setup procedure (historic; the method still works with another tool):

    1. Create the page variants, one per angle or version you want to test.
    2. Open Google Optimize and set up a split test (A/B test).
    3. Add the variants and set the traffic weights (e.g., 33/33/33 for three variants, 50/50 for two).
    4. Copy the generated link and use it in ads, bio or organic posts (organic = free posts, not paid ads).
    5. Read the performance data per variant to see which one converts best. [L387.012]

  • Google Optimize was shut down in September 2023, so it can no longer be used for weighted landing page split tests [L387.032].

  • The method still works with another split-testing tool. One link distributes visitors across several page variants by set weights, and results are compared per variant [L387.032].

  • Current tool: since Google Optimize was discontinued in 2023, use a current A/B testing tool for this procedure:

    1. After optimizing the landing page, collect another 200 to 300 visitors and measure the funnel (the path from visit to purchase, step by step) again.
    2. For a split test, send traffic 50/50 to two page versions, or 33/33/33 to three versions.
    3. Use a current A/B testing tool for this.

  • Historic procedure: Google Optimize (a historic tool) can divert traffic 50/50 or 33/33/33 across three landing page variants. Check results after another 200 to 300 visitors.

  • The confidence score (how sure the tool is) rises as the sample grows [L570.002].
  • Declaring a winner after only 1,000 samples per variant risks choosing a version that loses once more data is collected [L570.012].

  • Low-traffic tests must run longer to collect enough conversions.
  • Judge a test by whether the result is statistically significant, not by a rough impression.
  • If there is no significant increase, the element may not have been a real problem. Keep the current version or test a different option.
  • A/B tests need a large amount of traffic to reach statistical significance [L524.043].

  • Do not keep a test running indefinitely without signs of a result.

Significance and what counts as a win

  • A test wins if it shows a positive improvement toward the stated hypothesis.
  • A test with no change or a result going backwards counts as failed or inconclusive (no clear answer).
  • Use both checks: a winning test shows a positive improvement toward the hypothesis, and the result must be statistically significant to be trusted.
  • A test that shows no gain or a loss still has value. It shows the hypothesis was wrong or incomplete.
  • A small change of about 1 to 2 percent is too small to trust [L999.022].

Traffic changes during a test

  • During a CRO test, avoid big changes to traffic. Example: launching several new Google Shopping campaigns (Google's product ads in search) that send new traffic to the tested pages.
  • Traffic changes make results useless, because the conversion change can no longer be credited to the page change.
  • The source framework cites traffic targeting as a large share of conversion outcomes, roughly 40%.
  • Natural shifts can be accepted without stopping the test. Examples: growing organic search traffic (free visits from Google search) and small pay-per-click adjustments (paid ads, where you pay per click).

Testing ads, offers and landing pages

2 min

Ad creatives

  • Test at least 3 different ad creatives for each product. Do not test fewer than 3.
  • Allocate a minimum of $5 per day per creative.
  • The ideal budget is $5 to $10 per day per creative, so $15 to $30 daily for 3 creatives.
  • Judge the landing page by its add-to-cart rate. Judge creatives by CTR (click-through rate: the share of people who see an ad and click it) and CPC (cost per click). Each asset is optimized on the metric that matches it [L871.028].
  • Testing optimization priority:
    1. Find the creative with a high CTR: 1%+ on Facebook, or a high engagement rate on TikTok. This proves the ad is getting attention.
    2. Once the creative is locked, test offer variations (discount level, bundle structure, bundle price) to find the highest converter. A bundle is several products sold as one pack. [L307.028]
  • Do not optimize both at the same time. Isolate variables, meaning change only one thing at a time [L307.028].

Offers

  • If testing different offers, maintain consistency first [L308.030].
  • Do not increase price mid-campaign because sales slowed. Lower the price back to the consistency baseline (the starting point you compare against) [L308.030].
  • Test one variable at a time [L308.030].
  • Stop offers that do not work. Keep and duplicate what works [L308.030].
  • Test campaign-level variants:
    1. Run a normal conversion campaign for about three days.
    2. Send one group of traffic to a collection page (a page listing several products) where each variant has its own listing.
    3. Send the other group of traffic to a single listing that holds all variants.
    4. Compare the results and keep the setup that performs better.
    5. Use a total test budget of about 20-50 USD.

Landing pages

  • Build two landing pages for different buyer intents, such as a pre-selected starter kit and a full build-out.
  • Send the campaigns to both pages. After about 10 days, see which page converts.
  • Lean into the winning angle before testing gender-based angles.
  • Test the five first-view elements (the part of the page visible before scrolling) [L916.023]:
    1. Create a version of the page with three features or elements in the first view.
    2. Create a second version with five features or elements in the first view.
    3. Run both versions in an A/B test and measure the conversion rate of each version.
    4. Keep the version with the higher conversion rate. [L916.023]
  • Test a GIF (a short animated image) against a static image on the product page.
  • The GIF may not convert better, so test it instead of assuming it works.

Test ideas list

1 min

A test shows two versions of something to real visitors and checks which one works better.

Standing list

1 min
  • Build a standing list of test ideas: a running list you keep and update.
  • The list covers offers, page structure and product page content.
  • Add ideas to it as they come up.
  • Start tracking tests from the beginning, not later.

Product page

2 min
  • A product page is the page that sells one item in your shop.
  • Split test (also called A/B test): two versions are shown to different visitors at the same time, and you compare results.
  • Run split tests on different headlines (the big title text) for the same product.
  • Compare time on page (how long a visitor stays) and conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Other copy elements can be tested the same way.
  • Headlines should not be written once and never revisited.
  • An advertorial is an ad that looks like an article and leads to a sales page.
  • A headline test can show which headline grabs the most attention, for example a "10 reasons why" headline on a list-style advertorial followed by a sales page.
  • Testable page elements include: product descriptions, product titles, product images, opt-in forms (sign-up boxes), product prices where pricing can be changed, collection page content (pages that list a group of products), blog page layout, homepage layout, and entire theme versions (the shop's design template).
  • The three elements with the largest effect on conversion are images, headlines and colors. Test these before smaller changes [L570.008].
  • Customer reviews placed directly above the add-to-cart button (the button that puts an item in the basket) are a testable concept.
  • A hypothesis is a guess you want to check.
  • Your hypothesis: this raises add-to-cart clicks and conversion rate, because shoppers see social proof (other customers' trust signals) at the moment they decide to buy.
  • A USP (unique selling point) is the main reason to buy from you.
  • A product page USP test can run for one month.
  • Record the baseline beforehand.
  • Run no other test on that page during that month.
  • If the new USP shows no significant gain (no gain that is unlikely to be luck), or converts worse than the original USP on the same page, the original is judged stronger.
  • Still record the result as an insight.
  • Testing a button higher on the page is an example of A/B testing one element [L423.007].
  • Button color is not universal; test it in your own store [L390.007].
  • Measure the conversion rate after each layout change to see whether the new layout works.
  • Test several ebook cover designs (an ebook is a digital book sold online) for the same product.
  • See which cover converts best, instead of committing to the first cover.
  • Change only the cover between tests [L513.042].
  • Trust elements such as star ratings (customer score stars): test one position at a time.
  • Positions include lower on the page, directly under the product name, or next to the price.
  • Keep the placement that converts best, because differences from a single element can be measurable.
  • Test social proof and page improvements once steady traffic arrives (traffic means visitors).

Homepage and first view

1 min
  • The homepage is the front page of your shop.
  • The hero is the big top section of a page.
  • Testing ideas for the first audit output (the first review report): hero positioning, risk reversal (a promise that takes the risk away, such as a refund guarantee), and comparison modules (blocks that compare options).
  • Label each idea as a test only when traffic supports it. Otherwise label it as a fix [L886.021].
  • Five first-view elements: see the procedure in the section on ads, offers and landing pages [L916.023].

Offers, pricing and pop-ups

1 min
  • A discount of 5% is only a starting point.
  • A coupon is a discount code.
  • Edit the expiring coupon section over time and compare results, since 5% is not a proven best value.
  • An opt-in pop-up is a box that asks for a sign-up.
  • Compare a pop-up offering 15% off the first order with one offering a free set of examples.
  • The winning version is the one that earns more money, which can differ from the one with more sign-ups [L572.011].
  • Testing a price increase that keeps conversion stable can be worthwhile.
  • Testing a further price cut has little reason if you already have the lowest price in your market.
  • A pre-purchase return option is an extra choice at checkout (the payment step) that lets the shopper pay a fee for an easy return. Procedure [L955.054]:
    1. Split incoming shoppers 50/50 and show the pre-purchase return option to only one group.
    2. Keep the other group on a normal cart and checkout with no return option.
    3. Run the test for about two weeks, recording checkout conversion, cart abandonment (shoppers who leave before paying) and AOV (average order value: average money per order) for both groups.
    4. If the option group does not lose conversion and AOV rises, test two fee levels such as $2.99 and $3.99 and compare uptake (how many shoppers pick the option).
    5. Roll out the fee that gives the best uptake without a drop in conversion. [L955.054]
  • Postage-paid reply cards or envelopes (return postage already paid by you): test with and without them before you commit.
  • The author found they rarely improve response enough to justify the cost.

Cart and checkout

1 min
  • Do not change a checkout that still produces a good return on ad spend (ROAS: money earned for each dollar spent on ads) [L963.058].
  • An exit-intent offer is a pop-up shown when a visitor is about to leave.
  • Example of a candidate test to log as an idea: replace an exit-intent offer of a percent-off discount code with a short product-finder quiz (a few questions that suggest a product type).
  • The quiz routes visitors to the product type that suits them.
  • Writing the idea into the changelog (a written log of changes) before testing means it can be found again later.
  • This way it is not forgotten or retested blindly [L410.013].

Price testing

1 min
  • Use split testing for prices, not live price changes in Shopify (an online store software) [L999.067].

Analysis and deciding the winner

1 min

  • Analysis after a test:

    1. Record the outcomes for the test period using the same metrics as the original baseline.
    2. Ask what changed and what it means.
    3. Ask what the result implies for the rest of the business and for future tests.
    4. Decide the next step: a new test, a refined version of the winner, or a move to another page element.

  • Customer feedback that appears during the test is part of the result.

  • It can form a new hypothesis, such as the idea being right but the execution (the way it was carried out) being wrong.

  • Wait for more data where possible.

  • The confidence score (how sure the result is) rises as the sample grows. A sample is the number of visitors in a test [L570.002].

  • For sales-based tests, the winner is the variant that earns more money, which can differ from the one with more sign-ups [L572.011].

  • A variant is one version in a test.

  • Expected outcome: changing landing page elements based on testing (a landing page is the page a visitor arrives on from an ad) produced conversion differences of about 0.5 to 1 percent across more than 50 test variations.

Documentation, changelogs and naming

1 min

  • Making unrecorded site changes on impulse makes it impossible to tell later whether a change helped, hurt or did nothing.

  • Plan, record and measure every change. Record it before it goes live.

  • Each change should be planned, recorded before it goes live and measured.

  • Document each test before it starts, covering:

    • what is being tested,
    • The details of the change,
    • how the test will be implemented,
    • The baseline performance,
    • The planned start and end dates,
    • The duration.

  • Keep the record in the same place as the results.

  • Store every test record for as long as the business runs, not only until the test ends.

  • Old records prevent repeating a failed test.

  • They can later inspire a modified test with new insight.

  • A changelog is necessary early on.

  • Change tests every one to two weeks is a typical rhythm for active store owners.

  • Log each change with its date and platform (where the change is made), so that many simultaneous tweaks can be separated later [L410.012].

  • Name every Visually test (a test made with the Visually tool) with its device, scope, element and change.

  • Scope means which part of the site it covers.

  • Examples: "mobile, site-wide, promo bar, text color" or "mobile BDP social proof" (BDP means product detail page).

  • Clear names make the test report readable when several experiments run at the same time [L389.026].

Ongoing cadence

1 min
  • After a win is achieved and deployed (put live), keep testing.
  • Continue testing different recommendation ranking logic (the order in which suggested products are shown), design variations and customer segments (groups of shoppers).
  • This finds further improvements beyond the initial test [L197.024].
  • Test conversion changes one at a time.
  • Expect a result over a fixed period, such as two weeks.
  • Run each change long enough to measure a clear result before adding the next change, so the cause of any sales movement is identifiable [L410.011].
  • Change tests every one to two weeks is a typical rhythm for active store owners [L410.012].
  • Plan testing as a long-term effort.
  • A realistic pace is about 1.5 months per test, which allows roughly 8 hypotheses over one year [L423.014].
  • Of about 8 hypotheses tested per year, typically around 3 are confirmed as improvements [L390.011].
  • Stores with many product pages can run many tests at once, one per page.
  • Never run two concept tests (tests of a big idea) on the same page at the same time.
  • Do not change traffic sources during a test (traffic sources are where visitors come from).
  • Accept natural shifts only.
  • Do not run A/B tests with very small traffic.
  • Focus on fixing obvious problems first [L390.012].
  • Keep a standing list of test ideas and add to it as they come up.

Further details

6 min
  • A/B testing should be iterative and ongoing.
  • After a win is achieved and deployed, continue testing different recommendation ranking logic, design variations, and customer segments to identify further improvements beyond the initial test.
  • Do not turn off ad campaigns (groups of paid ads) after 1 or 2 days unless performance is extremely poor.
  • Analyze performance around day 3, not before, because early data is incomplete.
  • Testing optimization priority:
    • (1) Find creative (the ad image or video) with high CTR (click-through rate: the share of people who click), 1%+ on Facebook, or a high engagement rate (share who like, comment or share) on TikTok. This proves the ad gets attention.
    • (2) Once creative is locked, test offer variations (discount level, bundle structure, bundle price) to find the highest converter. A bundle is several products sold together.
  • Do not optimize both at once. Isolate variables (change one thing at a time).
  • If testing different offers, maintain consistency first.
  • Do not increase price mid-campaign because sales slowed. Lower the price back to the consistency baseline.
  • Test one variable at a time.
  • Kill offers that don't work (stop them). Keep and duplicate what works.
  • Procedure:
    1. Pick one element to test, such as a bonus offer or the button text.
    2. Run version A for one week with enough traffic.
    3. Run version B the following week and compare the results.
    • Use this only when enough visitors come in, because time-based testing is less reliable.
  • Page-builder based split testing (testing with the editor tool that builds your pages) changes the content on the same URL (web address) and shows each variation to about 50 percent of visitors.
  • It does this instead of splitting URLs.
  • Typical test items are offers, bonus items and button text.
  • In Google Optimize (a Google testing tool), the weights of the variants determine the share of traffic each receives.
  • With three variants, use 33 percent each. With two variants, use 50 percent each.
  • Any uneven split can be set to favor one variant.

Set up a split test in Google Optimize:

  1. Create the page variants, one per angle or version you want to test.
  2. Open Google Optimize and set up a split test (A/B test).
  3. Add the variants and set the traffic weights (e.g., 33/33/33 for three variants, 50/50 for two).
  4. Copy the generated link and use it in ads, bio (your profile description), or organic posts (unpaid posts).
  5. Read the performance data per variant to see which one converts best.
  • Testing completely different marketing angles (the main selling story) in one split test, for example gaming, home decor, and a therapeutic angle for the same product, shows which audience converts best.
  • You do not need separate ads for each angle.
  • When the target audience and angle are already known, test one element of the landing page at a time.
  • Example order: the headline first for about five days, then the product pictures, then the brag bar (a strip of short claims at the top of the page).
  • Changing several elements at once makes results hard to attribute (hard to link to a cause).
  • Only test multiple different angles together when the audiences and angles are truly different.
  • ABC testing adds a third or further variant of the same element, for example three possible positions for the add-to-cart button, with traffic split across all variants.
  • Multivariate testing combines several elements, such as 3 button sizes and 3 copy options, which means 9 combinations and a much larger sample size.
  • Test conversion changes one at a time and expect a result over a fixed period, such as two weeks.
  • Run changes long enough to measure a clear result before adding the next change, so the cause of any sales movement is identifiable.
  • Change tests every one to two weeks is a typical rhythm for active store owners, so a changelog becomes necessary early on.
  • Log each change with its date and platform so that the many simultaneous tweaks can be separated later.
  • A candidate test worth logging as an idea: replace an exit-intent offer of a percent-off discount code with a short product-finder quiz that routes visitors to the product type that suits them.
  • Writing the idea into the changelog before testing means it can be found again later, so it is not forgotten or retested blindly.
  • A/B testing compares two versions of one element to check one specific hypothesis.
  • Example: you could test whether moving the add-to-cart button higher on the product page increases conversions.
  • Split testing compares two distinct solutions against each other to see which performs better.
  • Example: two different brand or product options can be shown to visitors to find the stronger one.
  • A/B/C testing checks one hypothesis against three potential solutions.
  • Example: it can show which of three positions for a call-to-action button (CTA, the button that asks for the action) converts best.
  • Multivariate testing examines a complex hypothesis that combines several variables. It is the most complicated method.
  • The number of combinations equals options multiplied by variants: 3 options for 2 variants gives 9 combinations, and 3 size options with 3 copy options gives 9 combinations for one call-to-action.
  • Each version in a test needs a sufficient sample. The benchmark is about 10 samples per version.
  • Results from smaller samples are unreliable.
  • Running split tests is technically demanding, so setup errors can easily invalidate results.
  • Verify that each variant is delivered correctly before reading the data.
  • Test several ebook cover designs for the same product to see which one converts best, instead of committing to the first cover.
  • Change only the cover between tests so the result shows which design performs.
  • For an A/B test, aim for at least 1,000 samples per variant before treating the result as meaningful.
  • In a 50/50 split, this means 2,000 total recipients or visitors in the funnel (the path from ad to sale), not 500 per variant.
  • A result at 1,000 samples per variant can reverse once more data arrives, because small samples produce unstable winners.
  • The confidence score rises as the sample grows, so wait for more data where possible.
  • The three elements with the largest effect on conversion to test are images, headlines and colors.
  • Focus tests on these before smaller changes.
  • Declaring a winner after only 1,000 samples per variant risks choosing a version that loses once more data is collected.
  • Wait for a larger sample before acting on a result.
  • Split testing compares two versions of one element, such as an opt-in pop-up offering 15% off the first order versus one offering a free set of examples.
  • The winning version is the one that earns more money, which can differ from the one with more sign-ups.
  • Judge the landing page by its add-to-cart rate and judge creatives by CTR and CPC (cost per click: what each click costs).
  • Each asset is optimized on the metric that matches it.
  • Use a sample of about 200 landing page visitors before judging a change.

Test the five first-view elements:

  1. Create a version of the page with three features or elements in the first view.
  2. Create a second version with five features or elements in the first view.
  3. Run both versions in an A/B test and measure the conversion rate of each version.
  4. Keep the version with the higher conversion rate.

  • Procedure for the pre-purchase return option:

    1. Split incoming shoppers 50/50 and show the pre-purchase return option to only one group.
    2. Keep the other group on a normal cart and checkout with no return option.
    3. Run the test for about two weeks, recording checkout conversion, cart abandonment and AOV for both groups.
    4. If the option group does not lose conversion and AOV rises, test two fee levels such as $2.99 and $3.99 and compare uptake.
    5. Roll out the fee that gives the best uptake without a drop in conversion.

  • Price testing in Shopify by changing the price of the live store is less reliable than split testing.

  • It compares two periods, not two groups at the same time.

  • Use split testing for prices.
