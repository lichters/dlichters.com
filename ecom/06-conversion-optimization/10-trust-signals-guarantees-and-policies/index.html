Playbook › 6. Conversion optimization

Trust signals, guarantees and policies

Section 6.10 · 10 of 22 in this chapter · 34 min read

In short

  • Trust decides most sales. Shoppers who do not trust a store leave.
  • Show real reviews and customer counts. Never invent them.
  • Offer a clear money-back guarantee with a set time, such as 30 days.
  • Say clearly where you ship, how payment is safe, how to reach you and how returns work.
  • Customers buy more when they know the rules for delivery, damage and refunds before they pay.
More key points
  • Show only what is true: real reviews, real ratings, real partners and real payment methods.
  • Show trust badges (small logos that signal safety) and trust seals only when they help. Check sales before and after adding one.
  • Keep your social media pages active, with at least one post a week.
  • Show your phone number on every page so visitors can check that you are a real business.

Trust as the core of conversion

5 min

  • Trust is the strongest driver of a sale. "Conversion" means a visitor becomes a buyer.

  • Most abandonment comes from perceived distrust. Abandonment means a shopper leaves without buying. Perceived distrust means the shopper feels the store cannot be trusted.

  • Typical causes of distrust:

    • Unclear shipping times.
    • Missing security signals, such as no sign that payment is safe.
    • Unclear return rules.
    • A lack of customer reviews.

  • Modern customers recognise untrustworthy websites.

  • They compare prices across competitors.

  • They hesitate to share their email address.

  • Trust and retargeting are both needed to turn browsers into buyers. Retargeting means showing ads again to people who already visited. Browsers are visitors who look but do not buy.

  • When a shopper has never heard of the store, trust is the main thing being sold.

  • Every visible signal (a sign the shopper can see) before checkout adds or removes trust. Checkout is the final payment step.

  • Trust earned on the first purchase also decides whether the buyer returns.

  • Professionalism, not quick tricks, builds trust.

  • Professionalism comes from strong design, clear copy (the written words on the page) and excellent customer service.

  • An online business must look as professional as an offline one to be trusted.

  • Design and professionalism are underrated.

  • A store with a clearly better look can win traffic from competitors with poor design. Traffic means visitors.

  • Professionalism covers details such as grammar and a visible phone number. Design covers visual appearance.

  • Visitors lose trust when the store looks messy. Examples: errors, bugs (faults that break things), useless plugins (small add-on tools) or unwanted pop-ups (windows that jump up over the page).

  • Trust also fails when branding is weak. Branding means the look and name that make a store recognisable.

  • Trust also fails when design is inconsistent, meaning it looks different from page to page.

  • Trust also fails when no customers or social proof are visible. Social proof means evidence that others already buy.

  • Trust also fails when no staff or communication channels (ways to contact the store) are shown.

  • A store that does not look trustworthy can lose buyers at checkout, even when add-to-carts are fine. An add-to-cart is a click on the button that puts a product in the basket.

  • When people add to cart but do not buy, review the store's appearance and trust signals.

  • In dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships straight to the buyer, so the store holds no stock), many visitors have not heard of the brand.

  • Trust must therefore be built at once, using:

    • social proof (others already buying, reviews)
    • authority (press mentions, expert or doctor endorsements, notable customers)

    An endorsement is a public approval. Authority means a respected name vouches for the store.

  • Risk matters most when the buyer does not know the seller.

  • A new or unknown shop needs risk-reducing elements earlier and more prominently than an established brand. Risk-reducing elements are things that make buying feel safer.

  • Place trust signals near the headline (the big title at the top) and the buy button, not only at the bottom of the page.

  • Shoppers who doubt the seller will not reach the detailed sections.

  • Trust signals (visible signs that make a store feel safe) fall into three types:

    • Authority: experts, vets (animal doctors), celebrities, respected magazines.
    • Social proof: large customer counts, ratings, reviews, testimonials (written praise from customers) showing that many others already buy.
    • Risk-lowering statements.

    Authority and social proof should appear at the top of the page, before product detail.

  • Product-page trust elements (elements on the page for one item) that lift conversion (raise the sales rate) include:

    • active social media accounts
    • customer reviews
    • social proof badges (small labels showing popularity)
    • bonus offers on high-traffic products (products many visitors see)
    • competitor comparison ("do better than what they do")
    • financing options (paying in instalments)
    • live chat
    • Lucky Orange session recording (a tool that records how visitors use the page)

  • Trust signals such as authorised retailer badges do not produce an immediate jump in sales. A badge is a small logo or label.

  • Badges raise trust and conversions gradually. Expect a steady gain rather than a dramatic one.

  • Repeat trust elements in the cart (the basket of chosen items).

  • Shipping, a return policy, a warranty (a promise to repair or replace a faulty product) and an active promotion help the shopper decide at checkout.

  • Repeating them there is a cheap test, meaning a low-cost way to check what works.

  • Trust elements to place on the page:

    • International delivery information.
    • Secure payment information and secure checkout statements.
    • Payment and security badges.
    • Customer service availability.
    • Easy return terms.

    Each one reduces a specific perceived risk, meaning the fear a shopper feels that something could go wrong.

  • Shoppers reduce perceived risk when a page openly states:

    • international delivery
    • secure payment
    • responsive customer service (answers come quickly)
    • easy returns

  • If these points are missing, buyers fear unanswered problems, damaged goods or being unable to return an item.

  • This lowers purchase likelihood, meaning how likely a shopper is to buy.

  • Use a trust banner (a strip across the page) with the usual dropshipping trust signals:

    • international delivery
    • secure payment
    • fast customer service
    • easy returns

  • Loss aversion means people fear losing more than they enjoy gaining. Here is how each trust element lowers a shopper's fear:

Trust element Risk it lowers for the shopper
International delivery Doubt that the store ships to their country
Secure payment Fear that payment details are not safe
Fast customer service Fear of getting no reply when a problem occurs
Easy returns Fear of keeping an unwanted or damaged product
30 day money back guarantee Loss of money if the product disappoints (loss aversion)
  • Before a shopper buys, they often check whether the store is a real, active business.
  • A store that cannot be found looks suspicious.
  • Keep the store's basic footprint (its visible traces, such as contact details and address) visible and consistent.

Reviews and social proof

1 min

  • Social proof works as a mental shortcut.

  • Shoppers feel safer when they see many other people who are happy with the product.

  • Show happy customers high on the page.

  • Customer testimonials and review counts act as social proof.

  • A high star rating, shown together with a large number of happy customers, builds trust.

  • This works even if the testimonials are not verified (checked as coming from real buyers).

  • Social proof such as reviews, real customer photos and user-generated content (content made by customers, not the store) should be shown.

  • Even in dropshipping, use a professional product photo shoot and detailed descriptions.

  • This lets visitors judge the product as they would in person.

  • Customer photos and reviews work as social proof on the product page and increase conversion.

  • Collect them deliberately, rather than leaving them to chance.

  • Before-and-after photos from real customers are a separate form of proof from social proof.

  • They show how the product works in the customer's favour.

  • Use them alongside testimonials and customer counts.

  • Show store reviews on product pages.

  • Buyers spend more when they trust that the store will deliver the product.

  • Repeat trust on the page: a star rating in the hero section (the big top area of the page).

  • Expand it with more testimonials and a count of five-star reviews further down.

  • This gives the social proof more weight.

  • Social proof includes reviews, star ratings, verified testimonials and third-party posts (posts by other people, not the store).

  • Authority comes from:

    • press mentions
    • research-backed ingredients
    • expert or doctor endorsements
    • recognised review platforms such as Trustpilot (a public review website)

  • Combine several proof types in the same product copy.

  • Examples: customer reviews with video demonstrations, or reviews with third-party testing results.

  • Do not rely on one proof type alone.

Where to place reviews and testimonials

2 min

  • Show a selection of reviews near the top of the homepage (the front page of the store), not hidden at the bottom.

  • Add a clear link to read more.

  • Social proof must be seen before the visitor scrolls far.

  • Show reviews from existing customers on the product page as soon as they exist.

  • Present them in an easy-to-read way.

  • A brand-new product has no reviews yet, so this element takes time to build up.

  • Place a quoted customer testimonial near the top of the landing page (a single page built to sell one offer).

  • A photo of a happy customer with the product strengthens it but is optional.

  • A short quote alone still works.

  • A landing page must build trust fast, with a testimonial and a clear guarantee.

  • This must happen before the visitor reads the full sales copy.

  • Two things drive a first-time purchase: the offer and trust.

  • A short quoted testimonial of one or two lines works well.

  • Add a small image and a five-star rating.

  • Place it in the first scroll (the part of the page seen before scrolling).

  • It builds trust from the first visit.

  • Social proof sections such as Facebook testimonials, Instagram stories and a Results section should be placed on the page.

  • Show real customer feedback in a format familiar to customers.

  • This builds trust before purchase.

  • A Results section can present social proof about the product.

  • It supports conversion when placed on the page.

  • Trustpilot widgets (small rating boxes from the Trustpilot site) can be placed on cart and checkout pages.

  • They show your rating as social proof.

  • The example rating was 4.6 out of 5, rated 'Excellent'.

  • Social proof placed next to the product and bundle choices (packs of several items) supports quick buying decisions.

  • Example: a fall sock product page showed:

    • a 4.8-star rating with over 7,000 reviews
    • a 'Fall & Winter Must Have' positioning (how the product is presented to buyers)
    • a visible 'Best Seller' label on the bundle

  • Typical trust elements on a sales page (the page that sells the product):

    • a Trustpilot rating
    • a customer count such as 150,000+ satisfied customers
    • a 'featured in' row of recognised media brands
    • trust badges
    • a line such as 'trusted by thousands' with testimonials beneath it

  • Recent-order notices are social proof.

  • A recent-order notice is a live message that names a recent buyer, their state and how long ago they ordered.

  • Record them in the proof points column (the column where you list each proof point), together with how and where they are displayed.

Counts, ratings and what the numbers mean

1 min
  • Use a specific, growing review count in the copy, such as '3,500+ five-star reviews'.
  • Do not use a vague phrase like 'many happy customers'.
  • Large precise numbers signal active, ongoing social proof.
  • Place the count near the hero section or near the add-to-cart button.
  • Once a store has a large review count, the exact live total adds little.
  • A rounded figure such as '10,000+' is enough.
  • Note the difference between the two count styles:
    • Rounded totals ('10,000+') suit a large, settled count.
    • A precise, growing count ('3,500+ five-star reviews') signals that the social proof is still active.
  • Tell visitors in plain words what the review count means, for example 'people love us'.
  • Do not leave them to interpret a number.
  • Repeat a key message in a different format elsewhere on the page.
  • Never repeat the same phrase in the same spot.
  • A quantitative example (an example with numbers): a rating shown as 4.8 out of 5 stars from 1,000 happy customers.
  • Both the average score and the number of reviewers help shoppers trust the product.
  • Specific numbers build more trust than vague praise.
  • A rating of 4.8 out of 5 from a thousand happy customers is a strong social proof signal.
  • Customer totals such as 'over 40,000 customers' or '45,000 happy customers' must come from your own order records.
  • Do not take them from a competitor's page.
  • Update the number whenever your count changes, so the claim stays accurate.
  • A customer-count formula: 'Over [large number] [customers] and counting', placed near the top of the page next to ratings.
  • It works because a big, growing number shows that many people already buy and trust the brand.
  • This lowers the perceived risk for a stranger.
  • Example 1 for a kitchen gadget: 'Over 250,000 home cooks and counting.'
  • Example 2 for a skincare brand: 'Trusted by over 80,000 customers and counting.'

  • Social proof pattern for a large number and a rating: one line with the number and rating, followed by a short plain-language sentence that says what it means.

Collecting and checking reviews

1 min
  • Use reviews from real buyers only, even if the store is new.
  • Writing product reviews yourself and presenting them as customer reviews misleads buyers.
  • It can also violate consumer protection rules on fake reviews (laws that protect shoppers).
  • Reviews that mention customer service quality are the most valuable ones to feature on the store.
  • Excellent customer service ranked as the most important factor for consumer trust in a company in a Zendesk survey (a study by Zendesk, a customer service software company).
  • A Zendesk survey reported that 88% of customers trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations from friends or family.
  • Treat this as a vendor-sourced benchmark (a reference number from a software seller, not an independent check), not as a verified figure.
  • Testimonials and FAQ answers should come from real customers and real questions.
  • Do not invent copy.
  • FAQ items (frequently asked questions) usually capture objections (reasons a buyer hesitates).
  • They are answered with factual reassurance rather than persuasive copy.
  • Showing a store with few social media followers can still build trust.
  • Visitors expect an active account.
  • A page-likes campaign (a push to get more followers) can help.
  • It does not replace real customer reviews. (Recorded 2024.)

Why guarantees work

1 min
  • Risk reversal is everything a seller does to make sure the customer is not worse off after buying.
  • Examples: a returns policy or a price match guarantee.
  • Record every such element, since a returns policy is often the main form.
  • Money-back guarantees and hassle-free returns reduce the perceived risk of buying.
  • They build trust.
  • Mention them near the call to action (the button or line that asks for the sale).
  • Anything that lowers risk can increase conversion.
  • A money-back guarantee with a clearly stated period, such as 30 days, reverses the risk of purchase.
  • The buyer knows they can return the product and get their money back.
  • This reduces fear of loss and increases the likelihood of buying.
  • Risk reversal works partly because of loss aversion: people weigh a possible loss more heavily than an equal gain.
  • A guarantee that removes the downside of buying addresses that fear.
  • State the guarantee period clearly, such as 30 days, so the buyer can see exactly what they would lose or regain.
  • Shoppers fear losing money.
  • The page should reduce the perceived risk of buying with visible guarantees and protection, such as a money-back promise.
  • This makes the visitor feel safe to purchase.
  • Trust signals such as free shipping, a 30-day money-back guarantee and 'try risk-free for 30 days' near the top of the page remove purchase anxiety.
  • The wording should make clear the company will refund an unhappy customer.
  • This gives certainty rather than persuasion.
  • A money-back guarantee tends to raise conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Refunds may rise slightly, but the extra sales usually outweigh them.
  • Trust badges and checkmark-style benefit lines add credibility (believability) to a product page.
  • Place them near the product or buy area.
  • Only show badges and claims that are true for the store.
  • False credibility signals damage trust once a visitor notices.

Writing the money-back guarantee

2 min
  • Always include a 30-day money-back guarantee for first-time visitors to a store.
  • This significantly increases perceived safety and conversion.
  • Include email contact in the guarantee section so customers can claim refunds.
  • Most stores that only say 'money back guarantee' without detail are weak.
  • A better guarantee names the risk-free period (for example 30 days).
  • It states what the customer gets back (a full refund).
  • It addresses returns and replacements directly.
  • Guarantee comparison: a 60-day money-back guarantee was rated a medium-strength offer.
  • A generic 14-day guarantee covering only damaged or unwanted items was rated weak.
  • A clearly stated 30-day risk-free period with full-refund wording was preferred.
  • Competitor stores state a money-back guarantee with a clear time window, most often 30, 60 or 90 days.
  • Put the guarantee next to the buy button.
  • Make the window and refund terms easy to find.
  • Money-back guarantee copy to adapt for your own product.
  • Replace the product name, and adapt the warranty length to your product.
  • Do not copy it verbatim. Rewrite the wording for your product and brand.
Try the new [PRODUCT NAME] risk-free for 30 days. If you don't love it, we will simply give you a full refund. Our new [PRODUCT NAME] also comes with a 24-month warranty and no returns needed policy. If you have an issue with our product, we will simply replace it for you, no questions asked, and no need to return the item to us. The checkout process is guaranteed to be secure with Visa, Mastercard, Amex and Discover or PayPal.
  • Warranty lengths on guarantees: electronic products need at least a one-month warranty.
  • Non-electronic products can use a shorter period such as six months or a one-year warranty.
  • Show the supplier's warranty on the product page, for example a one-year manufacturer warranty (a promise from the company that makes the item).
  • It helps buyers choose between similar stores.
  • Use a warranty only in the form the supplier actually provides.
  • State its length exactly as the supplier does.

Returns statements

2 min
  • Adding a free-returns policy statement directly below the Add to Cart button on product pages generates a +3.5% conversion-rate lift (an increase in the sales rate).
  • Shoppers fear losing money if they buy and become dissatisfied with the product.
  • Displaying the returns policy at the point of decision reduces this perceived risk.
  • It also lowers purchase hesitation.
  • A free-returns policy reduces the perceived risk of buying.
  • The customer knows they can send the product back if it is not right.
  • Show it on the homepage right next to the free-shipping offer.
  • Show the return policy and shipping times prominently on the website.
  • Display plenty of customer reviews.
  • Buyers who distrust online stores, often after slow deliveries elsewhere, need these signals before paying.
  • Reviews reassure them that others have bought successfully.
  • Leaving delivery areas, customer service contact and return terms off the product page makes visitors assume the purchase is risky.
  • Buyers who cannot find a return policy often leave instead of buying.
  • State these three points visibly on the page.
  • Clear, published policies act as trust signals and reduce doubt at checkout.
  • Stating return windows, shipping times and contact details up front lowers the chance of disputes and chargebacks later.
  • A dispute is a disagreement with the store. A chargeback is when the buyer asks their bank to reverse the payment.
  • Short returns headline pattern: a no-cost signal, an ease word, the word 'returns' or 'swaps', and a plain permission to send the item back.
  • It works because the buyer sees the risk removed within a few words.
  • They can scan it on any page.
  • Examples: 'Free, simple size swaps: if it doesn't fit your room, send it back at no cost.' and 'Free, easy exchanges for any reason, with a full refund if you change your mind.'
  • Only use wording that matches a policy you actually honour.
  • A returns policy visible only in the website footer effectively does not exist for customers.
  • Only about 10% of visitors look in the footer.
  • Any important policy must be visible on the page where the buying decision is made.
  • A friendlier return policy can increase returns.
  • So track the returns rate (the share of orders sent back) alongside profit.
  • Show a no-return policy on the product page when a product cannot be returned.
  • Then the customer can be pointed to it if a refund dispute or chargeback arises.

Price match and price guarantee

1 min
  • Show the price-match policy on product pages, for example in tabbed product sections.
  • A price match is a promise to refund the difference if the buyer finds a lower price.
  • Also link it in the footer.
  • Competitors that offer it on the product page have an advantage.
  • The price-match tab often seals the decision because visitors see they will get the best price.
  • Showing it on the product page is therefore important.
  • A price guarantee page makes buyers feel secure and increases conversions.
  • It is worth having even though few visitors will ever claim it.
  • Price-match guarantee template. Use it as the basis for your own policy, with the restrictions included:
We maintain competitive pricing as a priority. Within six months of purchase, if you locate the same item at a lower price from another retailer, we refund the difference. This policy also applies if our own website later shows a lower price for the same item you bought within that timeframe. Submit refund requests by emailing a product link from a competitor's site or our own within six months of your order date. Restrictions on this Price Match guarantee include: purchase must occur on our site; promotional items like rebates and BOGO offers are excluded; the item must be in stock at the competing retailer; competitors must be online-only (no physical store presence); discounters and auction sites (eBay, Overstock) don't qualify; the competitor must be an authorized retailer; and the guarantee covers item price plus shipping but excludes sales tax.
  • Show the price-match tab on the product page for trust.
  • Show the same restrictions there, so the guarantee stays honest.

Shipping, returns and policy pages

2 min
  • Customers must not have to guess about shipping, tracking, damage or refunds. A clear Shipping & Returns page answers these questions before checkout (the payment step). This means fewer support questions and fewer customers who leave before they pay (cart abandonment).
  • A Shipping & Returns page must answer these six questions before checkout. Every answer must be clear. Customers will not guess about payment timing, delivery speed or refund rules.
    1. When will my payment be charged?
    2. How soon will my items ship?
    3. Will I receive a tracking number?
    4. How can I track my order or get delivery updates?
    5. What happens if an item arrives damaged?
    6. What is your return and refund policy if I don't like the product?
  • Make key pages easy to find, such as About, Contact, Shipping, Returns and Price Match. Customers check these before buying. The more expensive the product, the more they rely on them to reduce risk.
  • An order-tracking page can raise the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy). It shows the store is reliable before they buy.
  • Write shipping in a positive way. Instead of "free shipping", write "we pay for shipping". Instead of "10-12 business days", write "we ship your item within 1-2 days" when the supplier (the company that sends the product) ships within 24 hours. If delivery is slower, give a positive reason, such as high demand, and promise tracking within 12-24 hours.
  • Stating data-protection measures builds confidence. Examples are encryption (scrambling data so only the right system can read it) and limited access to payment data. Keep these statements accurate, because they describe what the store really does.
  • Show carrier logos (logos of delivery companies) in the shipping section of product pages. Buyers look there when they check delivery. Known carriers reduce doubt before purchase.
  • Reduce a long row of trust badges to four or five of the most valuable ones, such as a money-back guarantee and express shipping. Too many badges overwhelm the customer.

Payment logos, badges and seals

3 min

Payment methods and footer signals

  • Payment methods you accept, such as Shopify Payments (Shopify's own payment service) or PayPal, work as trust signals when shown in the store footer (the bottom of every page). Visitors recognise these brands. No special image is needed.
  • Shopify adds payment icons to the footer automatically. They show which payment options a customer can use and help build trust before purchase.
  • Tick only the payment methods your Shopify store actually accepts. Every ticked method appears in the footer as a trust signal. Showing a method you do not accept misleads visitors.
  • Do not use large payment logos as trust badges. They look scammy. Small, discreet payment icons look more professional and still signal that payment is safe.
  • Display payment and security badges from brands you genuinely work with, such as McAfee Secure, Visa and Mastercard. Shoppers borrow trust from names they already know.
  • Show logos of well-known security or payment providers, such as McAfee Secure, Visa or Mastercard, to borrow their authority and trust. The buyer moves trust from the known brand to the shop.
  • A local payment provider builds trust with customers who already use it. The example was the Swiss provider TWINT. Only shops in Switzerland use it, so it signals a local store.
  • Show a recognised local payment-provider logo and a local flag on the store. This helps most in a country where customers know that provider.

Badges and seals: what the evidence shows

  • Trust badges and credit-card icons below the add-to-cart button (the button that puts an item in the basket) are optional now. They used to matter more. They do not harm the store if used. (Recorded 2023.)
  • Trust badges increase conversion. Studies show the origin of the badge does not matter. Even a stock image (a generic photo) labelled as a trusted store performed about the same as a well-known security brand.
  • Any trust badge is better than none. A study found that a simple self-made security icon raised conversions about as much as expensive trust badges. TrustGuard was mentioned as a badge provider.
  • Split tests (two versions shown to different visitors) showed almost no conversion difference between badge types. If you use badges, choose ones that signal an encrypted or secure store, such as the badges Shopify provides, instead of generic "trusted" images.
  • Trust badges, such as authorised dealer badges or a trust seal (a logo from a security provider), can add credibility. Install a trust seal only after the store has three to six months of consistent sales. Before that, there is not enough data to see whether the seal helps or hurts.
  • A free trust seal from an antivirus brand such as McAfee may reduce sales, because visitors may link it with viruses. Check sales data before and after installing any seal. Remove the seal if conversions drop.
  • TrustGuard offered a 60-day money-back guarantee. A store can test whether conversion rates improve before committing to the monthly price. If it does not justify the cost, cancel and ask for a discount. In this example, the monthly price was negotiated down from a higher rate to about 15 dollars.
  • Trust badges and credit-card icons: how the advice has changed.
    • 2019: any badge is better than none.
    • 2020: badge origin does not matter.
    • 2022: choose badges that signal a secure store.
    • 2023: optional, harmless if used.
    • Current rule: use badges that are true for your store, and check conversion data before keeping a seal.

Charity, authority and third-party claims

3 min

Charity pledges

  • A charity pledge (a promise to give to a cause) builds trust only when it names the actual organisation and shows its logo and information. Vague claims, such as supporting abandoned dogs without naming a partner, look shady to new visitors.
  • Even a very small local nonprofit is enough for a charity pledge. Name it, show its logo and include information about it, so visitors can check the partnership.
  • A store that donates 10% of sales to a cause must state the 10% figure and name who receives it. The percentage alone, without a named partner, does not create credibility.

Authority and press

  • Authority signals include mentions in major publications and expert endorsements (recommendations from experts). An expert endorsement that also contains a benefit statement from a relevant professional gives both authority and social proof (other people's opinions) at the same time.

  • Press mentions, expert endorsements, notable customers and research-backed ingredients all build authority. Use each one only when it is true for the store.

  • Add third-party studies, reliable outside sources or a small self-run survey with a clear method to the page. Quote specific percentages from them, because statistics help buyers justify the purchase.

  • A named ingredient backed by published clinical research (studies printed in medical journals) can serve as proof in a feature list. The buyer sees a known mechanism behind the promised result. Use such a claim only when the evidence is real and can be cited. An unsupported "clinically proven" statement damages trust.

  • Claims such as damage reduction, frizz control or clinical-style wording need concrete proof on the page before they convert. Proof includes how the technology works, test data or guarantee terms. An audit (a review of the site) should flag homepage claims without this support as a trust gap.

  • Best-in-class claim pattern: formula superlative + category + location + measurable count or rating. A bare superlative (such as "the best") is disbelieved. A checkable number makes the claim credible. Examples: 'Most-reviewed sleep mask seller in Germany, with 4,800 verified reviews.' and 'Rated 4.7 out of 5 by 2,300 verified buyers across our pet bed range.'

  • Third-party certifications from independent organisations (such as organic certification, which confirms production standards) serve as proof. Display the certification badge and the organisation's name in product copy.

  • A product tested and rated by an independent testing organisation can carry the rating and test results in product copy as proof. Example: Choice, an Australian organisation that tests products for effectiveness and gives ratings.

  • Customers expect sellers to praise their own products, so they are skeptical. Independent proof points confirm that an emotional purchase is also a rational one. This makes all the copy before them more persuasive.

  • "Featured-in" logo bars often come from paid sponsored posts or affiliate placements (paid partner links), not from organic press coverage. Treat such logos as paid authority signals. Show them only where the placement is accurate and can be backed up.

  • Do not fabricate press or media mentions. Show "as seen in" logos only if the brand was actually featured.

  • Show press mentions as a marquee (a scrolling strip of logos), rather than a large block. Keep reviews visible and repeated on other pages.

  • A low-cost alternative to paid press: get a review or feature from niche blogs or affiliate publishers, who receive affiliate traffic (visitors sent through their links). Or create your own niche blog on WordPress that features the product.

  • Linking out from the landing page to outside press articles is debated. It can add authority and help SEO (search engine ranking), but it takes visitors away from the purchase path. Decide per store. The original advice was not to link them.

  • Trust signals such as authorised retailer badges work best when added brand by brand, in this order:

    1. Add the authorised-retailer badge to the collection page of each brand.
    2. Add the same badge to the product pages of that brand.
    3. Add further signals (payment methods, carrier logos, memberships, charities, customer list) as they become available.

  • Authorised retailer badges raise trust gradually, not as an immediate jump in sales.

Phone number, social media and local cues

3 min

Phone number

  • Include the phone number prominently for higher-ticket items (expensive products), with the header (the top of every page) recommended. Most calls come from people checking legitimacy before they order, not from complaints.
  • Display the phone number large on the homepage and every page, so visitors see it without searching. A visible number builds trust and, paradoxically, reduces how often people actually call.
  • Placement of the phone number: the header is recommended for higher-ticket items (2020 advice), and the later rule is to show it on the homepage and every page. Both fit together. The header covers every page, and a large number on the homepage supports it.
  • A vanity number (a phone number whose last digits spell a word) adds credibility at no extra cost. Example: 333-MICS for a podcast-microphone store. Number-search tools show which vanity numbers are available.

Social media as a credibility check

  • Before a shopper buys, they often check whether the store is a real, active business by looking for its Facebook page or Instagram account. Having both profiles and keeping them updated protects against a scam impression and raises conversions.
  • Social media is a credibility check, not a sales channel. The profiles must exist and show recent activity, so visitors who look them up find signs of a live business.
  • A survey reported that 78% of respondents said a company's social-media posts affect their buying decisions. This is a secondary figure from a single survey. Verify it before quoting it.
  • Brand social pages are mainly for credibility, not follower counts. First-time visitors often search the store name on Google. Active Facebook and Instagram profiles in the results reassure them before they buy.
  • The goal of social pages is credibility, not going viral or collecting many likes. Visitors who look up the business should feel confident enough to place large orders.
  • Post on the brand's Facebook and Instagram pages once or twice a week to show the business is active. Posts do not need big news. Product highlights, new supplier announcements or short updates are enough.
  • Post at least once a week, even if the post only links to a product in the store and gives a short reason why customers like it. This is the acceptable minimum for keeping the page looking active.
  • Frequency of posting: the recommended rhythm is one or two posts a week. Once a week is the acceptable minimum. Use the higher rhythm when you have material, and never let the minimum slip.
  • Do not create social pages, post once and then go a year without posting. A visitor who sees that the last post is a year old will assume the business has closed, which defeats the purpose.
  • Weekly-post concept prompt. Fill in the placeholders and use it as the prompt:
I'm [your name], and the owner of [your store domain]. I need concepts for our weekly posts on Facebook and Instagram, covering the next 30 weeks. Each week, please provide one post idea that's ready to publish without edits, such as a product spotlight, a store product link paired with a reason customers like it, a brief business update, or a tip tied to [your niche]. Keep every idea short, appropriate for a small online shop, and aligned with our brand.
  • After saving a Facebook page bio, check the live page to see whether the text is cut off. If it is, shorten the bio, save again and recheck until the whole text shows under the profile picture and header.
  • Services that artificially add likes and comments to ads do not directly improve CPM (cost per 1,000 ad views) or quality ranking (the ad platform's score). Visible engagement can encourage real users to comment, which grows engagement organically and lowers CPM over time.

Local and personal cues

  • Add a personal note from the staff or owner on best-selling product pages. It should explain the product's benefits in plain words, based on what reviews say customers like, instead of relying only on generic feature lists. The note builds credibility and reduces questions.
  • Customers look for signs of a real business behind the store. Show who runs the store and how to reach them. This answers the question of whether the seller exists.
  • Sell differentiators (things that set you apart) only if they are true. Examples: woman-owned, veteran-owned or surfer-owned status, free shipping, a price-match guarantee, a 24/7 customer service channel or secure checkout.

Differentiator icons and honest claims

1 min
  • The "why buy from us" icons in the pre-footer (the section above the footer) show what sets the store apart. They can show free shipping, a price-match guarantee, a 24/7 customer service channel, secure checkout or the owner's status. The Source A theme allows up to seven icons, and you can use one to seven.
  • Use only differentiator claims that are true for the store. For example, show secure checkout only when the store runs on Shopify's checkout. Each claim needs a matching icon and a short label.
  • A visitor who sees a claim the store does not keep will notice it. False credibility signals damage trust.

Honesty limits

3 min
  • Do not pretend things are what they are not. Building a perception must stay honest and trustworthy. This matters most in very competitive markets, or when you resell products that other sellers also carry.
  • Show only the real average star rating and the real number of reviews on the store. Do not edit widget text to display a higher rating or a larger review count than the store actually has.
  • Generating fake reviews with AI and importing them into a live store to make it look established is deceptive. It can break consumer-protection rules and app or platform policies, and it can put the store at risk of being shut down.
  • Writing your own reviews is acceptable only when they are based on real experiences or on material the supplier has approved for use. Inventing fake customer stories or posing as different people is not allowed.
  • Placeholder reviews (dummy text) are for layout and QA (quality checks) only. This prompt produces test content that must never go live, be used in ads or be presented as customer proof. Use it only for design and testing, with the output marked as required:
Act as a copywriter producing believable placeholder reviews, strictly for layout and QA work. Write 12 brief mock reviews, each one to three sentences long, for an item called [PRODUCT NAME]. Every entry needs an invented first name with a country flag emoji (for example Sarah 🇸🇬), a headline of one to five words, and a body of one to three sentences that refers to one concrete trait or advantage such as build quality, delivery, packaging, or outcomes. Put a star score from 1 to 5 after each review line. Vary the tone: some upbeat, some neutral, and one mildly negative. Start the output with the line "SAMPLE REVIEWS, FOR DESIGN/TEST ONLY. DO NOT PUBLISH." Each review goes on its own line in this pattern: SAMPLE, [Name] [flag], "[Headline]", [body]. ⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️ Include no personal data and never suggest these came from real buyers. Keep everything invented and limited to layout or testing. These reviews must never go live on a storefront or into ads, and must never be used as genuine customer proof.
  • Published statements about the store's data and processes must be accurate, because they describe what the store actually does.
  • Percentages such as 91 or 97 percent of users noticing a result need a published survey base: how many people answered and how long they used the product, for example 100 users over 90 days. A figure without this basis is an unsupported claim and can create advertising risk.
  • Only show "as seen in" logos if the brand was actually featured. Do not fabricate press or media mentions.
  • Testimonials and FAQ answers must come from real customers and real questions.
  • Trust badges and claims are only useful when they are true for the store. False credibility signals damage trust once a visitor notices them.
  • Badges from a seal provider must be checked against sales data, because a seal that does not help can hurt.
  • Use a claim such as "clinically proven" only when the evidence is real and can be cited.
  • Featured-in logos from paid placements must be backed up by the actual placement.
  • The 78% social-media statistic and the 88% Zendesk review benchmark (a figure from a help-desk software company) are secondary or vendor figures. Verify them before quoting.
  • Do not quote the 78% social-media statistic or the 88% review-trust benchmark as verified facts. Verify them before use.
  • Customer totals must come from your own order records, not from a competitor's page.
  • Do not show payment methods you do not accept.
  • Do not present a self-written review as a customer review.

Further details

1 min
  • Social media profiles for ecommerce stores build visitor trust through proof of active business operations, not through follower volume or viral reach. Prospective customers often search your store name on Google before completing a purchase. Active, professional social profiles that appear in the search results and are linked from your website reassure first-time visitors that your business is legitimate and currently operating.
  • The 'About us / our story' page converts poorly, with roughly 3% of visitors reaching it, but it should still exist for trust.
  • Competitor stores show free shipping, a money-back promise and easy returns as trust badges near the product. Place these badges close to the add-to-cart button and state the exact terms.
