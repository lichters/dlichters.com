Playbook › 6. Conversion optimization

Visual design, media and clarity

Section 6.9 · 9 of 22 in this chapter · 29 min read

In short

  • Keep every page simple. A confused visitor leaves without buying.
  • Use one main brand color, and one bright color only for buttons.
  • Use many clear product photos, but keep each file small so the page loads fast.
  • Keep each paragraph to four lines at most. Use short bullets and headlines.
  • Show real customer reviews and proof. Skip fake tricks.
More key points
  • Your homepage must show, in plain words, what you sell. A visitor should understand it at once.
  • Keep the homepage simple: a few clear sections, not a pile of posts, bestsellers and recommendations.
  • Most visitors land on a product page from an ad. Make products easy to find and the buy path short.
  • Check everything on a phone as carefully as on a computer. Many customers shop on phones.

Clarity comes first

2 min

  • Clarity comes first. A confused visitor does not buy. Your offer (the deal you sell) and your marketing message (what you say to sell it) must be easy to understand. Before you add anything to a page, ask two things: does it really help the customer, and can it be made simpler?

  • Complex or expensive products may need more information on the page than simple ones. Every extra element must earn its place. Apply the same simplicity to the rest of the site, not only to the offer.

  • Focus on the few most important facts a customer needs to buy this product from this store. Show them as cleanly as possible. A clear page removes the need for conversion tricks. Examples are fake scarcity (false "only a few left" messages) and fake discounts.

  • An offer the visitor does not notice effectively does not exist. Present it so it is easy to see. Make sure other page elements do not hide it.

  • Usability (how easy a site is to use) affects conversion (the share of visitors who buy) more than visual design. This is true once trust factors (signs that make people trust you) and good products are in place. - Overly decorative pages with heavy motion do not raise conversion if visitors struggle to find products and buy.

  • Usable and fast beats heavily designed. Heavy design slows load time (how long a page takes to open) and hurts conversion. Let people find products quickly. A beautiful design can still be kept if it loads fast.

  • A clean, simple, easy-to-use layout converts better than an impressive-looking design. You do not need to be an expert web designer. Copy a proven layout that works.

  • Keep the design minimal. Avoid flashy effects, motion, too much color, and extra bells and whistles (fancy features). They distract customers and rarely improve conversion. Design does not drive conversion as much as often assumed.

  • Do not rely on large decorative images (pictures used only for looks), video backgrounds (videos playing behind the text), or parallax effects (background images that move at a different speed when you scroll) to sell a product. They do not answer the buyer's questions. They can hurt speed and usability.

  • Avoid bright, flashy ad boxes on the home page and product pages. They can look tacky and reduce trust. Use a clean design that focuses on what your customers need. Do not copy competitors' layouts.

  • Common web design mistakes for a dropship store (a store that sells products shipped straight from suppliers) include:

    • a busy look: animations, motion, glitter-style effects, and many bright colors
    • very small text
    • blurry or tiny product images
    • very large image files that slow the site down

    Each one lowers trust, readability (how easy text is to read), or load speed.

Cognitive load and skimming

2 min
  • Shoppers avoid any work that makes them think. Examples are working out a saving or decoding an offer. This mental effort is called cognitive load (how hard the brain must work). Use simple, direct words on every element of a product page, so the value is clear at a glance.
  • Design pages so the visitor does not have to think. Every extra calculation or search for information adds load and reduces conversion. Keep the path to the decision simple.
  • Shoppers avoid mental effort. Each element of a product page should be understood at a glance, with no calculation or interpretation. Working out a percentage discount, or decoding a feature list, lowers conversion. State the result directly.
  • Customers often skip long product descriptions. They look at images, GIFs (short looping animated pictures) and reviews first. Visual proof and reviews matter more than text length.
  • Most visitors look at product images and GIFs before they read long text. The visuals carry the product description. Reviews should be visible. They should answer typical buyer worries, such as fit or size, in the review images.
  • Skimmers (people who scan quickly) need proof they can take in at a glance. Show each proof point (a fact that backs up a claim) as a clear chart, simple graph, or short quote from a respected professional. Colorful, easy-to-read data convinces skimmers faster than dense tables. Careful buyers will still check the detail.
  • Each section of a landing page (a single page built to sell one thing) should answer one question in a scannable way. Most visitors do not read long paragraphs. Use headlines, icons (small pictures) and bold key words so people can scan the page.
  • About 75 percent of visitors will not read three paragraphs of text on a page. Explain the product with visuals and short steps instead.
  • Dense copy with small text and long blocks gets skipped. Good wording has no effect if the layout discourages reading. This matters most on mobile (phones), where small, bunched-up text is harder to read than on desktop (computers). Check how the copy looks before you judge its persuasive power.

White space, proximity and layout

1 min
  • White space is any empty area, not necessarily white. It groups elements, focuses attention on the middle, and prevents clutter. Clutter (too much in one place) adds cognitive load and makes viewers leave.
  • Proximity means things placed close together look like they belong together. Space between them separates groups. On a product page, a title, subtitle, text, button and price box can be grouped or separated only by spacing, even if they look different.
  • White space is one of the strongest clarity tools. It makes text, images and buttons easier for the eye to process. Crowding elements into a small space makes visitors work harder and raises the chance they leave.
  • Generous white space helps visitors read and understand text and images. It reduces eye strain (tired eyes) caused by busy layouts. White space matters most on mobile screens, since most e-commerce traffic (visitors to online shops) now comes from phones, not desktops.
  • More space around text and elements makes a page easier to read. Increase section spacing rather than squeezing content together.
  • Break up long sections with spacing and shorter paragraphs. What you see in the Shopify editor (the editing screen of Shopify, the online store platform) is exactly how customers will see it.
  • Keep the area around the add to cart button (the button that puts a product in the shopping basket) clean. Remove competing colored elements, so the buy button cannot be missed. Visitors should never have to search for the main purchase action.
  • A cluttered area above the fold (the part of the page you see before scrolling) makes a page hard to read. Clutter includes many colors, mixed font weights (thick and thin letters together), tiny text, and tightly packed checkmarks. Text too small to recognize, and repeated lines, waste space. An example is two identical "best price guarantee" statements.

Color system

2 min
  • Use one main brand color for the design. Use one contrasting color reserved only for calls to action (buttons that ask the visitor to act, such as "buy now"). Keep the palette (the set of colors) as small as possible. Every extra color competes with the buttons.
  • The add to cart color does not need to be red, yellow, green or blue. It must contrast (stand out clearly) with the other main colors of the theme (the store's design template). A color wheel (a chart of colors) helps find a contrasting hue (shade) for a given palette.
  • Use a limited color system: white, black and one brand color, or a brand palette with lighter and darker shades. Never use more than four colors across the store. Make the call-to-action button a darker, bolder shade so it stands out.
  • Keep the store colors to at most four: white, black, one brand color or palette, and a darker bold color for the call-to-action button so it stands out.
  • Use a simple color scheme based on the brand's chosen colors, about three colors. Do not overuse them on the site. This is the older target of about three colors (2021-22). A limit of four colors applies when a brand needs a fourth tone for the call-to-action.
  • For a Black Friday store (the big sale day after Thanksgiving), use a theme color dedicated to the sale, in a simple modern palette. Avoid very bright orange, yellow or red. These can look skeptical (doubtful) to customers.
  • When a section background changes from light to dark, switch the text, icon and divider (line) colors from dark to light. Then they stay visible. Keep the colors taken from the brand palette.
  • Dark text on a dark tab background (a clickable section header) is hard to read. Use light text, such as white, on dark inactive tabs. Check the contrast (how clearly colors stand apart) in the live preview (a preview of changes before saving). Do this before you save the layout settings.

Typography and text length

1 min
  • Use one consistent, high-quality font (style of letters) across the whole store. Keep the same color and font use on every page. This builds brand value (how much people trust and remember your brand).
  • Use text large enough to read easily on mobile and desktop. Small text forces visitors to strain. They skim and skip important information.
  • Long paragraphs make visitors lose interest and leave. Bullets and short headlines work better.
  • Keep each paragraph to no more than four lines. Use bullet points and several short headlines instead. Landing page copy should never exceed about four lines per paragraph, because longer blocks make visitors leave. Text blocks of eight or nine lines make visitors leave.
  • Use one-line, benefit-driven bullet points on the landing page instead of large paragraphs. A benefit-driven bullet says what the buyer gains. First-time visitors leave pages that look like long text.
  • Long walls of text on the product page are hard to read on mobile. Compress the product description into short sections that buyers can scan.
  • Long product descriptions, especially AI-written ones, can overwhelm shoppers. Split them into collapsible sections (they open and close when clicked) using H6, a small heading level. This keeps all the text on the page. The visitor opens only the parts they want to read.
  • Long product descriptions are fine when the product needs them. Split them into short paragraphs and use several h2 and h3 subheadings (titles; h2 is the bigger level, h3 sits under it). Scanning shoppers look for headings that match their question. They read only that section.
  • Check that long text is split into chunks with subheadings. Use short paragraphs of two to three lines and a readable font size of 18.

Headlines and copy layout

1 min
  • The headline must be bigger and bolder than the body copy (the main text) and the rest of the text on the page. Its size and weight decide whether it is seen as the entry point to the text.
  • Show product benefits inside the product image gallery (the set of product pictures). Add short benefit text on the images. Visitors understand the offer and key benefits while scrolling through the pictures.
  • Keep star rating icons (small star symbols for reviews) visibly smaller than the price. The price should stay the most important element. Oversized icons pull attention away from it.

Product images and visual proof

2 min
  • High-quality product images are critical. Images can be digitally created rather than photographed. Freelancer sites such as Elance, Odesk, or 99designs (where you hire designers for small jobs) can produce professional images. These make products stand out from competitors.
  • Image quality matters. Product images can be edited with Canva (an easy design tool) or AI tools to look more professional. Testimonial images (pictures of customers with their words) in a raw, real-customer style also work well. Quoted written reviews are acceptable if you have no photos.
  • Use as many product images as possible, covering every angle, in large, clean, high-resolution (very sharp) format. Strong product pages can show over 10 images. Buyers spending a lot of money want to inspect the product fully.
  • Include product photos only if each one adds value to a purchase decision. Zoomed-in detail shots and a product in a room are valuable. Repeated lifestyle shots (photos of the product in daily life) add little. 50 photos is excessive.
  • Competitors with low-resolution photos, or only a few small screenshots, leave room to win. Upload high-resolution images from several angles. Add more product shots than the competitor.
  • Ask suppliers (companies that make or ship your products) for more product images. For example, ask for photos from their warehouse or from trade shows. Competitors often reuse the same two supplier images, so extra images help you stand out.
  • Use good-quality lifestyle images on the product page. They show real use and increase trust.
  • Replace the demo hero image (the big placeholder picture at the top of a page, supplied by the theme) with supplier lifestyle photos. Use photos that show products you actually sell, if you have them. Remove homepage and menu categories for product lines you have no approved supplier for. Add them back once you get one.
  • Use a short video or photo strip near the top of the page. Include three image placeholders (empty spots for pictures). One shows the product itself. One shows the product in use. One shows the result or a lifestyle scene. Real photos from the supplier or from customers beat stock images (generic photos bought from libraries).
  • When a product has no obvious size, show it being worn or held by a person in the product images. Shoppers can then judge its size. Without this reference, buyers cannot tell what they will receive.
  • Show the size information inside the product image, with clear indications. Buyers then do not need to scroll down to a separate size guide. Test this against the current layout.
  • For products that need explanation, add a landscape (wide) video of customer testimonials. Or add a longer case-study interview (a detailed story) with a real user. Show how the product is used and why it is valued.
  • For bundle product images (several products sold together), use pack images with a transparent background (no background color behind the product). This makes it easy to show discounts and the add-to-cart button on the bundle.
  • Enlarge product images on desktop, and keep the thumbnails (small preview pictures). Images are a major factor in conversion for products that need demonstration.

Image quality, customization and compression

2 min
  • Product images must be high quality and correctly sized, so the customer can see and identify the product. Keep file sizes small enough that the site loads quickly. Avoid blurry, tiny, or very large image files. Large files slow site loading and make pages load poorly. Images from good suppliers are usually high quality, which is also a sign of a strong supplier.
  • Low-quality or badly prepared product images directly reduce conversion. You face a trade-off. You can compress images hard for page speed, or keep them strong enough to persuade a purchase.
  • Before uploading product images, resize them to the correct dimensions. Then compress them (make the file smaller) so the file stays small while quality remains good.
  • Customize product images so they look different from competitors selling the same items. This matters most for resellers (sellers of other brands' products). It can mean shooting your own photos or editing manufacturer images.
  • A static ad creative (a still ad image) with text can also be used as a website image. The same layout works on a product page or homepage. Check that the text stays readable on a phone before you publish it. Mobile visitors make up most traffic.
  • Add a stamp or badge (a small label) to the main product image that states the offer. For example: product A plus product B, with the words "for free" below. An offer shown on the image itself works better than a pop-up (a window that appears over the page).
  • Product images with circular guarantee badges, benefit lines and a USP (unique selling point, what makes you different) overlay (a layer on top of the picture) get read more than plain paragraphs. The information is already visible in the image. Use this on all main product images.
  • High-quality infographics (pictures that explain facts with simple graphics) add information visitors actually read. This is especially true when placed on the product image itself. Early on, an infographic can be bought cheaply on Fiverr (a freelancer site) for about $5 to $10.
  • Place visuals of the product prominently near the top of the landing page. Use clean, high-quality images that show the product clearly. Strong product imagery supports the hero (the main top section) and reinforces trust.
  • Once the product image is set, add a product image that shows the bundle, with a transparent background.

GIFs and video

2 min
  • End product pages with an image or video of someone using the product, smiling. This creates a positive feeling. Avoid empty statements or plain benefit lists. Always pair benefits with visual social proof (visible evidence from other buyers).
  • Add a short GIF to the product description that shows how the product works. Visual demonstration helps customers understand the product before buying.
  • Use high-quality short GIFs that show the product in action and the key steps. Place them near the top of the product page. Avoid overusing GIFs. Check that they look right on the mobile layout.
  • If there is no UGC (user-generated content, meaning pictures and videos made by real customers), cut a 1 to 3 second clip from an existing video ad. Export it as a GIF for the product page. Keep the GIF under 800 KB (kilobytes, a unit of file size).
  • Show product demonstration early, such as a GIF or autoplay video (a video that starts on its own). Do not place video in the hero image. Put product videos below the add-to-cart button. Lazy load them (load them only when the visitor scrolls to them).
  • The first 10 seconds of a video should clearly state the benefit of watching. Viewers in the right place stay, even if the video starts slowly.
  • Livecom, a vendor (a company that sells a tool or service), claims that bubble videos (short FAQ-style product videos placed on the page) can lift on-page conversion rates as high as 40 percent. This is the vendor's own figure, not a verified benchmark (a tested standard). Test it on your own store before you plan around it.
  • Livecom's vendor states that bubble videos reach an engagement rate (the share of viewers who interact) of 20 to 30 percent. Treat this as a vendor figure. Measure your own engagement rate on the same videos.
  • Shoppable video (video where viewers can click products to buy) is meant to raise conversion. It shows products in use, highlights benefits, promotions and discounts, and adds UGC and reviews. It is also meant to increase time on site and buying confidence, compared with a plain video player.

Trust, badges and social proof

1 min
  • The halo effect means people trust a brand more when it looks polished and credible overall. Consistent visual and copy quality adds trust on its own. This is separate from comparing your product against competitors. Keep the whole page professional, not only the headline or the price.
  • Templates are a starting point only. A page-builder template (a ready-made page design you edit) used without changing its copy, layout or design makes a store look generic. This weakens conversion.
  • Unique branding and a professional logo, with matching product photos and site colors, make a dropshipping store look like a real brand. This sets it apart from most competitors.
  • A strong home page can use a moving announcement bar (a banner with moving text at the top), short benefit statements, clear product imagery, and a comparison section against competitors. These sections were presented as elements that helped visitors trust the store and convert.
  • Add genuine customer reviews to product pages.

Accessibility

1 min
  • Accessibility (making a site usable for people with limits) matters for 5 to 10% of visitors who have visual or other limitations. Use good color contrast and readable font sizes. Avoid small light gray text on white backgrounds. Consider voice search apps (apps you speak to for searching) only if your niche has many such users.
  • An overlay (a transparent layer placed over an image) helps text stand out. Without it, text over a busy or bright image can be hard to read. Add one when you place text on an image.

Homepage design

3 min
  • The headline is the big title at the top of the page. If a visitor cannot understand it, rewrite it so it clearly says what the store sells, or remove it completely. An example of a bad headline is a vague phrase about returning to a theme.
  • A homepage that stacks blog posts, bestsellers, recently viewed items and recommendations all at once looks random to a first-time visitor. Keep the homepage simple, with a few clear sections.
  • Make the best products easy to find on the homepage and in the navigation. The navigation is the menu that takes visitors to other pages. Visitors who have to search for 20 minutes before finding a product often leave without buying.
  • In a niche store, most visitors arrive directly on a product page. A niche store sells one narrow type of product. So the homepage only needs to look credible. Keep it simple, with one clear image and a collection list that sends visitors to the products quickly. A collection is a group of similar products, such as all slippers.
  • Remove the contact form from the homepage. A contact form is a box where visitors type a message to you. Keep the layout basic, because the main goal is for visitors to click into the catalog or a product page quickly. The catalog is the full list of your products.
  • Check the homepage and the top promotion bar in the order of the review list below. The promotion bar is the thin strip at the very top of the page.
  • Test the homepage on mobile. Mobile means a phone screen. Check that the homepage USP, the offer and the reviews appear on phone screens as clearly as on desktop. A USP (unique selling point) is the main reason to buy from you, in one line. Do not let a key message exist only in the desktop header. The header is the top part of the page with the menu.
  • Check that the USP highlight bars on the homepage still match what you offer. Update the homepage sections that feature products and collections so they show live products, meaning real items you sell now, not demo items.
  • Add quick links, which are shortcut buttons to categories. Show 5 to 6 visual category shortcuts. Style them like Instagram story cards, which are round or square picture bubbles. Place them above the fold, meaning visible without scrolling down. This generates a +2.8% conversion rate lift. Conversion rate is the share of visitors who buy. A lift is an increase.
  • Mobile shoppers cannot find categories when collection links appear below the fold, meaning only after scrolling. Use visual patterns that shoppers already know, such as Instagram stories. This reduces cognitive friction, which is the mental effort needed to figure out what to do next. It also encourages browsing.
  • Best sellers, new and trending sections do not change conversion rate in a meaningful way. Most visitors arrive from targeted ads on product pages, not from these sections. Targeted ads are paid ads shown to specific people.
  • On the homepage, a bold, clearly visible offer is easier to notice. Use simple visual icons, which are small picture symbols. Leave plenty of white space, meaning empty space around items, so the offer stands out. Offer presentation should follow this clean, prominent layout.
  • Homepage structure, in this order:
    1. Headline with the main promise, visible as soon as the page loads.
    2. Navigation to the main product types, with the most important ones first.
    3. A large, simple USP block about the business, surrounded by white space.
    4. A free shipping and free returns offer, with one short line of detail, such as who pays for return shipping.
    5. The review count and star rating, with a visible link to read reviews.
    6. Best sellers.
    7. Alternate business messages and product sections down the page. Do not stack product sections together.
    8. Lower sections with ingredient or safety information and social media links.
  • Set up the homepage collection list:
    1. Drag the Collection list section up on the homepage.
    2. Select a collection, such as Slippers.
    3. Remove collections that do not match the niche, such as Jewelry or Clothing.
    4. Add the products you want to feature.
    5. Save and check the layout on mobile.
  • Keep the homepage clear of decorative noise, meaning decoration that adds no meaning. The first view must answer what the store sells.

Hero, banners and sliders

2 min
  • The hero image is the big picture at the top of a page. Place text on it that tells the customer what the business does for them. Add a promotional message only when there is a real offer. Keep the wording clear enough to change later.
  • Use one hero image, not a slideshow. A slideshow is a set of pictures that change over time. Slideshows rely on JavaScript, which is extra code that makes pages do things, and that code slows the site down. Turn off autoplay, which means slides change by themselves without a click. Keep one static image, meaning one picture that does not move.
  • Reported research suggests only about 4% of visitors see or interact with the second and third slides of a slideshow. Each picture in a slideshow is one slide. This is the reason to keep only the first slide as a single hero image.
  • A slideshow as the main homepage visual distracts from the product. It is not necessary for a single-product or niche store. Use one clear image instead, and remove the second slide.
  • If a homepage slider has several slides, link each slide to a different collection. Do not link several slides to the same collection.
  • The Highlights banner section is a row of small blocks about benefits. It has four blocks as a starting point: Free shipping, Give us a call, Easy returns and Price match. Each block has a heading and text. The section can hold up to four blocks by default.
  • Each Highlights banner block can use a preset icon or a custom one. Custom icons should be 50 x 50 px, meaning 50 by 50 pixels. A pixel is one dot on the screen. Custom icons should look similar in style to the presets. Custom icons are optional.
  • Each Highlights banner block can link to a page on the store, such as shipping, contact us, returns or price match. Linking reinforces the benefit. It also gives the customer an easy path to details.
  • Use carousels that slide sideways for testimonials and featured items. A carousel is a row you swipe to see more. Carousels take less vertical space on mobile. Show a peek of the next item, a small edge of it, so visitors see that the row scrolls. In PageFly, a page-building app, the slideshow is set in the slideshow section of the element settings. Look under the General tab. There you can delete or duplicate slides.
  • A feature block can show an image with a title and explanation. When no images exist, it can show an icon, a title and an explanation instead.

Homepage sections and featured products

1 min
  • Repeat the same key call to action in several places on the homepage. A call to action is the button that asks the visitor to act, such as "Shop now." Repeat it in the header, a mid-page section and the footer. The footer is the bottom of the page. Different customers notice different parts of the page, so repetition increases the chance they act.
  • Place a search bar lower on the homepage. Shoppers who scroll past the header can then find products directly. Faster product discovery usually leads to more purchases than browsing through several collections.
  • Featured product section, as a pattern:
    • Use a heading and a subheading, which is the smaller line under it.
    • Choose a collection to show a small product grid, meaning products in neat rows.
    • Add a button labelled Shop collection.
    • Optionally, use a light background color to separate it from the white sections above.
    • Example: Featured Camping Gear as the heading, Our most-loved picks for the trail as the subheading, a collection of four products, and a Shop collection button.
  • Alternate business messages and product sections down the page. Do not stack product sections together.

Navigation, header and menus

1 min
  • UX (user experience) means how easily and pleasantly visitors use your store. Better navigation reduces friction, which is any obstacle in the way. It builds trust and increases conversions.
  • A mega menu is a big menu with several columns, images and featured products. It replaces simple dropdowns for main categories. A dropdown is a small list that opens under a button. Mega menus help customers find products faster.
  • Reorder the header menu from best-selling to worst-selling category.
  • Turn the first menu item into a Shop dropdown with clear category names.
  • The store logo should link to the full collection of all products. A Shop Now button should go straight to one product. This keeps the two paths clearly separate for visitors.
  • Avoid extra header links such as Home or Shop Now above a product page. They draw the visitor away from the purchase. Keep the visitor on one page, so more leads turn into sales. A lead is a person who shows interest, such as someone who clicked your ad.
  • A navigation bar on the product page distracts visitors who should stay on one landing page. A landing page is the page a visitor arrives on first. Remove the navigation bar so the page stays focused.
  • A sticky header stays on the screen as the customer scrolls. It shrinks into a thin bar with a hamburger menu, the three-line icon that opens the menu. Customers keep the navigation, search bar and cart within reach. They do not need to scroll back to the top.
  • Quick links and category shortcuts on the homepage should follow the same navigation logic as the menu.
  • Reduce the logo size and place it on the left side of the navigation.
  • Check the top promotion bar. Add free shipping plus a percentage discount with a code, if it fits. A discount code is a word the buyer types at checkout to get money off.

Collection pages and filters

2 min
  • Filters are options that narrow the products shown, such as type or color. Filters improve user experience by helping shoppers find products faster. They also make the store look more professional. This signals to suppliers, the companies that send you products, that the store is built to grow.
  • Filters let customers quickly narrow results when browsing large catalogs. This reduces frustration and raises the chance of a purchase.
  • Filters help visitors reach the product they want with fewer clicks. That is their purpose on the collection page. Filters matter most when the store has many products. A store with few products needs only a short list of filters.
  • Filters such as product type and color help shoppers find products faster within a collection. Add them once the store has several suppliers and enough products to filter through.
  • Filters that match real product attributes, such as product type and color, reduce search effort. An attribute is a fact about a product, like its color. Tags, which are labels on each product, only work when they are consistent. Use one agreed spelling for each attribute across all products.
  • Collection filters work only on product tags. A product appears under a filter option only if it has the matching tag. Add the tags to each product page. Then add the same tags to the tag lists in the collection sidebar, the side panel that shows the filter options.
  • Build filter options from the attributes customers use to sort products. Add tags to products as each new supplier brand and product is uploaded. Expand the tag lists over time, so filtering stays useful as the catalog grows.
  • Example: on a store with 500 products, a shopper who wants a blue longboard can narrow the grid by the Longboards type tag and the Blue color tag. Only matching products remain. Without tags on each product, this combination shows no results.
  • Pagination splits products across several category pages, such as 12 products per page. It hurts usability, conversion and SEO. SEO means getting found on Google. Customers miss the second page and bounce, meaning they leave at once. Google may also fail to crawl pages behind JavaScript pagination. Crawling means Google's robots reading pages. This causes indexation problems. Indexation means Google adding pages to its search results. Lost link value, which is the ranking strength from links to a page, follows as well.
    • Disable pagination and show all products on one category page.
    • Expect roughly 3-5% more organic traffic, meaning free visitors from search results, plus a small conversion lift.
    • This can take about two weeks to show.

Product page layout

1 min
  • Keep the product page to one purchase path. Remove navigation and extra header links so the visitor's attention stays on the product.
  • Customers often skip long product descriptions and look at images first. Place the visuals above the text they support.
  • Reviews should be visible. They should answer typical buyer concerns, such as fit or size, in the review images.

Reference stores and review procedures

2 min
  • When building or fixing a store, find an established store. It should sell in a related price range and product type, but in a different niche. Copy its page structure and navigation. Do not copy its colors or exact design.
  • Check the default currency. The default currency is the money shown first, such as dollars. It must match the main traffic source, meaning where most of your visitors come from.
  • Check the pricing display. Raise low prices. Show regular prices crossed out next to sale prices, so buyers see the saving.
  • Expand the footer with customer care links, an about section and an email opt-in. An email opt-in is a sign-up box for emails.
  • Add a brand story or about page. A brand story tells visitors who you are.
  • Create collections for sub-styles and group matching products in each. A sub-style is a smaller version of a style.
  • Review order for a store homepage:
    1. Check that the default currency matches the main traffic source.
    2. Read the homepage headline. Rewrite or remove it if a visitor cannot understand it.
    3. Check the top promotion bar. Add free shipping plus a percentage discount with a code, if it fits.
    4. Reduce the logo size and place it on the left side of the navigation.
    5. Reorder the header menu from best-selling to worst-selling category.
    6. Turn the first menu item into a Shop dropdown with clear category names.
    7. Create collections for sub-styles and group matching products in each.
    8. Add a brand story or about page.
    9. Raise low prices, and show regular prices crossed out next to sale prices.
    10. Add genuine customer reviews to product pages.
    11. Expand the footer with customer care links, an about section and an email opt-in.
  • Procedure for modeling a reference store:
    1. Pick an established store that sells in a related price range and product type, but in a different niche.
    2. Look at its homepage. Note what appears first and how sections are ordered.
    3. Note its navigation order, category names and dropdown structure.
    4. Note how its collections group products.
    5. Open its about or brand story page.
    6. Check its footer links and whether it offers an email opt-in.
    7. Check its Facebook and Instagram pages and how it posts there.
    8. Copy the layout and structure, not the colors or design details.

Mobile and review checks

1 min
  • Review each key landing page in both views before you decide it is fine. "Both views" means the desktop screen and the phone screen.
    1. Load the page on desktop. Confirm the headline and the main product image or intro text are visible without scrolling.
    2. Switch to the mobile view. Confirm the image does not push the title or first paragraph far down the screen.
    3. Check for pop-ups. No pop-up, interstitial or chat bubble may cover a significant part of the screen in the first 30 seconds. A pop-up is a box that appears over the page. An interstitial is a full-screen page shown before the content. A chat bubble is a chat button floating on the screen.
    4. Check the text. Split long text into chunks with subheadings. Chunks are small blocks of text. Use short paragraphs of two to three lines, and a readable font size of 18.
    5. Note any issue, fix it, and recheck both views.
  • Most customers shop on mobile. Check that the homepage USP, the offer and the reviews appear on phone screens as clearly as on desktop.
  • Check the presentation of your copy on mobile before judging its persuasive power. Copy means the words on the page. Persuasive power means how convincing they are.
  • Long walls of text on the product page are hard to read on mobile. Compress the product description into short sections that buyers can scan. Scanning means quickly looking over the page to find what matters.
