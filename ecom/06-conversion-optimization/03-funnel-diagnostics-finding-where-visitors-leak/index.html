Funnel diagnostics: finding where visitors leak

Section 6.3 · 3 of 22 in this chapter · 44 min read

In short

  • Check traffic first, then the offer, then the web page. The page usually matters least.
  • Conversion rate means the share of visitors who buy. A low number points to a specific step, so measure each step.
  • A high click rate with no sales usually means the store is the problem, not the ad.
  • Don't decide a product is bad until traffic, offer and page have all been checked.
  • If ads get clicks but no sales, do not blame the ads first. Check the link, the offer and the store.
More key points
  • Test the offer (price, bundles, deals) before you redesign the store.
  • Check the add-to-cart rate (the share of visitors who put an item in the cart). Below 4% means the offer or product page needs work.
  • Before you switch off ads after $300 spent with no sales, go through the checklist.
  • A store has four steps: visit, add to cart, start checkout, buy. Check each step by itself to find the weakest one.
  • 6% or more of visitors should add to cart from the landing page. If not, fix that page first.
  • You need 150 to 200 real visitors before you judge a store's funnel. Fewer is too little data.
  • Diagnose in order: traffic quality, then competitors, then the website. Never skip ahead.

Diagnosis rules: traffic, offer, page, and the ad-to-store split

3 min
  • Conversion means a visitor buys. The conversion rate is the share of visitors who buy.
  • Conversion work starts in one of two ways:
    • Customers report a problem.
    • Conversion falls below target.
  • This section covers the second case, when no direct feedback exists.
  • Diagnose in this order:
    1. Traffic: who visits and where they come from.
    2. Offer: what you sell and the deal you make.
    3. Web page: how the page looks and reads.
  • The page usually has the smallest effect on conversion chance.
  • So start with the traffic source, not the page layout.
  • Under the 40-40-20 rule (a rough split of what drives sales):
    • About 40% depends on traffic targeting: who is sent to the site and where they land.
    • About 40% depends on offer quality compared with competitors.
    • About 20% depends on how the offer is presented on the page.
  • A product that gets traffic but no sales is not automatically bad.
  • Do not conclude that nobody wants it, or that the product is wrong, until traffic, offer and page have been checked.
  • Ads bring visitors. The store has to turn them into buyers.
  • High CTR (click-through rate: the share of people who see an ad and click it) with no sales points to a store problem.
  • Store problems include price, offer or page content.
  • Store changes belong in the offer, the pages and the checkout.
  • Basic ad creatives (the pictures or videos in the ad) can still catch attention and bring clicks.
  • A weak store will still fail to turn those clicks into sales.
  • These weak-store problems include a poor product description, a poor offer or poor packaging.
  • Once ads already bring clicks, improving the store is often the bigger lever.
  • When ad numbers are strong but the store makes no sales, the traffic is already the right kind.
  • So the work goes into the store.
  • If ads perform well (good CTR and traffic) but sales are missing:
    • Improve the website, especially the offer.
    • Do not start with the ads.
  • Read the CTR like this:
    • Low CTR means the creative is the problem. Test different creatives first.
    • High CTR but no sales means the landing page is the problem. Work on the page and offer, not the ad.
  • A good CPC (cost per click: what you pay for each click) with weak sales points to a conversion problem on the site.
  • Ads only deliver the right visitors, at the right time and price.
  • The conversion itself happens on the website.
  • If search terms, bids and clicks all look fine but sales stay low:
    • The cause is the offer, the competitive position, or the site's conversion rate.
    • The cause is not the ad settings.
    • No amount of ad management fixes this.
    • Review market position, offer and site.
  • Audit each customer step from first ad to repeat purchase, like a store consultant audits a shop:
    • The ad.
    • The entry point.
    • Product placement.
    • The checkout upsell (an extra offer shown at checkout).
    • The follow-up emails.
  • Test each step on its own.
  • A stalled business usually has a fault in one of these steps.
  • Measure the conversion rate of each step separately. Then you know which step needs repair.
  • A low opt-in rate (few people give their email) points to the squeeze or pre-frame.
  • A low buyer rate points to the offer.
  • Squeeze and pre-frame here mean the early pages that come before the main offer.
  • Diagnose before changing anything.
  • Make every step from landing on the store to a paid order as easy as possible:
    • Reach the site.
    • Find the product.
    • Add it to the cart.
    • Pay.
  • Remove anything that gives the visitor a reason to stop or leave.
  • Improve conversion before adding traffic.
  • Check product pages, pricing, shipping and abandoned carts first (carts left behind without buying).
  • Only then increase the number of visitors.

Funnel benchmarks by stage

3 min
  • A funnel is the chain of steps from first visit to paid order.
  • The store funnel has four stages:
    • LP: landing page visit (the first page a visitor sees).
    • ATC: add to cart.
    • IC: checkout start (initiate checkout).
    • PUR: purchase.
  • Each stage rate is measured against the stage before it.
  • This shows the exact drop-off point.
  • Each funnel stage has its own problems and its own fixes.
  • These are different issues:
    • Low add-to-cart rate.
    • Drop-off between add-to-cart and checkout.
    • Drop-off between checkout and purchase.
  • Diagnose each stage separately. Do not treat the funnel as one problem.
  • Shopify's store analytics (the reports in Shopify, the online store software) show these stages:
    • Added to cart.
    • Reached checkout.
    • Purchased.
    • Also shown: sessions (one visit each) and visitors.
  • Check which stage loses the most visitors before changing the overall conversion rate.
  • The final rate alone does not show where the drop happens.

  • Optimisation should reduce this drop.
  • Target check: 16 add-to-carts and 3 purchases is significantly below target.
  • Aim for at least 6 purchases from 16 add-to-carts.
  • That is roughly 37% to 40% checkout conversion.
  • Checkout to purchase: at least 50%.
  • Aim for about a 50% drop-off between each stage of the checkout flow.
  • A much larger drop-off signals a problem to troubleshoot.
  • Landing page conversion of about 3% to 4% was used as a reference point when reviewing ad performance.
  • Compare CTR and landing page conversion together.
  • This shows whether the ad or the page is limiting sales.
  • A 2.5% conversion rate is the working benchmark for qualified traffic (visitors who are likely buyers). (2023 and 2024 sources)
  • A conversion rate of 0.2% is far below the working target of about 2.5%.
  • The source used that gap as the goal to close within four weeks.
  • Conversion of about 2% from quality traffic is the threshold for running the next check.
  • If quality traffic is below about 2%, move on to a usability test (a test where real people try to use the site).
  • Click-to-sale expectation. Sources differ:
    • Older minimum: 1,835 clicks should give at least 15 sales, about 0.8%.
    • If the result is significantly lower, the problem is the store or the product presentation, not the traffic source.
    • Current working benchmark: clicks ÷ 40 = expected sales at a 2.5% conversion rate.
    • For 1,835 clicks, that is about 46 sales.
    • If actual sales are lower, compare the product page with competitors.
    • Use clicks ÷ 40 as the working expectation.
    • Treat 15 sales as the older, lower floor.

CTR, CPC and traffic signals

2 min
  • CTR below 1%: the creatives are not connecting with the audience.
  • Test entirely new video or image creatives.
  • CTR of 1.5% to 2%: pre-click performance (how the ad does before the click) is good.
  • If there are still no sales, the issue is post-click (what happens on the website after the click), not the ads.
  • CTR above 3% with a conversion rate of 0.5% to 0.8% on a 70 EUR product signalled weak demand or weak copy. (case in the kill section)
  • CTR above 3%, sometimes 4%, with a conversion rate of about 0.8% after a week was the pattern in a 60 EUR product case.
  • A strong hook rate (the share of viewers who keep watching the first seconds of a video ad) and strong CTR, but low purchases, means the problem sits in the offer or landing page.
  • Possible causes:
    • The ad may not clearly sell the product, or may mislead.
    • The product page may block ordering.
    • Unexpected fees may appear.
    • The page may lack trust.
    • The price may come as a shock.
  • A high CTR with a low CPC, many visitors and no sales usually means visitors are curious but not convinced.
  • Likely causes: the offer, the price, the copy (the written sales text), the store's trust level, or the product itself.
  • Very high CTR with no sales can point to a checkout or offer problem, not an ad problem.
  • Test the checkout yourself and confirm it completes before judging the ad.
  • If most visitors leave within 9 to 10 seconds, the traffic is probably not the right kind.
  • Check the traffic source and campaign settings before changing the store itself. (2025)
  • Sample sizes: check these numbers before you judge a result.

Finding the stage that lost performance

2 min
  • If conversion drops by 35%, check these metrics in order:
    • Did cost per link click rise?
    • Did CPM (cost per 1,000 ad views) rise?
    • Did CTR fall?
    • Did the add-to-cart percentage fall?
    • Did the initiate-checkout percentage fall?
  • Cost metrics point to problems at the top of the funnel (early steps).
  • Percentage metrics point to website conversion.
  • Each metric reveals which stage lost performance.
  • If sales or traffic dropped:
    1. Check the Google Ads numbers for changes in impressions (how often your ad was shown), clicks and the keywords showing.
    2. Check Google Analytics (Google's visitor reports) for changes in traffic and bounce rate (the share of visitors who leave after one page) on key pages.
    3. Look for limited products or feed warnings (errors in the product data sent to Google) that could be restricting ads.
    4. If the dip continues into the next few days, review all ads and products more closely.
  • Shopify sales funnel, step by step:
    1. Open the Shopify analytics and look at the number of sessions.
    2. Compare the number of add-to-cart actions with the number of sessions.
    3. Compare the number of checkouts started with the number of add-to-cart actions.
    4. Compare the number of completed checkouts with the checkouts started. Note the number of abandoned carts.
    5. Find the step where most visitors leave. Focus the fixes there.
  • Each funnel metric points to a specific stage.
  • So the funnel is faster to diagnose than total numbers.
  • Example: a store spent $50 per day on Facebook ads and got good traffic but no sales.
  • The spend showed the website was the problem.
  • The founder rebuilt the site. The test revealed the bottleneck (the step that slows everything) that was not visible before.
  • When a product gets clicks but no sales, diagnose in this order:
    1. Check how many days the ads have been running before judging results.
    2. Check the offer.
    3. Check the landing page.
    4. Check the checkout process for issues.
    5. Check creative performance using CTR and CPC in the Facebook ads account.
    6. In Shopify analytics, check:
      • The add-to-cart percentage.
      • The initiate-checkout percentage.
      • The visitor count. Low traffic can explain weak results in a new campaign.
  • Seasonality (sales that rise and fall with the calendar):
    • A two-month-old store saw traffic rise.
    • Google cost per click fell and add-to-carts increased.
    • But conversion did not rise with them.
    • Sales peaked during Black Friday, Cyber Monday and early Christmas shopping.
    • Then they dropped as buyers spent their budgets.
    • Wait a couple of weeks to see where sales level out.
    • In Google Ads, check whether keywords have become less relevant. Falling costs can also mean weaker keyword matches.
  • Holiday weekend check:
    1. Check the conversion rate for the product against its normal baseline (its usual number) before blaming a sales weekend.
    2. Check the traffic first. Confirm the keywords and products getting impressions and clicks.
    3. Open the store as a buyer on mobile and desktop and walk through the purchase.
    4. Fix any step that interrupts the path from click to purchase.
    5. Optimise the product pages before increasing ad spend.
  • Compare conversion results to a baseline, not to a single weekend.
  • If normal conversion was already low, the problem is more likely traffic or the offer than the holiday.

Traffic or store: attribution checks

2 min
  • Attribution means working out which part caused the result.
  • Check the traffic before the store.
  • Traffic quality comes before design. Visitors who are not relevant to the product will not buy, however many there are.
  • Check audience and keyword relevance before changing the page.
  • A store can be well optimised and still sell to the wrong visitors.
  • Check that keywords reach buyers first. Then optimise the store for conversions.
  • Example: a store had about 15 to 20 clicks per day on an $11 daily budget for about six weeks.
  • It had roughly 1,000 visitors and no sales.
  • Actions taken:
    • Check keywords for buying intent (words that show the person wants to buy).
    • Add negative keywords.
    • Lower the max CPC (the highest price you pay per click) on products that do not produce results.
    • Then work through the conversion checks step by step.
  • The traffic was enough. So the problem was conversion and traffic quality.
  • A store with many visitors but few orders most likely has a problem on the site itself, not in the traffic.
  • Review the site for conversion problems.
  • Exclude non-customer traffic before judging conversion. Examples: suppliers and virtual assistants.
  • They skew the results.
  • If visitors leave a product page within seconds, the traffic is usually the problem, not the page.
  • Better design or copy will not produce sales until the visitors who arrive match what the store sells.
  • Traffic checks, in order, for a store with no sales after real traffic:
    1. Check the number of unique visitors (each person counted once). About 200 is too small to judge. About 2,000 visitors with no sales means something needs investigating.
    2. Check traffic quality by country (only countries you sell in) and by search term. Add negative keywords for broad or irrelevant searches.
    3. If qualified visitors reach relevant product pages and still do not buy, focus on on-site conversion (what happens on your site).
  • Bounce and session-time check:
    • One store had an average session of 26 seconds.
    • Its bounce rate was 83.94%.
    • Compare these numbers for mobile and desktop in Google Analytics.
    • Slow mobile loading often causes most of the bounce.
  • Traffic well targeted but checked on one device: when most conversions come from one device, adjust bids by device. (older, 2024 framing applies to current account)
  • If a store has had traffic for many months without sales:
    • The cause is usually a front-end problem driving visitors away. (The front end is what the visitor sees.)
    • Have an experienced reviewer check the live store as a first-time buyer.
    • Look for usability and trust problems.
    • Do this before spending more on traffic.
  • The 40-40-20 split puts traffic first.
  • Read the search terms or audience settings that send visitors to the page before editing the page.

Diagnosis tree: low add-to-cart

3 min
  • Work the funnel top to bottom.
  • Check the LP-to-ATC rate first.
  • Only when it is healthy, check ATC-to-IC.
  • Only when IC is healthy, check IC-to-PUR.
    1. LP to ATC below 6%: work on the offer, images, branding and trust, copywriting, friction (anything that slows or annoys) and speed.
    2. ATC to IC below 25% to 50%: work on audience quality, trust, shipping prices, taxes, address issues and currency.
    3. IC to PUR below 50%: work on shipping prices, currency, payment processors (the companies that take the payment) and taxes.
  • If visitors do not add products to the cart, check these causes in order:
    1. Bad offer.
    2. Poor product images.
    3. Poor branding and weak trust factor.
    4. Poor copywriting.
    5. Too much friction on the page.
    6. Speed-related issues.
  • When people do not add to cart, the usual causes are:
    • An overly complex page.
    • A price that is too high.
    • Unexpected fees.
    • Shipping cost shock.
    • A lack of trust.
    • A functional problem on the page (something that does not work).
  • Simplify the page and remove surprises before lowering the price.
  • A product that converts well at checkout but has low add-to-carts usually has a funnel issue.
  • Look at the product page or pricing.
  • Check the funnel before deciding the product is weak.
  • Product detail page (PDP) check:
    1. Check whether the PDP (the page for one product) converts visitors at a reasonable rate.
    2. If conversion is weak, optimise the PDP first.
    3. If conversion is still weak, check whether the landing page or the ad creative is the problem.
    4. Keep testing until a winner appears. Then scale it harder (spend more on it).
  • Visitors who reach a product page but never add to cart point to a page that is not driving the main action.
  • The page should focus on the add-to-cart button.
  • Visitors who wander to the home page or collection pages may be distracted.
  • Diagnosing a product page where many visitors do nothing:
    1. Check how long visitors stay on the product page and whether they scroll.
    2. Load the page on mobile, including a slow connection (for example 3G, an older mobile network), to see whether it appears quickly.
    3. Check page speed. Visitors who see a blank screen or wait for loading often leave before the page appears.
    4. Compare the result with the expected conversion rate on the actual number of visitors who reached the product page.
  • Time on page: if visitors stay under 15 seconds and do not add to cart, the messaging is not compelling.
  • Google Ads, when ads get clicks but few add-to-carts:
    1. In Google Ads, open the alpha and beta campaigns and go to the Keywords view. (2023)
    2. Check which keywords triggered the clicks and whether they show buying intent.
    3. Add vague or unrelated terms as negative keywords. This sends budget to buying terms.
    4. Compare the product page with the top two or three competitor pages side by side. Check whether they make a stronger offer.
  • A product with clicks and add-to-carts but no sales: the store and pricing are the first suspects.
  • Diagnosing add-to-carts without purchases:
    1. Confirm add-to-carts are happening but no sales follow.
    2. Place a test order to check that the checkout button and checkout flow work.
    3. If checkout works, review the offer against competitors' offers.
    • A broken checkout button alone can stop all sales, even when add-to-carts are high.
  • Consider a full-page review using the six factors in the page-scoring section below.

Diagnosis tree: low checkout completion

3 min
  • Checkout drop-off after add-to-cart: first check functional and technical issues (things that break).
    1. Is the price displaying correctly?
    2. Is the currency correct?
    3. Are unexpected fees appearing at checkout?
    4. Is the shipping cost visible before checkout?
    5. Is there a payment method issue?
  • Shipping cost shock is the main reason for the typical drop.
  • Use wording like free tracked shipping instead of standard shipping. It sounds more attractive and trustworthy.
  • Some people add to cart only to see the final price.
  • If the offer is not shown clearly (for example, a free gift revealed only after adding to cart), simplify:
    • Reduce the price by an equal amount.
    • Show the offer on the product page.
  • If add-to-cart is high but purchases are low, check in this order:
    1. The shipping price shown.
    2. The offer.
    3. The tax settings.
    4. Whether checkout is enabled for the visitor's country.
  • These are the most common causes of drop-off after add-to-cart.
  • If ATC is strong but few visitors start checkout, work through these causes in order:
    1. Audience quality: if the audience is weak, test the offer again.
    2. Trust factor.
    3. Shipping prices.
    4. Taxes.
    5. Address-related issues.
    6. Currency issues.
  • If ATC and IC are good but few visitors complete the purchase, check in order:
    1. Shipping prices.
    2. Currency.
    3. Payment processors.
    4. Taxes.
  • Shipping prices, taxes and currency can cause drop-off both at checkout start and at the purchase step.
  • Check them at both stages.
  • When a store gets clicks but no sales, check in order:
    1. Check shipping options and whether shipping is charged.
    2. Check that every targeted market and country is enabled in Shopify Markets (Shopify's tool for selling in several countries).
    • Customers from countries that are not enabled cannot pick their country at checkout.
    • They leave the cart.
    1. Test payment providers. Place a test order with a 95% discount code (a code that cuts the price by 95%, so the test costs little) on PayPal, Shopify Payments and Stripe.
    • If a provider fails, uninstall it and reinstall it.
  • If checkout is poor, check the store's traffic and ads for the same issues.
  • If traffic is high but conversion is zero over two months:
    1. First, test checkout with a $1 product on desktop and mobile.
    2. Second, check abandoned carts immediately.
    3. Third, review traffic source keywords for quality.
  • Debugging a conversion problem, for example 1,835 clicks with fewer than one sale:
    1. Create a $1 test product and verify checkout works on desktop and mobile.
    2. Add PayPal, Amazon Payments, Klarna and Affirm financing options (ways to pay in parts).
    3. Analyse Google Ads data: which keywords and products receive clicks?
    4. Find unrelated keywords and add them as negatives.
    5. Find products with strong competitor pricing and adjust bids.
    6. Check for checkout errors or technical issues on both devices.
  • Shoppers who reach checkout but do not buy:
    • Audit the website and compare it with established brands in the niche (your small market).
    • Copy the structure and presentation of those brands. Do not guess.
  • A store solution such as Debutify (an add-on for Shopify stores) can support conversion at the final step.
  • Pair it with a site audit to find the friction points.
  • Checkout drop-off test: about 50% drop-off between each stage is the expected range.
  • A much larger drop-off indicates a problem.
  • Abandoned checkouts: check them under Orders in the Shopify admin (the back office). Call those customers for feedback.
  • Mobile bounce: a bounce problem on mobile often hides a checkout or load-speed problem.

Diagnosis tree: high CTR and no sales

6 min

Some terms first:

  • CTR (click-through rate) is the share of people who see your ad and click it.
  • A conversion is a completed sale.
  • A funnel is the path from ad click to purchase.

Use this check when a campaign performs badly or makes zero sales:

  1. Check spend per ad. Look inside each ad set (a group of ads that share one audience and one budget).
    • Find the single ad or video that takes most of the budget.
    • If one creative (one ad video or image) takes 80% or more of the ad set budget and performs badly (no sales, low CTR, high CPC), turn that one ad off.
    • CPC means cost per click: what you pay for each click.
    • Let the other creatives spend another full break-even amount (the spend at which sales just cover the cost, so you make neither profit nor loss), so they compete on equal terms.
  2. Check all creatives. If every creative in the ad set performs badly, even with a balanced budget, check their CTR.
    • If CTR is below 1%, the creatives do not connect with the audience.
    • Test entirely new video or image creatives.
  3. Check before and after the click. If CTR is 1.5% to 2% (good ad performance) but there are no sales, the problem is after the click.
    • "After the click" means the website funnel.
    • The problem is the store, not the ads.
  4. Test the checkout. Checkout is the set of payment steps.
    • Run a $1 test purchase from each targeted country.
    • Check the checkout flow and shipping prices.
    • Look for surprise charges at the last step.
    • Check that Shopify Markets (Shopify's tool for selling in several countries) is set up and switched on in Settings.
  5. Check shipping and payment.
    • Verify shipping prices for each country.
    • Verify that the payment processor (the company that handles card payments) works.
    • Test free shipping against paid shipping to see which sells more.
    • Make sure no hidden charges appear between the product page and checkout.
  6. Test the offer. If ads bring traffic (good CTR and CPC) but no sales, the offer may lack appeal.
    • Test proven offers: BOGO (buy one, get one free), buy 2 get 1 free, buy 2 get 50% off, free gift with purchase, or tiered bundles (packs at several sizes: single, duo, trio).
  7. Build trust.
    • Show customer reviews (text and video) prominently.
    • Keep the website to three to four colours at most: black, white and one brand colour.
    • Consistent design makes a site look more trustworthy.

  • Check the landing URL (the web address the ad opens) first.

  • Confirm the ad opens the correct product or collection page (a group of products).

  • A wrong link can look like a conversion problem.

  • Diagnosing a high-CTR, low-conversion store, in this order:

    1. Confirm the ad link opens the correct product or collection page.
    2. Check the add-to-cart rate in Shopify.
      • If it is near zero while CTR is high, treat the offer as the first suspect.
    3. Test price, bundles and offers before changing the design.
    4. Once sales come in, run a split test (two versions shown to different visitors, to see which sells more).
      • Send part of the traffic to the standard landing page.
      • Send another campaign to a page-builder version (a version made with a page-building tool).
    5. Choose the version that sells more before rolling it out.

  • A high CTR with almost no add-to-cart actions means the ad works. Shoppers just do not find the store offer convincing.

  • Traffic is arriving, so the problem sits in the store.

  • When ads have a strong CTR but no sales, the offer is usually the cause (a rough estimate of about 90%).

  • Store design explains only about 10%.

  • Test price, bundles and offers before redesigning the store.

  • If CTR is high and add-to-cart is near zero, the problem is almost always the store, not the ads.

  • Fix the store before changing the ads.

  • Diagnosing a product with high CTR, low CPC, lots of visitors and no sales:

    1. Check the add-to-cart rate and the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
    2. If add-to-cart is below 4%, review the offer, the price, the copy (the sales text) and whether the product page looks trustworthy.
    3. If add-to-cart is fine but conversion is low, check shipping cost (free or not). Then run a test order through the whole funnel.
    4. Only then decide whether the product itself is the issue.

  • Example with numbers: a 6% add-to-cart rate and a 1% conversion rate.

  • The problem sits in the offer or shipping, so check those first.

  • Also run a test order through the funnel to confirm checkout works.

  • Case, French market:

    • A seller tested a product. It got a high CTR and low CPC, with many visitors but no sales.
    • The add-to-cart rate was 2.5%. That is far below the 4% benchmark (a standard target).
    • The seller lowered the price and tried a second offer. Neither changed the result, so the ads were stopped.
    • The lesson: check the offer, price, copy and page trust signals (signs the page can be trusted, such as reviews) against the add-to-cart benchmark.
    • Do this before deciding the product fails.
    • Competitors scaling the same product in that market is normal.

  • Ads that perform but do not sell need store changes, not ad changes.

  • Change the store's offer, pages and checkout.

  • Checklist before switching off ads after $300 spent with no sales:

    1. Improve the offer.
    2. Improve pricing.
    3. Improve copywriting.
    4. Try another product name.
    5. Improve the product description.
    6. Activate markets so each targeted country sees prices in its own currency.
    7. Check that payment providers work and that everything is set up correctly.

  • Split test product pages (two or more pages compared side by side):

    1. Confirm CTR and CPC are on target.
    2. If there are no sales, change the store first.
    3. Run the same product to different product pages with different offers, bundles and copy.
    4. Compare which pages produce sales.
    5. Keep the winning page and test the next variation.

  • A high CTR with no sales means the store is the problem, not the ads.

  • Change the store design, offer, bundle, pricing or copy.

  • Do not rely on the first version of the store.

  • Running ads to only one product page with one offer, then judging the product, is a mistake.

  • Test several offers and bundles before concluding it fails.

  • Strong hook rate (the share of viewers who keep watching past the first seconds) and CTR, but few purchases: see the hook-rate note in the signals section.

  • Basic ad creatives can still drive traffic while a weak store fails to convert.

  • See the attribution section (which ad gets credit for a sale).

  • If ads perform but the product page is too busy, reduce it to one clear decision that is largely made for the buyer. (2025-26)

  • Product page with clicks but no sales (high price): improve the product page before removing the product.

  • Add education, reassurance, extra information or a bonus the competitor does not offer.

  • Product page friction (anything that slows or blocks the buyer): do not add friction.

  • Reduce it at every step.

  • Search terms (the words people type into Google) and bids (the price you offer per click) are all in order, but sales stay low.

  • Review the offer and the market before changing ads.

  • A product that converts poorly on a new page should be checked against the funnel before the product is blamed.

  • Ads that get clicks but no sales in a targeted market should be checked for market-level issues before changing the product.

  • Very high CTR with no sales on one ad: see the high-CTR framework above.

  • Product page with a TikTok audience: if add-to-cart is high but purchases are low, audience quality (how well the people reached fit the product) is often the cause.

  • Remove age groups that do not fit the product, and test stronger offers.

  • Example: removing the 18 to 24 group improved conversion, even though add-to-cart fell. (2025-26)

Google Shopping and Google Analytics troubleshooting

6 min
  • Google Shopping means product ads shown in Google search.
  • Google Analytics is a free tool that tracks what visitors do on your site.

Google Shopping: clicks without sales

  • If Google Shopping underperforms after three months:

    1. Go to Google Ads (Google's ad tool).
    2. Run a report for the past three months.
    3. Identify products with many impressions (times your ad was shown) but few clicks.
    4. Focus the ad budget on the 20 to 30 products already getting traction.
    5. Pause or reduce spend on products with no impressions.
    6. Optimise landing pages for high-impression products to increase conversion rate.
    7. Build up initial sales volume and margin (profit per sale) before expanding to more products.

  • If the daily budget is not fully spent:

    1. Increase the product-level CPC (the price you offer per click for each product) step by step.
      • Start at the baseline (for example 50 cents).
      • Raise it to 75 cents, then to $1.
    2. Run each increase for several days.
    3. Continue until the daily budget is fully spent each day.
    4. Once all products show, there is enough data to identify winners and optimise.
    5. Add negative keywords (words that stop your ad from showing) to prevent wasting money on non-relevant searches.

  • If impressions are high but clicks are low (for example 900 impressions, 4 clicks, 90% search impression share, meaning how often your ad showed out of the searches it could have shown on):

    1. Check the product feed (the product list you send to Google) in Google Merchant Center or Microsoft Merchant Center (the tools that hold this list).
    2. Look for unapproved items, errors or warnings.
    3. Clean up feed issues: missing fields (empty information boxes) and policy violations (breaking the platform's rules).
    4. Resubmit the feed.
    5. On Microsoft, try removing some negative keywords. The impact differs from Google.
    6. Test a slightly higher CPC for a few days to improve ad position (where your ad appears on the page).

  • To find out why Google Shopping traffic does not convert (2020-22 framing, same method):

    1. In Google Ads, pull a keyword report for the Shopping campaigns. See which keywords get most of the clicks.
    2. Flag irrelevant or too-broad keywords. Add them as negatives (blocked search words). Use exact match (blocking only that exact wording) for generic terms, so budget stops going to them.
    3. Keep budget on quality keywords: brand names, product names and SKU numbers (product codes).
    4. Find which products take most of the budget on quality keywords, and where those clicks land on your store.
    5. Open the product page on one side of a split screen. Search for the same product on Google on the other side.
    6. Open the top two or three competitor sites. Ask why a shopper would choose them over your store, looking at the offer and not only the price.
    7. Improve the product pages that receive quality traffic, so the offer beats competitors, not just the visuals or price.

  • Product gets clicks but no sales on Google Shopping: audit competitor product pages (look closely at rival pages).

    1. Search the product on Google Shopping as a shopper would.
    2. Open the other stores selling the same item.
    3. Note anything their product page does more persuasively than yours. For example: a clearer offer, trust elements, imagery or content.
    4. Apply the changes that could realistically raise conversion on your own page.

  • Traffic quality for Google Shopping: check which search terms generate clicks to the page under review.

  • Judge whether they come from buyers ready to purchase or from people still researching.

  • Shopping and product-level checks, with a full 2019-era procedure:

    1. Run a test purchase on desktop and mobile after any app or code change. Changes can break one variant (one version of a page).
    2. In Google Ads, find which products are getting clicks and which search terms triggered them.
    3. Add irrelevant search terms as negative keywords.
    4. For relevant keywords, search the product on Google Shopping. Compare competitors' prices, shipping speed, policies, bonus gifts and review counts.
    5. Use that data to improve your own product pages and add the conversion triggers competitors use.
    6. Make sure every major payment method is offered and easy to use.
    7. In Google Analytics, review the top exit pages (where visitors leave the site most) and the bounce rate (the share of visitors who leave after one page). Look for reasons visitors leave.
    8. Check that the traffic reaching the store is relevant to the products.

  • Google Ads bids by device (2024): use this when most sales come from one device (phone or computer).

    1. Open the alpha and beta campaigns (the two campaigns with those names) in Google Ads.
    2. Check past conversions (sales) by device. Mobile means phones. Desktop means computers.
    3. If most budget goes to mobile but sales come from desktop, lower the mobile bid.
    4. Example: a 60 cent mobile CPC can be cut by 50%, to 30 cents. This shifts traffic toward desktop users, who buy.

  • Conversion of Google Ads products (2024 procedure):

    1. Identify product pages receiving qualified clicks (clicks from people likely to buy) from Google Ads through the correct keywords.
    2. Calculate expected sales: divide clicks by 40. That gives expected sales at the 2.5% benchmark, since 40 clicks should produce 1 sale.
    3. If actual sales are lower, visit competitor stores and pull up the same products side by side.
    4. Compare product page layout, images, description, reviews, pricing and warranty information.
    5. Identify visual or functional differences that favour the competitor.
    6. Update the product page to match or exceed competitor pages.

  • Google Ads conversion check (2023 procedure):

    1. Monitor keyword quality by checking which keywords trigger your ads.
    2. Review conversion rates on the ads. Target a minimum of 2.5% from qualified traffic.
    3. If a single product has received 200 to 300 clicks with zero sales, compare the store with the top 3 to 5 competitor stores.
    4. Document differences in product presentation, pricing, descriptions or additional features.
    5. Implement improvements based on the competitor analysis.

  • Products with impressions and clicks but no sales, when traffic itself is not the problem:

    • Work on the store page and the conversion setup (how the sale process is built) of those products.
    • Do this before cutting them from the ads.

  • If a single product gets more than 100 clicks from relevant search terms and still has no sale:

    • Optimise that product page before changing campaigns.
    • Do this only after the negatives (blocked search words) are cleaned up and the product has clear relevant traffic.

  • Clicks with no sales across Google Ads over three weeks: check whether visitors reach checkout at all.

Google Analytics checks

  • Google Analytics Insights flags top landing pages (the first pages visitors open) whose e-commerce conversion rate is below the site average.

  • Example: below 0.46% when that is the site average.

  • Use this flag to decide which product or landing page to fix first.

  • Then confirm the problem with the page's conversion rate column.

  • Compare product pages with each other over time, as traffic builds up:

    1. Collect Google Analytics data for product pages over time.
    2. Compare pages using average time on page (how long visitors stay) and bounce rate.
    3. Identify pages where visitors stay longer (low bounce rate, high time on page).
    4. Study those high-performing pages to understand which design and content elements work.
    5. Find pages with high bounce rates (visitors leave immediately).
    6. Review those pages for errors, unclear messaging, design issues or missing elements that cause quick exits.

  • Behaviour data for a landing page:

    • Read the bounce rate, time on site, time on page and exit rate (the share of visitors who leave from that page).
    • Do this for the page used as a landing page.

  • Read the page with Shopify's funnel report. It shows where add-to-cart, checkout start and purchase drop off.

  • Then return to the Google Analytics figures.

Scoring the page: six conversion factors

3 min

Full-page diagnosis for a low-converting page:

  1. Review traffic targeting. List the search terms or audience settings that send visitors to the page.
    • Judge whether they reach people near the top of the funnel (just becoming aware) or the bottom (ready to buy).
  2. Check the traffic-to-page match. Confirm the landing page fits the search intent (what the searcher wants) and the customer awareness stage of the visitors.
  3. Check follow-up. Confirm that retargeting (showing ads again to people who visited before) and email exist.
    • Retargeting should use varied ads, not one dynamic product ad (an ad that shows the products a visitor viewed) for 30 days.
  4. Review the offer. Confirm it is a CURV offer (a named offer model), compare it with competitor offers, and check that it matches what visitors are looking for.
  5. Test the page on several devices. Test on desktop, mobile and tablet, and on several connections (cellular data and home Wi-Fi). Check page speed.
  6. Read behaviour data in Google Analytics: bounce rate, time on site, time on page and exit rate for the landing page.
  7. Score the page against the six e-commerce conversion factors: value proposition (the reason to buy, compared with the price), relevance, clarity, anxiety, distraction and urgency.
  8. Turn each weakness into a single test. Change one element, then measure whether the conversion rate improves.
  • The six factors apply to any page meant to drive an action.
  • That includes product pages and opt-in pages with a form (a page where visitors leave their email).
Factor Question to ask
Value proposition How well does the page explain the product's value, including the marketing offer, and what is the main perceived benefit for the customer?
Relevance How closely does the content match what visitors expect, and how well does the value proposition address their primary problem?
Clarity Does the page explain the value proposition, the offer and the calls to action clearly, with a logical sequence and enough white space?
Anxiety What is missing or unclear that could make a buyer feel uneasy about completing the purchase?
Distraction What does the visitor see first, and does anything compete with or pull attention away from the goal?
Urgency What convinces the visitor to act now, through incentives, the offer itself, tone or message?
  • Value proposition: the visitor must see enough value for the price.
    • The page should explain the product's pros and cons, the marketing offer and the main benefit.
    • It must do this clearly enough that the price feels justified.
  • Relevance: lead with the main problem the visitor wants solved.
    • Show the product as the solution through its main promise.
    • Naming the problem early keeps visitors on the page.
  • Clarity: the page must follow a clear sequence.
    • Make calls to action (buttons or prompts to act) easy to find and identify.
    • Leave plenty of white space (empty room around content).
    • A cluttered page with too much text or too many elements lowers conversion, even when the copy is good.
  • Distraction: the first thing a visitor sees must support the goal.
    • Intrusive pop-ups and stacked widgets (chat, messenger, wishlist) pull attention from the main call to action.
    • So product pages should show very little beyond what is needed to buy.
  • Split test the design after sales come in (see the high-CTR framework). Test one variation at a time.
  • Improve the product detail page before testing the ad. The product page is the lever for product-level conversion.

When to kill a product

3 min

"Kill" means stop selling the product.

  • Do not stay emotionally attached to a product because it makes some sales.

  • If it is unprofitable, continuing to spend in hope of profit wastes money.

  • A product with good CTR but very low conversion is first a problem with the offer or market.

  • That is not yet a reason to stop.

  • Try another market, a new offer or a new marketing angle (a new way of selling it) before killing the product.

  • Only kill a product when the loss reaches about $200 to $300.

  • A product at break-even still deserves more offers, angles or markets.

  • Before abandoning a product after the first day or two of ads, check two things.

    • Check that product validation (proof there is real demand) was sound.
    • Check that each funnel stage has been optimised. The weakness is often in the store, not the product.

  • Product kill checklist after $300 spent with no sales: it is in the high-CTR section. Complete it before switching ads off.

  • If the product has been tested with all elements optimised and still does not convert after two testing rounds, switch products.

  • Do not judge a product before checking:

    • The days ads have run,
    • The offer,
    • The landing page,
    • The checkout,
    • and CTR/CPC in the ad account.

  • Case, 70 EUR product (older, 2019-22 framing not applicable; current sources):

    • A product priced at 70 EUR was tested for 14 days in January.
    • It made 18 sales with no recorded returns. Customers said they were happy with it.
    • Conversion stayed around 0.8%.
    • The product was later relaunched with a new, better offer and new creatives.
    • CTR was above 3%, but conversion was about 0.5% on the first day.
    • Low conversion at this price points to weak demand, weak copy or an unconvincing offer. The product page should be reviewed.
    • A conversion rate around 1.5% could still be profitable at this price if the margin (profit per sale) is good.

  • For a 70 EUR product, a conversion rate of 0.5% to 0.8% signalled weak demand or weak copy, despite CTR above 3%.

  • A conversion rate around 1.5% can still be profitable if the margin is good.

  • Case, 60 EUR product in one market: CTR was over 3% and sometimes 4%, but conversion after a week was 0.8%.

  • The product was at break-even at about 60 euros.

  • The mentor would keep testing rather than kill a product at break-even.

  • Case, 60 EUR product in one market, full sequence:

    • The product sold at around 60 euros. Ad metrics were strong (CTR above 3%, sometimes 4%).
    • Conversion was about 0.8% after one week.
    • About 400 euros had been spent, and the campaign was at break-even.
    • Several offers were tried with no change in conversion.
    • Buyer data showed mostly male customers. The product angle was rewritten for that group, which also changed nothing.
    • All campaigns were paused over the weekend.
    • The recommended next step was not to kill the product at break-even. Test a second market instead:
      • Translate the store with Shopify Translate (Shopify's translation tool).
      • Activate the UK market.
      • Set GBP prices (British pounds), such as 24.90 or 26.90.
      • Spend about 100 euros on English ads, with copy (sales text) tied to a specific use situation.
    • Losses of about 200 to 300 dollars would be the point to stop.

Funnel stages and benchmarks

1 min
  • A Shopify funnel (Shopify is the online store software; a funnel is the path from visit to sale) has four stages:
    • landing or product page: the first page a visitor sees
    • add to cart: the visitor puts an item in the basket
    • initiate checkout: the visitor starts entering payment details
    • purchase: the order is paid
  • Measure each stage on its own so you can find the weakest step.
  • Strong ad numbers do not prove the store works. Analyze the store separately.
  • Use these benchmarks (normal target numbers) as guidelines. Check them against your niche (the type of product you sell).
Stage Metric Benchmark
Landing page to add to cart Add-to-cart rate 6% or higher is good
Add to cart to initiate checkout Checkout start rate 25% to 50% is normal; aim for 50%
Initiate checkout to purchase Purchase rate 50% or higher is ideal
  • Use these stage actions when a rate is below target. A rate of 1-2% at the add-to-cart stage is a clear failure.
Funnel stage Metric Target If below target
Landing page to Add to Cart Add-to-cart rate 6% or higher Fix the landing or product page; 1-2% is a clear failure
Add to Cart to Initiate Checkout Checkout start rate 25-50% (aim for 50%) Fix friction and trust on the way into checkout
Initiate Checkout to Purchase Purchase rate 50% or higher Fix checkout trust, payment options, shipping and taxes
  • Initiate checkout is counted once a visitor enters contact details, such as an email address.
  • Example: 12 add-to-carts should produce about 3 to 6 initiate checkouts. 6 is the better result.
  • Initiate checkout to purchase should be at least 50%. If it is lower, optimize the checkout. That means:
    • trust elements (signs that make buyers feel safe)
    • shipping display (how shipping costs and times are shown)
    • taxes
    • currency
    • payment methods

Calculating the rates

1 min

  • Calculate funnel rates from clean visitor counts. Remove bot traffic (fake visits from automatic programs) and launch-spike traffic (a sudden burst of visits when something launches) first.

  • Use these formulas. A rate is a share, shown as a percent. "Events" means recorded actions, such as one add to cart.

    • Add-to-cart rate = add-to-cart events / landing-page visitors x 100
    • Checkout start rate = initiate-checkout events / add-to-cart events x 100
    • Purchase rate = purchases / initiate-checkout events x 100

  • Use genuine landing-page visitors (real people, not bots) as the base for the add-to-cart rate. Example: 12 add-to-carts from 200 genuine visitors gives 6%, which meets the benchmark.

  • Worked example: with 200 real visitors and 12 Add-to-Cart events, the Add-to-Cart rate is 6%, which meets the benchmark. Twelve add-to-carts leading to three to six checkout initiations would be within the 25-50% range.

  • A funnel judgment needs at least 150-200 real visitors. 50-100 visitors is too little data.

Stage-by-stage check

1 min
  1. Check landing page to add to cart against the target of 6% or higher. If it is 1-2%, the landing page or product page is the problem.
  2. Check add to cart to initiate checkout against the target of 25-50%. With 12 add-to-carts, that means at least 3 to 6 checkout starts.
  3. Check initiate checkout to purchase against the target of 50% or higher. If it is lower, optimize the checkout.
  4. Fix only the stage that fails before you move on.
  5. Do not change checkout or other stages at the same time as the landing page. Otherwise you cannot tell which change caused the result.

Diagnosis order

1 min
  • Diagnose a low conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) in this order. Never skip ahead.
    1. Evaluate traffic quality: look at search terms (what people type to find you) and check whether visitors are the right people at the right stage of the buying journey (how close they are to buying).
    2. Run competitor analysis: list every seller of the same product. Compare price, returns policy (rules for sending items back), review count, site design, and similar factors in a matrix (a comparison table).
    3. Only then optimize the website and offer, based on what the competitor comparison showed. Doing the website first wastes effort and can make your position worse.
  • Speed and friction issues (slow pages or anything that makes buying hard) matter most when visitors arrive but do not add to cart. Fix these first, before other changes.
  • If the landing-page add-to-cart rate is below benchmark, fix the landing or product page first. Leave later funnel stages for now.

Diagnosis: low add-to-cart

1 min
  • Check the landing and product page first. Speed and friction issues matter most when visitors arrive but do not add to cart.
  • Product pages should clearly show these points. Gaps explain why competitors win sales. [older advice, 2020]
    • stock status and shipping time
    • The shipping and returns policy
    • The price match policy (a promise to match a lower price elsewhere)
    • more and better images
    • descriptions that answer buyer questions

Diagnosis: low checkout completion

1 min
  • Check initiate checkout to purchase against the 50% target. If it is lower, check the checkout: trust elements, shipping display, taxes, currency, and payment methods.
  • When TikTok traffic (visitors from the TikTok app) adds to cart but does not buy, check the offer, the trust signals (proof that a store is real and safe), and the checkout, in that order. High add-to-cart with low purchases usually points to checkout or trust level rather than the ad.
  • When an ad brings many checkout starts but no sales, place a test order yourself through the full checkout. This confirms payment works before you change the ad.
  • If the checkout works, run the original ad again. This shows whether the low conversion came from the ad or from a checkout fault that has since been fixed.

Diagnosis: high add-to-cart with few purchases

1 min
  • High add-to-cart with few purchases can be caused by a broad audience (a wide group of people the ads reach). For example, TikTok audiences under 24 years old. Exclude non-buying age groups, such as 18-24, if they convert poorly. Then test a stronger offer.
  • Example: a store with 18+ targeting (ads shown to everyone aged 18 and up) had good add-to-carts but weak conversion. After removing the 18-24 age group, add-to-carts dropped but the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) improved. Watch the conversion rate, not the add-to-cart count.

Diagnosis: high CTR with no sales

1 min

  • If CPC (cost per click, what one click costs) is below $1, CPM (cost per 1,000 ad views) is below $10, and CTR (click-through rate, the share of viewers who click the ad) is above 1.5%, but the store converts poorly, work on the store instead of the ads.

  • Test new offers, a new store design, and new copywriting (the words on the page and in the ad). Change something on the store every day until the conversion rate reaches the target.

  • When a product gets clicks and spend but no sales, check the parts you control in this order:

    1. Landing page first
    2. creative (the ad's images, video and text)
    3. offer (price and deal)
    4. The product itself, only last

    Changing the product before the page is fixed leaves the real cause unknown.

  • Clicks and spend may not show sales on the same day. Orders can come from people who save an ad and keep it until a need appears. So a good ad can produce sales months after it stops running. Plan for delayed responses (sales that arrive late) when you measure an ad campaign.

Diagnosis: traffic but no sales

1 min

  • Assuming a traffic problem when sales are missing is a mistake. If visitors arrive and do not convert, the issue is usually the store experience or the offer compared with competitors.

  • Diagnose in this order.

    1. Check the bounce rate (the share of visitors who leave a page without doing anything else) on the pages that receive traffic.
    2. Check whether shoppers add products to the cart and then leave without completing the order.
    3. Check whether visitors are comparison shopping and leaving for better deals elsewhere.
    4. Re-research the top competitors, focusing on the pages already getting traffic, to see what they do differently.
    5. Only after you know what competitors offer, add incentives such as bonus gifts or expiring coupon codes (discount codes with an end date) to make buyers choose your store.

  • Case: a second store with over 100 products had been open since April. It ran Google Ads (Google's paid ads) and worked on SEO (getting found in free search results). It received a fair amount of traffic and had zero conversions. The previous Black Friday produced zero revenue.

    • The recommended diagnosis was a conversion problem, not a traffic problem.
    • Check the bounce rate and the add-to-cart-then-leave behaviour.
    • Check comparison shopping.
    • Then re-research competitors on the pages that already get traffic.
    • Set up email campaigns (mass emails to subscribers) before peak season (the busiest sales period), so non-buying visitors can be followed up.

Google Shopping troubleshooting

1 min
  • The Google Shopping conversion rate should be around 2.5 percent or higher, roughly five times the 0.5 percent that signals a problem. Measure it on Google Shopping traffic only (visitors who come from Google's product listings), not overall site traffic.
  • Conversion fix when Google Shopping traffic does not convert.
    1. In Google Ads, list the products that receive clicks and where the spend goes.
    2. Search those products on Google Shopping the way a customer would, and note which competitors show up.
    3. Open the competitor ads and product pages, and compare them side by side with your own pages.
    4. Check how each competitor shows shipping timing and stock, shipping and returns policy, price match policy, number and quality of images, and product descriptions.
    5. Cut spend on products that cost money without sales and that you cannot compete on.
    6. Improve your product pages where competitors are stronger. Then move budget to products that convert.

Traffic versus store attribution

1 min

Attribution means working out which part caused a result.

  • Strong ad metrics do not prove the store works, so analyze the store separately.
  • Use the funnel stages to decide whether a problem sits in traffic or in the store. Ad clicks with a healthy landing-page add-to-cart rate point to later stages. A 1-2% add-to-cart rate points to the landing or product page.
  • Measure ad effect with delayed responses in mind, because a good ad can produce sales months after it stops running.
  • Do not treat a traffic problem as the cause of missing sales without checking the store.

When to kill a product

1 min

"Kill" means stop selling a product.

  • Do not conclude that a product is dead after the first day or two of traffic. Low sales often come from fixable problems in the offer, images, trust elements, or checkout. Optimize each level before giving up.
  • Before cutting a product, check the parts you control in this order: landing page, creative, offer, then the product itself.
  • Cut spend on products that cost money without sales and that you cannot compete on. Then improve product pages where competitors are stronger, before moving budget to products that convert.

Further details

1 min

  • In Phase 5 (Days 60-75), after 48-72 hours of ad testing, diagnose whether poor performance is caused by:

    • an ad problem (weak creative, or ineffective targeting, meaning the wrong people see the ad), or
    • a funnel problem (low product page appeal, unclear offer, or checkout friction).

    This diagnosis determines which system needs optimization effort.
