Playbook › 6. Conversion optimization

Site speed, mobile and technical hygiene

Section 6.18 · 18 of 22 in this chapter · 14 min read

In short

  • Aim for a page that fully loads in under 2 seconds. Stop chasing speed once the whole store averages about 5 seconds.
  • Most shoppers use phones. Test speed on a phone with a weak connection, not on your fast home Wi-Fi.
  • Slow pages lose sales and repeat buyers. Remove the heaviest items first: big images, unused apps and messy code.
  • Change one thing at a time and test it, so you know what actually helped.

Page speed targets

2 min
  • Older target, 2021: a fully loaded page should take under 2 seconds. "Fully loaded" means every part of the page has finished downloading. Look for speed gains when a page loads slower than about 2 to 2.5 seconds.
  • Older advice, 2021-22: stop at about 5 seconds on average. Keep reducing speed until the store averages about 5 seconds. Then move your time to core conversion work. "Conversion" means turning visitors into buyers. Chasing 1 to 2 second load times usually costs developer time (paid hours for a coder) that does not pay back, unless measured results show a sales gain.
  • Both values are given because the sources do not reconcile them. Use the 2-second figure as the aim for individual pages. Use the 5-second average as the point where more speed work needs proof from measured conversion results.
  • Slow mobile connections: shoppers on slow phone connections may leave a page that takes more than about 3 seconds to appear. Then your paid ad clicks are wasted. Example: a visitor from Google Shopping ads (paid product ads on Google) looked at a $2,500 dining table. The visitor left after a 3-second spinner (the loading circle) and bought from a competitor.
  • Judge speed on a weak connection: mobile traffic (visits from phones) is a large share of ecommerce visits. So mobile speed matters more than a fast desktop or home Wi-Fi test suggests. Test your store on a phone with a weak connection, such as an airport network or a slow mobile connection.
  • Already-good stores: a website speed score around GTmetrix grade A with a performance score near 97 is considered very good. GTmetrix is a free tool that grades page speed. Further speed work is then not a priority compared with competitors. This is a quick reality check for stores already at that level. It does not replace the targets above.
  • Speed and conversion: load time directly affects conversion rate, which is the share of visitors who buy. Slower pages result in fewer purchases. That makes image file optimization critical to ecommerce profit. Optimizing means making files smaller and faster to load.
  • Speed and bounce: fast load times and clear details reduce product page bounce. "Bounce" means a visitor leaves right away.
  • Speed and experience: page speed affects how users experience a store and how many of them buy. Check and improve speed on store pages.
  • Speed and repeat purchases: customers who are unhappy with page performance are 79% less likely to buy again. Speed therefore affects repeat purchases. It also affects how well email and retargeting campaigns work. Retargeting means showing ads again to people who already visited.

Measuring speed

3 min
  • Test the page as a customer would:
    1. Load the page on desktop (a computer), mobile (a phone) and tablet.
    2. Load it on different computers, on cellular data (phone internet) and on home Wi-Fi.
    3. On each one, check the page speed and look for loading problems.
  • GTmetrix check:
    1. Run the URL in GTmetrix. A URL is the web address of the page.
    2. If the scores show a lot of yellow or red, open the waterfall view. The waterfall is a chart that shows each item loading, one after another.
    3. Find the elements that load furthest to the right. Elements are the parts of a page, such as images and scripts. Furthest to the right means they finish loading last.
    4. Remove or optimize the slowest items, such as images or app code. App code is the code that apps add to your store. Third-party trackers (tools from other companies that record visits) that load late, such as an email platform script, can be acceptable.
  • Fastest route to the bottleneck (no year given): Run the store through GTmetrix or Google PageSpeed to find the main bottleneck. A bottleneck is the slowest part that holds everything else back. It is usually images or GIFs (small animated pictures).
  • GTmetrix recommendations on Shopify. Shopify is an online store platform:
    • Recommendations such as leveraging browser caching, reducing DNS lookups, enabling server-side compression and using a CDN are largely controlled by Shopify's hosting. Browser caching saves files on the visitor's device so they load faster next time. DNS lookups are the address checks a page makes. Server-side compression shrinks files before they are sent. A CDN is a network of servers that delivers files from close to the visitor. Ignore these on a Shopify store, since they can usually be ignored.
    • Focus instead on the recommendations about images, scripts, apps and redirects. Scripts are small programs that run on the page. Redirects automatically send visitors from one web address to another.
    • GTmetrix ranks its recommendations by impact (how much a fix helps). The top rules improve the overall score most. Not every rule applies to every page, so treat the list as general best practice, not a checklist to finish.
    • Keep total page size and the number of requests low. A request is each separate file the page asks for. GTmetrix reports both next to the fully loaded time. A page with more than 100 requests and close to 2 MB of total size (MB means megabytes, a unit of file size), as in typical unoptimized Shopify stores, usually has clear app and image problems to fix. Unoptimized means not yet made smaller or faster.
    • Shopify's LCP (Largest Contentful Paint, the time until the main content appears) and CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift, how much the page jumps around while loading) are driven mainly by apps and JavaScript for LCP. JavaScript is the code that makes pages interactive. For CLS, they are driven by the theme (the store's design template) and by apps that push page elements around as they load. FCP (First Contentful Paint, the time until anything first appears) is harder to control and is usually not a problem.
  • Speed score availability: the Online store speed score is not available while password protection is on. After the password is disabled, it can take 1 to 3 days for the score to be calculated. Installed apps, edited theme code, and the size of images and videos all affect the score.

Speed fix sequence

2 min

Work from easiest to hardest. Procedure from Source A:

  1. Reduce homepage slideshows. Slideshows are rotating pictures at the top of the home page. Six to ten slides that nobody watches waste resources (computer power) and annoy visitors. Remove them unless you need them.
  2. Reconsider quick-shop functionality. Quick-shop is a button that lets shoppers add items from a product list without opening the product page. It is heavy on resources. Keep it where it helps upselling (selling extra or pricier items), such as customers buying several low-price items together. Remove it for high-ticket products (expensive items) where it adds little.
  3. Use the right image format. Use SVG for icons and logos. SVG is a small format made of shapes. Use JPEG for photos, such as hero images (the large main picture at the top of a page). Use PNG only when a transparent background is needed.
  4. Crop and scale product images to the size the site needs. Crop means cutting away edges. Scale means resizing. For example, reduce a 5000 pixel wide supplier image to 2048 pixels. Pixels are the dots that make up a picture. This keeps high definition without loading unnecessary data.
  5. Compress images. Compression means squeezing a file smaller. Tools can reduce file size by up to 70 to 80% without visible loss. Use the manual upload tool TinyPNG, or an automatic image app such as RushPix.
  6. Remove apps you do not need. Apps are add-on tools installed into your store. Review your app list. Apps are among the heaviest elements after images, so keep only the few the business truly needs. Replace small functions with simple code where possible. For example, a terms-and-conditions checkbox on the cart page.
  7. Update the theme if it is old or has been patched by multiple developers. Check the browser console (a developer panel that shows errors) for JavaScript errors. Broken scripts can stop features like cart upsells from working.
  8. Minify and optimize the code. Minifying means removing extra spaces and characters from code so it is smaller. This usually takes 2 to 4 hours at most for a skilled developer. Treat longer estimates with caution.
  9. Back up the theme before any change. A backup is a saved copy you can restore. Then test the entire store afterwards.

Source C procedure

1 min
  1. Run the store through GTmetrix or Google PageSpeed to find the main bottleneck, which is usually images or GIFs.
  2. Compress images with TinyPNG or Photoshop. Photoshop is an image editing program. Target image files below 300 KB and GIFs below 800 KB. KB means kilobytes, a small file size unit. Product images should ideally be under 50 KB while keeping visual quality nearly unchanged.
  3. Remove unused apps and their code. Each active app loads its own JavaScript on every page view. A page view is one visitor loading one page.
  4. Page builders such as PageFly add an extra layer on top of the theme and slow the page somewhat. A page builder is a tool for designing pages without writing code. They can still be worth it for their conversion features.

Images, media and video

1 min
  • Compress selectively: compress image files selectively during website setup. Focus on larger files and those that noticeably affect page load speed. Compressing every image on the site is unnecessary. This refines the all-at-once compression step above.
  • Autoplay and loop: autoplay means a video starts by itself. Looping means it repeats. When the store has no videos yet, turn off video autoplay and looping in the product page media settings. Turn them off again whenever videos are added later. Autoplay and loop can distract visitors and slow the page down.
  • Homepage video test: before adding a video to the homepage, test the page with GTmetrix. A video can noticeably raise the load time. Remove it if the score drops sharply.
  • Videos on product pages: keep videos where they help buyers, but compress images so they do not slow the page.
  • Self-host or embed: self-hosting means storing video files on your own store. Embedding means showing a video that is stored on YouTube. Embed product videos from your own YouTube channel instead of self-hosting them, because self-hosting slows the page more. Embedding even from YouTube lowers the page speed score, but the page still loads fine for visitors.
  • Social links in a new tab: social media links on the store can be set to open in a new tab, so visitors do not leave the site. This needs a small code snippet (a short piece of code) from the store developer.

Apps, code and theme minimalism

1 min
  • Less is more: add features and apps one at a time. Test whether revenue or sales increase after each change. Without testing, it is impossible to know which feature or app improves conversion.
  • Avoid extra apps and custom code (2023): avoid extra apps or custom code (code written just for your store) that slow down the store. Page speed affects both conversion and search performance, which is how well your pages rank on Google. Remove unused apps. Verify that no code is left behind after uninstalling them.
  • Leftover code (2024): mobile traffic that does not convert can point to page speed problems. Remove leftover code from uninstalled apps. Check speed for mobile before adding more apps.
  • Store speed factors: store speed depends on three things. These are how many apps are installed, whether the theme's code has been modified, and how large the images and videos are. To keep the speed score healthy, limit the number of apps, keep custom code lean (small and simple), and make media files small.
  • Plugins and pop-ups (no year given): plugins are add-on tools, like apps on other platforms. Stores with bugs, errors, or unused plugins that slow the site frustrate visitors and push them away. Several stacked pop-ups for the same offer do too. Pop-ups are boxes that appear over the page. Remove plugins that do nothing.
  • Plugins and pop-ups (no year given): limit pop-ups to one useful message.

Hidden faults and pre-launch checks

1 min
  • Case: a faulty app hid behind a healthy-looking store.
    • The store had substantial traffic (many visitors) and enough revenue to employ a small team. Its conversion rate was 0.24%.
    • The owners did not see the problem because they were too close to their own site.
    • An outside expert checked the store within about five minutes of trying to check out (paying for an item).
    • The expert found a faulty app, plus code left three months earlier by an inexperienced developer.
    • After the expert removed the faulty app and corrected the code, conversion rose roughly eightfold within 24 hours.
    • The expert charged only for a review, not a fee, because the fix had taken five minutes.
  • Buy your own product before launch: before launching a store or checkout (the page where a customer pays) on a new platform, buy a product through it yourself. Walk through the full customer journey (every step a buyer takes). Test each payment method to confirm it works from start to finish.

Mobile responsiveness and device split

2 min

Responsive means the site adjusts to fit the screen it is shown on.

  • Share of mobile visits, by traffic source (traffic source means where the visitor came from):
Traffic source Mobile share Source
Facebook and Instagram ads (heavy traffic) 70-80% of visits
Landing page traffic Roughly 80-85% of visitors by smartphone
TikTok 95-99% of users shop on a phone
  • Consequence: a website that is not responsive will lose most of its potential customers when 70 to 80% of visits come from mobile devices.
  • Responsiveness signal: a relative mobile conversion rate well below about 30% usually means the website is not responsive. Relative means compared with desktop. Fix layout and responsiveness problems before drawing conclusions about mobile traffic quality.
  • Landing page top section: a landing page is the page that ads send people to. Landing page conversion depends heavily on mobile. The top of the page must show value within seconds. Product images, star ratings and a benefit-driven headline (a headline that says what the buyer gains) should be visible without scrolling.
  • Mobile-first design: when traffic comes from TikTok, 95-99% of users shop on a phone. So build the mobile version of the store first.
  • Pre-launch phone check: before launch, check the landing page on a phone-sized preview, such as the iPhone 12 screen size. Scroll the full page, because stacked content on mobile can become very long. Confirm that:
    • carousels (sliders that move sideways) scroll sideways smoothly,
    • The offer and the add-to-cart button (the button that puts an item in the cart) stay visible near the top,
    • accordion sections (blocks that open when tapped) open correctly.
  • Mobile bounce:
    1. Compare bounce rate and session time on mobile and desktop in Google Analytics. Bounce rate is the share of visitors who leave right away. Session time is how long a visit lasts.
    2. If mobile is much worse, speed up the site on mobile connections.
    3. Optionally, set mobile bid adjustments in Google Ads to reduce spend on the weaker device. A bid adjustment means paying more or less for clicks on one device type.
    4. Check which search terms send traffic, and add negatives for terms that do not match your pages. Negatives are search words that stop your ads from showing.
  • Mobile browsing, desktop buying: customers often browse on mobile and buy later on desktop. If desktop buying is high, adjust bids by device rather than moving all traffic to desktop.
  • Device review every 30 to 60 days:
    1. Set a recurring reminder for the review.
    2. Compare the conversion rate for desktop and for mobile in your reports.
    3. Group products by price or sales cycle (how long a buyer takes to decide) into separate campaigns. A campaign is a group of ads.
    4. Adjust device visibility at the campaign level, since device bids are not set per product.

Bot traffic and quick exits

1 min

A bot is a fake visitor made by a program. A quick exit is a visitor who leaves within seconds.

  • Investigating visitors who leave within seconds:
    1. Find the pages where many visitors leave right away, and test their speed.
    2. Check the traffic source of those visits.
    3. If one source causes the quick exits, adjust or pause those ads.
    4. If the visits are direct or from unknown sources, check the IP addresses (the number that identifies each internet connection) and look for bots.
  • Direct and unpaid visits: direct means the visitor typed your address or used a bookmark. Unpaid means the visit did not come from a paid ad. Visitors from direct or unpaid sources who leave within seconds are often bots. Verify by looking up their IP addresses. Do not panic over unpaid traffic.

Tracking and conversion events

1 min

Tracking means recording visitor actions. A conversion event is a counted goal, such as a purchase.

  • Phone calls count once per person: when a single person makes multiple calls to your tracked phone number, it counts as one conversion total, not multiple conversions.
  • Revenue value for calls is optional: assigning a revenue value (a dollar amount) to phone call conversions is optional. You can leave this field empty.
  • Bundle discounts skip the add-to-cart event: a bundle discount is a deal for several items together. Skipping the add-to-cart step through a bundle discount may not be tracked as an add to cart in Shopify tracking. Check whether the bundle path is counted correctly before judging the page.

Technical hygiene: indexing

1 min

Indexing means Google adds your pages to its search results. Duplicate URLs are copies of the same page under different web addresses.

  • Duplicate URLs in Search Console (2024): Google Search Console is Google's free tool for checking how Google sees your site. It may report pages as not indexed. Shopify generates duplicate URLs for variants (versions of a product, such as colors) and collections (groups of products). Only the main product URL is meant to be indexed. So a count like 204 indexed out of about 5,000 pages can be normal and needs no fix.
