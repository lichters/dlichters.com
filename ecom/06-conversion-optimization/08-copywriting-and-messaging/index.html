Copywriting and messaging

Section 6.8 · 8 of 22 in this chapter · 57 min read

In short

  • Copy is writing that makes a buyer take one action, such as buying.
  • Copy must answer three questions: why buy this, why buy from you, and why buy now.
  • Start with the buyer's problem. Then show your product as the answer.
  • Map the product before you write. Write down the problem, the special feature, the benefits and the proof.
  • Promise only what you can prove, with real numbers and reviews.
More key points
  • Start with the problem your buyer wants to avoid.
  • Put a benefit next to every feature: what the feature does for the buyer.
  • Back every claim with proof, or remove it.
  • Answer the buyer's doubts on the page before they leave.
  • Use the homepage template exactly as written.
  • Name a free ebook as a bonus and give it a value. Do not just write "Includes Ebook."
  • Every product copy needs a clear call to action (the line that tells the reader what to do next), and reasons to buy must come before it.
  • Change one part of the copy at a time, test it, and keep the version that gets more buyers.
  • Write product copy in six parts, one part at a time, then join them into one page.
  • Answer doubts before the buyer raises them, and show that the result is easy to reach.
  • Write from the buyer's side. Find the real reason they want the product.
  • Ask for a small yes first. A small yes makes the big yes easier.
  • End every letter with one clear action, a reason to act now, and an easy way to do it.
  • Check every letter: does the reader trust you, like the product, believe the urgency, and find the offer exciting?

Copy foundations and the job of copy

3 min

  • Copywriting is persuasive writing, meaning words that make a reader do something. It aims at one specific action, such as a click, an opt-in (signing up, for example to an email list) or an add to cart (putting an item in the online basket). In ecommerce most of these actions are add to cart and purchase, so any product page text that pushes toward them is copywriting.

  • Content writing informs and educates. A product description, however, has to sell the product. Describing what the product is does not persuade on its own.

  • The marketing message (the main reason to buy) can be delivered as text, video, images or any mix. No single format is required. What matters is that every element of the marketing message is present, whatever the format.

  • Copy has to answer three questions: | Question | Where the answer comes from | |---|---| | Why buy this product? | Mainly the marketing message | | Why buy it from you? | Can be covered by a unique selling proposition (USP, the one reason you are different) of up to five points | | Why buy it now? | Mainly the marketing message |

  • Copy does not need to be the best copy in the world. It only needs to be more developed than the marketing messages of direct competitors (other shops selling the same kind of product) to persuade customers to buy from you.

  • Marketing messages for the product (copy, videos and images) should be better than competitors' messages. This element is separate from the offer structure (how the deal is built: price, bonuses, guarantee) and gets its own treatment later.

  • Write website copy from the customer's point of view. Answer their questions before they ask them, such as shipping time, return policy (the rules for sending items back) and product materials. Then they feel comfortable buying without doubt.

  • Education builds trust by giving useful information before asking for money. Explaining the problem and its causes gives the reader value up front and makes the product feel like the logical answer.

  • Consistency means people want their actions to match the identity they show publicly. Copy that appeals to an identity, such as "of course you want the best for your pet", makes the reader more likely to act in line with it.

  • A complete landing page (the page a visitor arrives on) must surface the customer's problems, accentuate benefits (what the product does for the buyer), demonstrate the features (what the product has) that prove those benefits, show the desired outcome, and handle objections (doubts a buyer has). This is usually done in an FAQ (a list of common questions and answers) section.

  • Use an AI chat tool such as ChatGPT to rewrite benefits in a more striking or conversational style. Use it also to produce problem-solution copy and lists that turn each feature into a benefit, based on the product description. If the copy is weak, use such a tool to draft it.

  • Specific, consumer-addressed wording in guarantees and product copy outperforms generic phrasing. Generic text reads as template text that buyers have seen many times.

  • Copy quality rarely decides a page alone. A poor headline (the big title at the top) or an unclear page is a common reason for a 1 to 2 percent conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy). Small changes to landing page structure often produce larger gains than split testing (showing two versions to visitors to see which sells more).

Bottom-of-funnel and psychological framing

1 min
  • Bottom-of-funnel (BOF) means the last stage before a buyer decides. The path to conversion runs through negative emotions, not around them. BOF ads (ads for people close to buying) must address the psychological state of prospects (how they feel right now). First acknowledge the problem they face, before presenting how your solution changes their situation.
  • To generate BOF ads, ask Claude (an AI chat tool) for ad copy and concepts. Base them on all gathered market research, the emotional framework analysis and the target evidence metrics (the proof numbers you aim for). Claude creates the ad copy and concepts from the customer psychology (how buyers think and feel) and language patterns (the words buyers use) that you provide.
  • Competitor headlines pair a specific benefit with a time frame. This makes the promise measurable and sets clear expectations. Use the same formula of benefit plus time window, for example Fall Asleep Faster in 14 Days or Feel Less Stiff by Week Three. Only use time frames you can back with real customer results.

Mapping the product before writing

5 min
  • Product copy is built from separate elements such as headline, benefits, features and proof (evidence that backs your claims). It is not written in one pass from top to bottom. Gathering these elements in a structured document before writing reduces the stall (being stuck in front of an empty box). It shortens total writing time, even though it adds a step.
  • Answer these questions about the product before writing any copy, in this order:
    1. Product name (used as the tab name and header, so the record can be tracked)
    2. Primary problem: the one main problem the product solves for most customers
    3. Secondary problems: any additional problems the product solves
    4. Unique mechanism(s): the special attribute that solves the primary problem in a way competing products do not
    5. How the unique mechanism works: the process that produces the result
    6. Features: the attributes of the product you would tell a customer to justify buying it
    7. Benefit of each feature: a list that mirrors the feature list point for point
    8. How the product improves the ideal customer's life: a picture of what the customer experiences once they have used it
    9. Proof points: reviews, demonstration videos, and third-party tests (checks done by an outside group)
  • Use a Google Sheets (a free online spreadsheet) product mapping template for the exercise. Make a copy and save it to Google Drive (online file storage). Or convert it to Excel if you prefer to work offline.
  • Keep a master template tab (a blank copy you reuse) in the mapping sheet. For each new product, right-click the template tab and choose Duplicate. Then right-click the copy and choose Rename to set the product name. Each product then gets its own tab in one file, so products can be flicked between and compared.
  • The primary problem should be the biggest and most common problem the product solves for most customers. People buy a product to solve a problem they have. Pick that one for the main slot and record any other problems as secondary problems.
  • Secondary problems can be recorded in addition to the primary problem. A product may solve several, and that is not a problem. Only the biggest one goes into the main slot when writing copy.
  • A unique mechanism is an attribute of the product that solves the primary problem in a way competing products do not. Ideally it is built into the product, such as a technology or a specially designed feature. A product can have more than one.
  • Claiming a unique feature without explaining how it works is a mistake. After naming a unique mechanism, always state how it produces the result. Otherwise the claim is not convincing.
  • Five product features is the recommended number to list. Three is acceptable if you want to focus, and fewer than five is fine. Beyond five, the value of each added feature drops.
  • Each feature must be paired with a benefit that answers why it matters to the customer. Write the benefit list as a matching list to the feature list, so every feature has its own benefit line.
  • The customer transformation (how the buyer's life changes) describes what the ideal customer experiences in real use after owning the product, in concrete terms. It should show how daily life changes, not just restate the product's features.
  • Proof points are recorded in the mapping for use in copy and on the product page. Typical proof points are customer reviews, demonstration videos showing use and effect, and third-party tests that verify the results.
  • Worked example: a compact two-person infrared sauna for home use. This was technical and expensive, and it sold outside the field the mapper knew well.
    • Primary problem: the range of health benefits people seek from heat and infrared light (invisible light that warms the body), including better sleep, weight loss, relaxation, improved circulation (better blood flow), relief from joint and muscle pain, and improved skin.
    • Secondary problem: convenience, since users no longer have to travel to a gym or spa, pay repeatedly for sessions, or lose travel time.
    • Unique mechanism: a trademarked technology (a brand-protected name) called True Wave II Full Spectrum Heating Technology. Its heaters produce near, mid and far infrared (three kinds of infrared light) together at a full 1000W. This delivers the benefits of infrared saunas in less time.
    • Features listed: True Wave Technology, low EMF (electromagnetic field, the invisible energy given off by electrical devices), 360 degree heating, non-toxic materials, easy assembly, lifetime warranty, and chromotherapy lighting (coloured light therapy).
    • Matching benefits:
      • full-spectrum heat in a shorter session
      • electromagnetic output of only 1mg against a recommended healthy limit of 3mg, which makes everyday use considered safe based on testing to date
      • consistent infrared access across the whole body from 360 degree heating
      • no toxic chemicals escaping from the materials
      • assembly by two people in about an hour with no professional installation needed
      • a lifetime warranty so the customer never pays again for a defective unit
      • full-spectrum light therapy linked to reduced swelling, pain relief and faster recovery
    • Customer transformation: the customer can practise heat therapy at home. This makes them more consistent and helps them feel better in both physical and emotional life.
    • Proof points: product reviews, a video demonstration of the product, and third-party testing of the heat sources.
  • An infrared sauna example listed 1000W of infrared energy as the full output. It stated this as 25x more than the closest competitor. It also gave an electromagnetic field output of 1mg against a recommended healthy maximum of 3mg. These figures were used as concrete, checkable benefit claims in the copy.
  • Fill in a marketing message sheet (a one-page plan of your main message) for each product before writing its description:
    1. Write the primary promise (the main result you promise the buyer) or promises.
    2. Write the unique mechanism.
    3. List the features and their benefits.
    4. List the dimensionalized benefits (benefits with a number or detail attached) to include on the page.
    5. Give the sheet to the copywriter together with the product data. Or use it yourself as the first step before writing.

Problems, pain points and desire

2 min
  • Buyers do not always consciously recognize their problems. Status-related problems (worries about how others see you) are usually felt subconsciously (without being noticed). So copy has to name them, even when the buyer has not put them into words.
  • A problem, also called a pain point (the thing that hurts or annoys the buyer), creates a desire for either a solution or a pleasant outcome. Copy should connect the problem to the result the buyer wants.
  • Buyers are motivated either by moving away from pain or by moving toward pleasure. Copy can address only the pain, only the pleasure, or both. It depends on what fits the product and audience.
  • People buy for only two reasons: to escape pain or to pursue pleasure. Copy that triggers these feelings more strongly than competitor pages tends to sell more.
  • People buy a product to solve a problem that creates a desire. So the copy must first name the problem the product solves. Match it to the problem the target customer is actually experiencing. It is fine to address more than one problem the product solves.
  • After naming the problem, the copy must explain how the product solves it. Then it must describe how the customer's life changes, in a way they can picture. This is what is meant by benefits.
  • Visitors on product pages already have a strong interest in solving a specific pain point. A headline that names that pain point or its direct outcome gets the most attention.
  • Copywriting should surface the customer's problem first, then present the solution as a benefit. Features are shown to prove the benefit. The copy should ask what each feature means for the customer.
  • For products that solve a health problem, name the specific conditions and the pain the customer wants to escape. When the problem is clear, the customer does not need false urgency (fake time pressure, such as "only today") to buy. They only need a direct call to fix it.

Emotion before logic

2 min
  • The primary promise and the unique mechanism trigger emotion. Proof, dimensionalized benefits and comparisons supply the logic that confirms the feeling is right. Specifications (the exact product details) and clearly written features also serve as logic.
  • People buy an emotion and then justify it with logic. So each section of the page should add rational reasons that match the pain point the visitor is feeling.
  • When buyers are asked why they bought, they recall the logical reasons easily. But they often cannot name the emotion they felt. This effect is strongest for expensive purchases. So high-ticket (expensive) copy must trigger the emotion before it offers the reasons to justify the purchase.
  • Once the headline and primary promise have hooked the visitor, the copy should trigger emotion before the product is pitched. People buy for emotional reasons that logic then supports. So an emotional response built early raises the chance of a sale considerably.
  • The first one or two paragraphs under the headline should name the main problem the primary promise addresses. They should show what life looks like once that problem is solved. Describe the feeling the customer will have, such as relief, happiness, excitement or satisfaction.
  • Getting the reader to picture the end result makes them start to experience the emotions tied to that result. If product images or video already created this picture, the copy reinforces and multiplies the effect. So it is still worth doing.
  • Two proven ways to create this emotional visualization are telling a story about the product and using thought commands (short guided scenes that ask the reader to imagine something). Story is the stronger choice when a real story exists. Thought commands work for any product.
  • Before writing the imagined scene, build a short product map. It lists the primary problem, the primary promise and the secondary problems. Take the scene's angle and vocabulary from these entries. Then the picture follows directly from the headline and keeps the copy consistent.
  • If the product page already has sales copy, the story or thought-command section can be added as a few extra paragraphs. There is no need to rewrite the rest. Highlight the new text while editing, so it can be reviewed and compared against the original version.
  • Concrete everyday details make a scene easier to picture than general benefit statements. Examples are a specific time of day, a short distance from the bed and one simple action. Show a routine the reader can imagine happening in their own home.
  • Copy that opens with a thought or story and then moves into the offer is a structure to test. Add the copy elements one at a time: opening story or thought command, primary promise, unique mechanism, then benefits. Save each version. Comparing the versions shows whether each added element makes the text easier to read and more convincing.
  • A mechanism placed right after the primary promise keeps readers moving toward the call to action (the request to buy, such as a button saying "Add to cart"). At the same time, it builds the logical case for the product. Every section of the copy should keep the reader moving down the page.

Primary promise headlines

4 min
  • Good copy has no effect if nobody reads it. So the headline's first job is to earn attention before any selling happens. A product page or sales page headline should make visitors read the next sentence, not decide whether to buy.
  • Headlines carry most of the copywriting weight, followed by short bullet points. Keep bullets short and benefit-driven. Add a testimonial section (customer quotes), because shoppers look for reviews before they trust a site.
  • In a video sales presentation, the headline is the first one or two sentences spoken at the start of the video. Its purpose is the same as in text: to make viewers decide to keep watching.
  • The headline must include searchable keywords (the words customers type into search engines) that customers use. Example: Spiralizer Envy Spiral Vegetable Slicer includes both the brand name and the product keywords customers search for. (undated)
  • Place a unique selling proposition with the product name at the top or directly below it. Include a money-back guarantee in it. Keep the line short and benefit-led.
  • Hero value-proposition formula (the big top section of a page): a short headline states the outcome the customer wants. A qualifier (a word that makes it specific to the product) follows. Then a trust line sits directly beneath. Example pattern for a grooming product: Remove all pet hair from your home easily and effortlessly, followed by Rated Excellent on Trustpilot. Second example for a fitness recovery product: Boost recovery and blood flow after every session, followed by Trusted by 10,000+ athletes.
  • Procedure for writing a primary promise headline:
    1. Read the primary problem the product solves from the product mapping worksheet.
    2. Check whether the product sits in the health or finance space. If it does, do not put health or financial result claims in the headline.
    3. If it is health or finance, pick a secondary problem (such as convenience, time saved or money saved), or the biggest feature or unique mechanism, as the basis of the headline. If it is not health or finance, start from the primary problem.
    4. Write the headline as a direct reflection of that problem. Make the promise specific and include the how.
    5. If you use a number, make sure you have evidence for it. You must be able to explain where it comes from when asked.
    6. Make the headline bigger and bolder than the body copy (the main text). Include keywords if organic search (free results from search engines, not ads) matters.
    7. Split test the headline against at least one alternative. Keep the version with the better time on page (how long people stay on the page) and conversion rate.
  • Case: infrared sauna headline. The product is in the health space, and its primary problem is health benefits. So a health-based headline, such as weight loss within a time frame, would put ads at risk and could not be proven. The copywriter chose the unique mechanism (full-spectrum infrared heating) as the basis. The manufacturer provided heating-power and efficiency figures that were claimed to be backed by evidence. The copywriter avoided naming any health benefit. The headline promised 25 times more heating power and efficiency from the IS-2 sauna. The product title and image stayed above it, and the headline was placed as the first text element visitors read. No sales outcome is reported. The stated goal was to create enough curiosity that readers continue to the body copy. The copywriter noted that the figure must be explainable if a customer asks where it comes from.
  • Outcome-led headline with a proof stack (a set of trust points stacked under the headline). The headline names the result the buyer gets. A short support line makes the product specific. Three perks or guarantees follow, such as free shipping, a free gift or a risk-free trial. The buyer reads the benefit first and sees low-risk reasons to buy right away, instead of a temporary discount.
    • Example 1: Keep Your Knives Sharp Through Every Dinner Rush, followed by a forged steel blade, free resharpening for a year and a 60-day return window.
    • Example 2: Get Clean Sound Without the Cable Mess, followed by a 30-hour battery case, free shipping and a 30-day money-back guarantee.
  • Headlines that name the specific risk a buyer fears, and then offer one simple action to avoid it, convert well. The formula is risk first, then the low-effort fix.
    • Example 1: Don't book the trip before you check the seat map.
    • Example 2: Don't buy the sofa before you measure the doorway.
  • Formula: compare the abstract benefit (an idea that is hard to picture) to a familiar professional service the buyer would normally pay for. The product then feels like a personal solution. This makes the benefit tangible (easy to picture) and memorable in headlines and copy.
    • Example 1: A masseuse for your back, in your pocket.
    • Example 2: A personal trainer on your wrist, without the monthly gym fee.

Unique mechanism and features-to-benefits

5 min

  • Introduce the unique mechanism with a subheading. A unique mechanism is the special way your product works. A subheading is a short title that breaks up the copy. Copy means the written words on the page.

    • The subheading can be a question, a statement or just the technology name.
    • A subheading is optional, not required.

  • Write a landing page that explains why earlier solutions failed. A landing page is the single page a visitor arrives on.

    • First, state that most treatments only act on the surface.
    • Then, state that the real problem sits deeper, in the layer where the damage is.
    • A follicle is the small pocket in the skin where a hair grows.
    • Then, present your product's mechanism as the deeper fix.
    • This works because it explains past failure. It also makes the new mechanism believable.
    • Example 1: Most hair serums only coat the strands, but thinning starts in the follicle, which is why nothing you tried worked. Our formula reaches the follicle, so the result comes from the root.
    • Example 2: Most eye creams sit on the skin and only hide puffiness for an hour. The swelling is caused by fluid under the surface, and our gel-patch works there.

  • A feature is something the product has or does, such as a part or an ingredient.

  • A benefit is the result the buyer gets from that feature.

  • Never state a feature on its own. It does not tell the customer why it matters.

  • Always follow each feature with the benefit it delivers in practice.

  • Never write a feature list without benefits. Without them, the customer must work out why each feature matters. Many will not.

  • Attach a benefit statement to every feature in the description.

  • Focus product page copy on benefits, not features. Benefits are what the customer will feel or achieve. Features are the technical specifications, the plain facts about the product.

  • Show the end experience. Instead of multiple different features, write transform your skin appearance and confidence.

  • Include stories and feelings, not plain technical details.

  • Pair each concrete feature or ingredient with what it does for the customer. State it in outcome terms, meaning the result the buyer gets in real life.

    • Example 1: Our joint chews contain fatty acids; this supports everyday mobility and strong bones.
    • Example 2: Our bottle is made with a fast-dissolving powder; you have a smooth coffee in ten seconds, no blender needed.
    • Features prove the benefit and make it believable.

  • Show the desired outcome: what the customer will be able to do or feel after buying.

  • Then future-pace them into it. Future-pacing means helping the buyer picture life after the purchase.

  • Example pattern: Glowing skin: get selfie ready with a radiant complexion.

  • Pair each feature with its result for the buyer. Here is a two-person infrared sauna. An infrared sauna heats the body with infrared light instead of hot air. W means watts, the unit of power.

    • A full-spectrum heater with 1000 W output gives full benefit in a shorter session.
    • 360-degree heating gives consistent heat across the whole body.
    • Non-toxic materials mean no harmful chemicals can escape during heating.
    • Two people can assemble it in about one hour, so buyers need no professional installation or prior installation knowledge.
    • Each feature is followed by the practical result for the buyer and why it matters.

  • Pair each feature with the comfort or practical result it gives, not with the feature alone. Each benefit should answer what the customer avoids or gains.

    • Fleece traps air to insulate, so the wearer stays warm for longer. Writing it this way links the material directly to a customer result on the landing page.
    • An open toe on a warm sock or legwear can be explained as letting the toes breathe, so the customer understands why the design matters.
    • A full-length stocking covers the whole leg. Explain it as keeping the entire leg warm, not only part of it. The benefit should name the body area the customer gets covered.
    • A static (fixed in place, not moving) microfiber brush head with a hair collection lid can become two benefits on a pet-hair cleaning page. It keeps furniture and surfaces intact. It lets the user clean without the brush getting cluttered.

  • Use a credible statistic in problem-agitation-solution copy. This is a three-step pitch: name the problem, make it hurt more, then offer the fix.

  • Example statistic: 25% of the body's heat is lost through the legs. This makes a cold-legs problem concrete.

  • Confirm any such figure against a published source before putting it on a landing page. A published source is a report or study that anyone can check.

  • Use this pattern for the feature block on a product page. The feature block is the section that lists the product's features.

  • Start with a short lead line: [number] reasons to [action] today.

  • Then add bullets. Each bullet pairs one feature with one dimensionalized benefit. A dimensionalized benefit is a result the buyer can picture. It is not a bare spec. A spec (specification) is an exact technical detail.

  • The lead line adds a reason to act now without inventing scarcity, which means fake shortage.

    • Example 1: Four reasons to order the kettle today: followed by Double-walled steel keeps tea hot for two hours so that you can sit down for a proper break.
    • Example 2: Five reasons to book your bike service today: followed by Fresh brake pads and a true wheel so that every ride feels smooth and you can enjoy the hills again.

  • Compare the product with a familiar alternative, using a concrete figure. Example: a 30 minute sauna session is claimed to burn up to 600 calories, against about 300 calories from 30 minutes of jogging.

  • A claim is a statement you make about the product.

  • Cite a source for every such figure. Without one, it counts as an unsupported claim, meaning a statement with no proof behind it.

  • Reframe a physical effort in a familiar unit to make it feel easier. Example: state that a short workout equals a walk of a specific distance.

  • The familiar comparison turns an abstract effort into something the buyer can picture.

  • It also answers the objection that the activity is too hard. An objection is a doubt that stops the buyer from buying.

  • List the concrete features buyers compare on a connected home infrared sauna. Connected means it can be controlled by phone or tablet.

    • Wood species, for example Western Red Canadian Cedar or Basswood. A wood species is the type of tree the wood comes from.
    • Glass front with a glass skylight roof. A skylight is a window in the roof.
    • Recessed accent lights, which are lights built into the ceiling.
    • Digital keypad with intensity control, which is a setting for how strong the heat is.
    • Medical-grade chromotherapy. Chromotherapy is coloured light used for health. Medical-grade means made to a medical standard.
    • Bluetooth (a wireless connection) and AUX audio (a cable input for music).
    • Optional control by tablet or smartphone app.
    • A reversible bench with an ergonomic side (shaped to fit the body) and a flat side.

  • Write each feature with its exact specification. Avoid general words such as premium.

Proof, claims and credibility

2 min
  • Back every claim with proof. Proof is evidence that shows a claim is true. More proof points make the copy more convincing.
  • Remove any claim that cannot be backed by evidence.
  • A health claim needs links to the relevant studies. A claim of highest quality in a class needs support you can demonstrate.
  • Buyers want answers to three doubts beyond the product itself:
    • How is the product better than similar products?
    • How is the buyer protected?
    • Why is this the best deal?
  • Answer them with comparison points, a risk reversal (a guarantee that takes the risk off the buyer), and perceived value bonuses. Perceived value means how valuable the buyer thinks the offer is. A bonus is an extra item added to the offer.
  • Use scientific or research claims to counter a doubt about effectiveness. Effectiveness means whether the product works.
  • Example: cite studies that show a method works better than a common alternative.
  • Each claim needs a verifiable source, meaning a source anyone can check.
  • Show scientifically or quantitatively backed benefits, meaning benefits measured in numbers. Put exact numbers below the fold. The fold is the part of the page you see before scrolling down.
  • Example: a 15-minute routine delivers the benefit in 2 minutes and reduces pain by 9% and soreness by 5%.
  • Numbers back up the emotional buying decision.
  • A product positioned as the most cost-effective option (the best value for money) attracts price-sensitive buyers, meaning buyers who watch the price first.
  • This is a conscious targeting decision. You choose that audience on purpose.
  • Aim premium products (top quality, higher price) at buyers who want the best, not the cheapest.
  • Back the claim with a comparison document.
  • Know that a product that seems too perfect can hurt sales. Buyers suspect exaggeration, meaning stretching the truth.
  • Admit acknowledged flaws and explain them honestly. This increases credibility, which is how much buyers trust you.
  • Put a source behind every concrete figure in a headline or description.
  • Example: in the infrared sauna example, 1000W, 25x and 1mg against 3mg were presented as claims you can check.

Full product description structure

5 min
  • Build the product description in this order:
    1. Primary promise: a headline-style promise that is the opposite of the main problem that brought the customer to the page.
    2. Unique mechanism: the feature or attribute that delivers the promise. Ideally it is unique to this product, such as patented technology (a protected invention) or a special shape.
    3. Features followed by benefits: state what each feature does in practice for the customer.
    4. Dimensionalized benefits: turn each benefit into an outcome that triggers an emotion and can be pictured.
    5. Proof of claims and social proof: back every claim with evidence and show existing customer reviews. Social proof means other buyers' reviews and results that show others trust the product.
    6. Comparison: show how the product is better than similar products, which attributes differ, and who it suits best, using a comparison document or an on-page comparison tool.
    7. Urgency hook: a reason to act now. Connect the problem to an emotional response and state what happens if the customer does not buy now.
    8. Call to action (CTA): tell the reader exactly what to do, such as buy now. A CTA is the button or line that asks for the action.
  • A well-structured product description for a more expensive or technical product runs to about 500 words or a little more.
  • Simple products, such as t-shirts, can use a much shorter version, as long as all elements are still present.
  • Length does not matter. What matters is that each element of the marketing message appears on the page. The marketing message is the full set of reasons to buy.
  • Simpler products can carry the same elements visually, with little text.
  • Study this worked example: a product description for a low-EMF far infrared sauna. Low-EMF means low electromagnetic fields, the invisible energy given off by electric devices. Far infrared means a gentle heat that reaches deep into the body.
    • The writer did not sell this product and knew little about it. They wrote the description to show the method.
    • Starting point: a 4-person unit priced at $4,999, positioned as the most cost-effective infrared sauna on the market. Positioned means placed in the buyer's mind as.
    • The copy opens with a promise for buyers who want better health with convenience and a lower price. Then it contrasts the usual alternatives.
    • A typical installed sauna costs over $8,000. A trip to a gym or outdoor run takes about 60 minutes in total for a 30 minute workout.
    • The mechanism is far infrared rays. They raise blood circulation (blood flow) and oxygen supply at a lower temperature than steam saunas.
    • Health claims cover inflammation, pain, skin and conditions such as arthritis (painful, swollen joints), hemorrhoids (swollen veins near the bottom) and psoriasis (a skin condition with red, scaly patches).
    • The copy notes that links to peer-reviewed studies (studies checked by other experts before publishing) must be inserted.
    • The cardio comparison says a 30 minute session can burn up to 600 calories, against about 300 calories for 30 minutes of jogging. That figure needs a cited source.

    • The description closes with a call to action to buy today, with delivery within 72 hours. It also includes a link to a model comparison document and a separate specification list on the page.
    • The finished text runs a little over 500 words. This example reports no sales results.
  • Lead the product description with the problem the customer wants to avoid. Then state the product's unique ingredients or mechanism.
  • Visitors on a product page are already looking for a solution. Describing what they can see does not help the decision.
  • A description that only lists specifications and what is visible in the image does not sell. Specifications are still needed.
  • The description must also state the primary promise, the benefits, and why to buy this product from this store rather than others.
  • Follow this order for a complete sales page, which is a long page built to sell one product:
    1. Immediate problem: name the problem now.
    2. Consequence of action or inaction: what happens if the buyer acts, and what happens if they do not.
    3. Reframing why the old way fails: reframing means showing the problem in a new light.
    4. New mechanism: the new way the product works.
    5. Who it is for.
    6. Who it is not for.
    7. What you get.
    8. Proof that it works.
    9. Social proof.
    10. Risk reversal.
    11. Comparison against alternatives.
    12. FAQ: a list of frequently asked questions and their answers.
    13. Call to action.
  • Follow this procedure for a complete landing page of product copy. The hero is the big top section of the page. The value proposition is the clear reason to buy.
    1. Build the hero and value proposition.
    2. Surface the problem, make the benefits stand out, prove them with features, show the outcome, and handle objections in an FAQ.
  • For the hero, see the homepage procedure below.
  • Format the description and features on the page so they are easy to read.
  • Put the description first, then the features. This also matches what Google Merchant Center expects. Google Merchant Center is Google's tool for sharing product details with its shopping ads. Search ranking is where your page appears in search results.
  • Optimize bullet points and the product description with compelling sales copy, meaning persuasive wording that makes a buyer act.
  • Format the description with paragraphs, bold text and colours to improve readability.
  • Test and iterate. Tweaks to copy can increase conversion by 25%. Conversion means the share of visitors who buy. (undated)
  • Use expanded, clean product descriptions and clearly formatted specifications. They work better than basic supplier text. A supplier is the company that makes or sells the product to you.
  • Present the specs so they are easy to read. (undated)
  • If competitors (other sellers of the same product) copy and paste the supplier's descriptions, rewrite the descriptions of your best-selling products first.
  • Make them unique and more useful to the buyer. (undated)
  • State clearly what is and is not included. Leaving out details, such as whether light bulbs are included, upsets buyers when the box arrives. (undated)
  • Show exactly how to use, apply or wear the product to reach the dream outcome, the result the buyer wants most.
  • Lack of clear usage instructions is a competitive disadvantage. It makes you lose to rivals.
  • Customers without a clear path to success convert at significantly lower rates than those with detailed usage guidance.
  • Use a simple three-step explanation, such as one, two, three. It makes a product feel easy to use.
  • This removes the objection that the product is complicated.
  • Overcoming objections and explaining how a product works can be combined.

Objection handling

2 min

  • Objection handling means finding the barriers that stop a purchase. A barrier is a doubt that blocks buying.

  • Barriers include doubts about ingredients, quality, price, whether the product works, and whether the buyer believes it will work for them.

  • Answer each barrier on the page with proof, a solution or a reframing.

  • Follow this process for objection handling:

    1. Research the common objections customers have. Cover price, quality, ingredients, safety and believability.
    2. Address each objection on the page. For example, use a full ingredient list, an all natural statement, or proof.
    3. For price objections, reframe in terms of savings. Or spread the benefit over time so it compounds, meaning it builds up.
    4. Use FAQs to answer common questions, such as how the product works, how many uses one bottle gives, and the size of the product.
    5. Use comparison tables to set the product against competitors or against substitutes the customer already uses.

  • Address buyer concerns directly in the product description and in reviews. Identify the main doubts a customer might have, such as a shoe being too tight.

  • Then answer them in the description and in review text. For example, a review states the fit was perfect.

  • This builds trust and speeds up the decision to buy.

  • A serum (a light, thin skin liquid) product page answered five buyer objections in one block, before the add-to-cart button. The add-to-cart button is the button that puts the item in the basket.

    • Whether it hurts or suits sensitive skin.
    • How long results take.
    • Whether it combines with other skincare.
    • What happens if it does not work for the buyer.
    • How long one bottle lasts.

  • Answering these together reduces the hesitation that blocks a first purchase.

  • The FAQ section is a natural place for objection handling. It answers what a buyer needs to know before buying.

  • Typical objections for a premium beauty tool priced around $149 to $299 (a beauty tool is a device for hair or skin):

    • Why it costs more than well-known branded devices or cheap marketplace tools.
    • Whether it really reduces hair damage.

  • Listing these objections in the input metrics (the facts you feed the audit, meaning the review) lets the audit answer them on the product page and in the FAQ.

  • Address the specific objections and regrets a buyer fears before buying. A regret is a feeling of wishing they had not bought.

  • Examples: a setting that feels bulky (the setting is the metal part that holds a stone), stones that look smaller or larger than expected, or proportions that do not suit the hand.

  • Name the common solution the buyer already uses, and tell them to ditch it.

  • Switch to your product for a specific result.

  • End with the benefit and a word of emotional urgency, a word that makes the buyer feel it is now.

  • This surfaces the problem before offering the solution.

    • Example 1: Stop paying for bottled energy drinks full of sugar; switch to our powder for steady energy all afternoon.
    • Example 2: Ditch the late-night takeaway habit; switch to our meal kit for dinner ready in 15 minutes.

  • Use scientific or research claims and the problem-solution framing above as the main tools against doubts about whether the product works.

  • Each doubt needs a reframing or evidence on the page.

Framing, reciprocity and identity

2 min
  • When giving reciprocity gifts, such as free shipping or discounts, frame them as a personal investment from leadership, not corporate promotion. Reciprocity means people feel they owe something back when someone gives to them. Framing means the way a message is worded.
  • The line I'll take that out of my pocket and pay it for you creates a stronger sense of obligation (feeling you owe) than Here's your discount.
  • Reciprocity works because people feel obligated to return value when someone sacrifices for them.
  • In a classic experiment, seventy students received a substitute teacher's biography, which is a short life story. A substitute teacher fills in for another teacher.
  • The two versions were identical except for one phrase. The teacher was described as either warm or cold.
  • Students with the warm description rated the lecture and the teacher much more positively.
  • One framing phrase changed how people saw the same event.
  • Use this pattern: Most [buyers] don't buy [product]. They buy [outcome or offer].
  • It moves the purchase decision from the item to the result the buyer wants. This shifts attention from price to value.
    • Example 1: Most runners don't buy shoes. They buy pain-free miles.
    • Example 2: Most new parents don't buy a baby monitor. They buy quieter nights.
  • Use this pattern: I'm not just getting [product], I'm also getting [bonus or expert value].
  • It shows the stacked value of a bundle (several items sold together) as the buyer's own thought. Stacked value means the extras added on top. This makes the extras feel earned, not pushed.
    • Example 1: I'm not just getting a water bottle, I'm also getting a daily hydration plan.
    • Example 2: I'm not just getting a desk lamp, I'm also getting a simple evening wind-down guide.
  • Formula for a comparison section headline: Not just another [product category]. [A better way to do the job].
  • It reframes the category the visitor already knows. It positions the brand (places it in the buyer's mind) as an upgrade, not one more option.
    • Example 1: Not just another kettle. A faster way to start your morning.
    • Example 2: Not just another desk chair. A better way to get through long workdays.

Bonuses and offer framing

1 min

  • A free ebook (a digital book) can be offered as a bonus. Use this formula:

    • A bonus label that names it as free, for example FREE BONUS.
    • The ebook's specific title.
    • A stated money value.
    • A line saying it is included free today.

    Stating the value makes the gift feel real. The wording avoids the flat feel of a standard inclusion.

    • Example 1: FREE BONUS: Get Our Exclusive Guide "Calmer Mornings In 7 Steps", Value: $14.95, Included Free Today.
    • Example 2: FREE BONUS: Get Our Exclusive Guide "Quick Dinners For Busy Weeks", Value: $12.95, Included Free Today.

  • Writing Includes Ebook as a plain feature makes the bonus sound like a standard inclusion. It loses its perceived value. Name the ebook, label it as a free bonus, and attach a value instead.

Calls to action and urgency

1 min
  • An add to cart button (the button that puts an item in the basket) alone is not enough. It does not tell the reader what to do.
    • Use clear calls to action (CTA: the line that tells the reader the next step) in the copy.
    • Repeat them through the description.
    • Add a time frame where possible, such as delivery within 72 hours.
  • The call to action should only come after the copy has given both the emotional reason and the logical reason to buy. Without that base, a closing button or urgent line has nothing to support it.
  • Urgency hook (a line that makes the buyer feel the offer may not last) in a product description:
    • Connect the problem to an emotional response.
    • State what happens if the customer does not buy now.

  • Split test (show two versions to real visitors and see which one wins) the headline against at least one alternative.
    • Keep the version with the better time on page (how long visitors stay) and conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Test and iterate (test, change, test again) on product copy. Tweaks to copy can increase conversion by 25%. (undated)

Working method and copy structure

3 min

  • Treat product copy as separate parts.

    • Write and rework one part at a time.
    • Then combine them into one page.
    • This makes it easier to improve each part without rewriting the whole text.

  • A complete product copy has six segments (parts):

    1. The primary promise or headline (the main claim).
    2. A "paint a picture" section.
    3. The unique mechanism.
    4. The power benefits.
    5. The proof points.
    6. A call to action.

    Check every product page for all six.

  • Arrange the segments in a fixed order. The finished page then reads as one logical sequence, from promise to call to action, following the segment order above.

  • The segments do not have to be written from top to bottom.

    • Start with the headline, the features and benefits, or the product story.
    • Fill in the other segments afterward.

  • The "paint a picture" segment helps the customer imagine the life or result they want after buying.

    • It sits between the headline and the product mechanics (how the product works).
    • So the reader sees the outcome before the details.

  • The segment method works for a single product page in an ecommerce store (an online shop). It also works for a sales page inside a sales funnel (the path from first visit to purchase).

    • It applies to any product type.
    • The same six segments can be reused in both formats.

  • Writing product copy as one continuous text from start to finish often leads to:

    • Weak transitions (the links between sentences and sections).
    • Missing segments.

    Mapping the blocks first shows gaps before the copy is finished.

  • To practise, run the product mapping exercise. Then write copy with the segment method on the first product.

    • If a team member writes product copy, give them the same method.
    • Have them build the copy from the same blocks.

  • Copy alone does not sell an ecommerce product. It has to work together with product images and video that show the product in use and the benefits it delivers.

    • Strong images without copy, and strong copy without images, both underperform.
    • So plan them as one set.

  • Persuasive copy that beats competitors gives an advantage. Many competitors have good images but weak copy.

    • Improving copy that persuades prospects (potential buyers) more effectively is expected to raise the conversion rate.

  • Copying a weak website or landing page (a single page built to get one action) and expecting good results does not work.

    • The store page needs its own improvement in copywriting (writing persuasive words) and presentation.

  • Copy is one lever (one way to pull results) for raising the conversion rate and sales from existing traffic (visitors who already arrive).

    • It does not work alone.
    • A strong product, good traffic generation, and other on-site conversion work are needed too.

  • Many small and medium-sized ecommerce stores put far less time and effort into their website copy than it deserves.

    • This leaves sales on the table, even when traffic and products are already in place.

  • Ecommerce copy does not need to be expert-level to noticeably increase sales.

    • In most cases it only needs to be more persuasive than what competing stores offer the same customers.

  • Model product and sales copy on the sequence a good in-store salesperson follows. An online visitor has no one to ask.

    1. Identify the buyer's problem.
    2. Clarify the outcome they want.
    3. Give a clear view on how to solve it.
    4. Recommend the specific product.
    5. Ask for the purchase.

    A good product may sell itself. But the copy still has to explain why it fits this buyer.

  • Use headlines pulled from five-star Amazon reviews to find marketing angles (ways to frame the product).

  • Use one benefit-driven headline, one benefit line, and a maximum of two short paragraphs.

    • Use longer copy only for complex products that need education.
    • Otherwise, follow with an image or GIF (a short looping animation), then benefit bullets.

  • AI-generated copy can produce very brief micro-story blocks (tiny story pieces) that do not fill a benefit section.

    • Expand each section's copy so it matches the image size.
    • It should explain the feature.

  • Product descriptions must sell the product with concrete benefits, features, and specifications, including dimensions.

    • A description that only names the product does not persuade buyers.

Product mapping and message sheet

  • Before writing, run a product mapping exercise (a list of what you know about the product) for each product.
    • List which of the six segments the available product information can support.
    • Mark the building blocks that are still missing.
    • Then write the missing blocks and assemble the copy.
  • Once the product map is filled in, pull each answer out of it while assembling the product copy.
    • The primary problem, unique mechanism, feature and benefit pairs, and proof points each go into their matching part of the description.
    • So you refer back to the map instead of writing from memory.

Primary promise and headlines

2 min
  • Landing page headlines work best as a big, bold claim or benefit that states the desired outcome right away.
    • People move toward pleasurable outcomes and away from painful ones.
    • So surfacing the main benefit first captures attention immediately.
  • Headlines on the landing page should be emotional and benefit-driven.
    • The visitor should feel that the product solves a current pain point (a problem they feel now) after reading the headline.
    • Visitors first check the main image, the headline, and the product name before they scroll.
  • Benefit headline formula: [desired outcome] + [a word that signals novelty] + [a word that removes perceived effort or sacrifice].
    • Novelty signals something different from past failed attempts.
    • The effort-reducing word works because the desired goal is usually seen as resource-intensive (costly), slow, or hard.
    • Meal-replacement brand: "The simple new way to lose weight without giving up dessert."
    • Home-office chair: "The fresh, comfortable way to stop back pain at your desk."
  • Benefit headline formula: state the outcome the buyer wants, add an effortless qualifier, then name the everyday result they keep afterwards.
    • Structure: [desired outcome] + [effortless qualifier] + [result they live with].
    • It works because a visitor understands within seconds what the product does for them, and what their life looks like afterwards.
    • "Sleep through the night without waking to a cold room, and start every morning rested."
    • "Keep your kitchen counters spotless with one wipe, and spend your evenings on what you enjoy."
  • Semi-headline: a one-line statement placed directly under the product name.
    • It names the buyer's specific pain or desired result, and signals who the product is for.
    • Visitors arriving on mobile (a phone) decide within seconds whether the product fits their problem.
    • A precise problem statement makes them feel understood.
    • Formula: [specific pain or outcome] + [short promise of how the product delivers it].
    • "Sore feet after every shift? Cushioned insoles that keep you moving all day."
    • "Frizz ruining your style? A leave-in serum that smooths hair in one pass."
  • Test headlines with this procedure:
    1. Identify the audience and the one or two claims that matter most to them.
    2. Write a specific headline that addresses that audience and includes a concrete claim or offer.
    3. Write body copy that contains every important claim, with figures, and a clear next step.
    4. Include a coupon or reply device so the reader can act later.
    5. Test the headline against alternatives while keeping the body copy the same. Keep the winner by cost per customer (what it costs to win one buyer).

Unique selling points and shorthand

1 min
  • USPs (unique selling points, short key benefit lines) work as shorthand for visitors who skim (scan quickly) or already have high intent (are ready to buy).
    • Write them as short benefit lines.
    • They should make the visitor believe they will reach the desired outcome.
  • Add short USP lines below the headline, for visitors who skim or who already have high intent.
    • Each USP should be one quick line.
    • It should reinforce the desired outcome and lower effort or sacrifice.
  • Shorthand USPs are short, benefit-led phrases placed near the top. They are for skimmers and high-intent visitors who do not read the full body copy (the main text).
    • Effective USPs lower effort and sacrifice.
    • For example, they state suitability for all ages, or that no experience is required.
    • This lowers the barrier to entry (what stops a new buyer from starting).
  • Test every USP by asking: does it lower effort or sacrifice on the way to the outcome?
    • Points that do not reduce effort, sacrifice, or risk add little to the page.
  • Remove barriers to entry with USPs such as "suitable for all ages" or "no experience required".
    • They signal that anyone can get started.
    • They lower the perceived effort and skill needed to buy and use the product.
  • Pointing out in advance which qualities to notice makes buyers perceive those qualities.
    • Identical products presented without guidance are chosen almost at random.
    • Name the features that set the product apart, so the buyer sees them.

Unique mechanism and features-to-benefits

2 min
  • The unique mechanism explains why this product delivers its result differently from alternatives.
    • Power benefits and proof points then back up the main promise.
    • So each claim has support on the page.
  • Benefit copy should describe what the product does for the customer to reach their goal, not list product features.
  • Write product copy around the benefit the buyer gets in daily life, not around a list of features.
    • Explain why the buyer would keep reading, and why they would buy.
  • Benefit line pattern: state the feature, then say what the customer gains from it in their own terms (time, money, health, peace of mind).
    • Warranty: a lifetime warranty means the customer never has to pay again if the product fails.
    • Size: a compact unit fits a home, so the customer no longer needs to travel for the same result.
  • Product features should be presented as proof that the promised outcome is achievable, not as stand-alone specifications.
    • Each feature should support the desired result.
    • For example, it shows that the product fits a wide range of sizes, or is comfortable to use.
    • This raises the perceived likelihood of success.
  • Feature-to-outcome pattern: state a feature with a concrete range or measurement, then link it to a benefit that supports the promised result.
    • Backpack: "Fits laptops from 13 to 17 inches, so it works for students and commuters alike."
    • Kitchen knife: "Hand-forged 8-inch blade that stays sharp for months, so prep takes minutes."
  • Feature bullets for a fitness hula hoop should cover:
    • Universal use (fully adjustable design).
    • A wide size range (span 20 to 42 inch for all waist sizes).
    • A soft, comfortable fit.
    • Stability (it does not fall down, unlike conventional hula hoops).
  • A size range of 20 to 42 inch on a fitness hula hoop supports the claim that one product fits all waist sizes.
  • Feature proof example for a sizing product: adjustable design with a 20 to 42 inch waist range to fit all waist sizes, a soft comfortable fit, and a modern design.
    • Each feature answers the question of whether the product will work for this specific buyer.

Proof and credibility

1 min

  • When a promise sounds big, shoppers want proof.

    • Specific features, such as a clinically proven ingredient (one tested in studies), raise the perceived likelihood that the outcome will be achieved.

  • Clinically proven ingredients or claims act as proof. They increase the perceived likelihood of reaching the outcome, especially when the buyer doubts the promise.

    • Add a verifiable proof element (something the buyer can check) when a benefit sounds too good to be true.

  • A study of a large ecommerce store that optimized its product titles (improved product names) reported:

    • About 20% more traffic (visitors).
    • About 70% more ROI (return on investment: profit compared with money spent).
    • A higher conversion rate.

    The gain came from appearing for more specific, relevant searches.

  • Show a specific count of satisfied results to prove a claim.

    • For example: 300 employees who received a raise in one week after studying a course.
    • Or: 280 new bank depositors in one month from circular letters alone.
    • Counts certified by records carry more weight than adjectives (words like "great").

  • A "best choice" badge without any supporting reason means nothing to a shopper.

    • Use badges and claims that describe a concrete benefit the buyer can understand.

Simplicity and three-step setup

1 min
  • Reduce a process to three simple steps to lower the barrier to entry.
    • People understand threes quickly.
    • This avoids cognitive overload (more than the brain can handle at once).
  • Three-step setup format: present setup or usage as three simple numbered steps. This reduces perceived complexity and avoids cognitive overload.
    • Complex processes should be distilled into three clear steps.
    • Water filter: "One: fill the jug. Two: insert the filter. Three: start pouring."
  • Setup procedure:
    1. Show the setup as a simple numbered sequence with exactly three steps (e.g. "one two three let's go").
    2. Keep each step short and concrete.
    3. State that the product is simple to use, so the buyer sees low effort and time required.

Effort and comparison framing

1 min
  • Humans conserve energy. They look for maximum output for minimum input.
    • Presenting less effort and less time for the same result makes the purchase feel like a quick win.
  • People try to conserve effort and get the maximum result for the minimum input.
    • So promises like "less effort, more results" make a product more attractive.
    • Message reduced effort, sacrifice, and time together.
  • Compare your product's cost and results with the alternatives buyers already try, such as supplements or doctor visits.
    • Showing only features does not explain why the product is worth buying.
  • Framing example: 30 minutes of a workout is presented as equal to a two-mile walk in calories.
    • This connects a new value to a reference the buyer already understands.
    • Choose a familiar reference point (distance, time, cost) that makes the benefit easy to grasp.
  • Effort-comparison framing: describe the required activity using a familiar everyday reference the buyer already understands. For example, relate a short workout to the calories of a known walking distance.
    • This makes an unfamiliar result easier to judge and more believable.
    • Supplement: "One scoop gives you the protein of a full chicken breast, without the cooking."
    • Language app: "Ten minutes a day gets you the vocabulary of a semester-long class."
  • Cost-calculation formula: ask the reader to add up what their current method costs, in money and time.
    • The reader reaches the gap themselves before you present your product.
    • A figure the buyer calculates themselves is more convincing than one the seller states.
    • They feel the cost now.
    • Delivery van: add up each month's fuel, insurance, repairs and driver hours for your current delivery van. Then compare that total with the monthly cost of the service offered.
    • Data entry: total what your team spends each month on manual data entry and rework. Then compare that total with the software subscription.

Similarity and social proof

1 min
  • Shoppers quickly check whether others are similar to them, as a threat test.
    • Wording such as "customers like you" signals that the product is meant for people like the visitor.
  • Buyers judge whether someone is a threat by checking similarity.
    • So describing reviewers as "customers like you" signals that the shop's customers are like the visitor.
    • Use this similarity principle in testimonial (customer quote) and review wording.
  • Similarity pattern: [customers like you] + [specific result or time].
    • The similarity cue lowers the feeling of threat and makes the result feel reachable.
    • Calling the buyer part of a group signals that the buyer is not a threat, and that others like them succeeded.
    • Pairing this with a count of satisfied buyers strengthens trust.
    • "Busy parents like you can finish this routine in 10 minutes."
    • "Beginners like you see results in the first week."
    • Skincare brand: "Customers like you saw clearer skin in 14 days."
    • Running shoe: "Runners like you finish their first 10K in 60 days."

Problem, agitation and solution

1 min

  • Start with a pain that the audience already agrees with, such as a universal complaint. Then introduce the product as the fix.

    • Shoppers are more ready to buy when the problem feels familiar.

  • Problem-then-solution structure: state a universal pain point in a short sentence the reader easily agrees with. Then introduce the product as the fix.

    • Dramatizing the result with a vivid metaphor (a comparison picture) adds emotional weight.
    • Bathroom cleaner: "Scrubbing tile is exhausting, so we made cleaning take seconds."

  • Problem-solution copy works well below the hero (the top section of the page).

    • Name a frustrating problem, then present the product as the fix.
    • Keep this section simple.

  • Problem-agitation-solution formula:

    1. State the buyer's problem in clear terms they already agree with.
    2. Stir up the emotions the problem causes, such as anger, fear, guilt or embarrassment.
    3. Reveal the product as the answer, with its benefits.

    People act more readily to avoid pain than to gain something.

    • Backup service: "Your laptop can die tonight and take five years of family photos with it. Every hour without a backup is a gamble, and the losses hit hardest when you least expect them. Our service copies everything every night so a broken drive costs you a coffee, not your memories."

Objection handling

2 min

  • Address objections, such as cheap knockoffs or many competitors, before the buyer raises them.

    • For example, state that imitations are not the same product.
    • Framing the brand favourably against competitors, for example by contrasting results, makes the brand look better in comparison.
    • Handling the objection on the page removes a reason to leave.

  • Objection-handling pattern: name the most likely doubt in a short statement that answers it before the buyer asks.

    • Leather wallet: "Cheap wallets crack in months; ours is built to last ten years."
    • Electric toothbrush: "Most timers are inaccurate; ours buzzes exactly every 30 seconds."

  • Make the buyer feel the offer is fair and not too good to be true, by answering doubts up front.

    • Skepticism often stops buyers who otherwise have the money and interest.
    • So an unaddressed objection costs response, even when the offer is solid.

  • Answer objections in order:

    1. State the direct answer clearly.
    2. Add a supporting testimonial, case history, or short story that proves the answer.
    3. Restate or reference the guarantee or free trial offer.

    Use a FAQ (frequently asked questions) page with the objections phrased as questions, when that suits the format.

  • Objection-framing opener: this turns objections into a sincere, helpful presentation.

    1. Admit that the offer converts only a small percentage of recipients.
    2. Express personal concern that someone might miss out because of an explanation gap.
    3. Mention how many customers praised the product.
    4. Say you held a brainstorm to list the reasons someone would say no.
    5. List each reason, and answer it.

  • Guarantee terms should spell out what happens. The buyer returns the item at the seller's expense and receives a full refund, with no obligation during the trial period.

    • Stating the terms clearly removes doubt better than general reassurance.

  • Guaranteed letter: offer a small payment, such as $10 to $50, to any recipient who reads the entire letter and feels their time was wasted.

    • The payout should be affordable.
    • The message should be strong enough that the reader will want to read.
    • Measure the total cost against the profit from the campaign.

Emotional (feeling-based) and logical (fact-based) reasons

1 min
  • Motive (the buyer's reason to want something) selection checklist, used before writing a sales letter (a written sales pitch):
    1. Ask whether the product would make the buyer richer, healthier or happier.
    2. Ask whether it would raise his standing with the people he respects.
    3. Ask whether it would let him do, write or say something better than before.
    4. Ask whether it would protect his money, time or reputation from loss.
    5. Ask whether it would help the people he loves.
    6. Choose the strongest answer as the main motive and present it only from the buyer's point of view.
  • Present membership or access as an exclusive group (a group only some people can join) to appeal to the reader's desire for recognition and status. This works especially well for premium (top-tier, higher-priced) memberships and premium card-style offers.
  • Present the product or service as a status symbol (a sign of prestige). Ask what it says about the reader if they lack it. For example, ask why they have no fax number when asked for one. Ego (pride and self-image) drives purchase decisions alongside practical benefits. The same appeal can be reused for new technology, such as cell phones or websites.
  • Lead (open) with the buyer's pain (the problem that frustrates them), and put the promise second. Example: a computer-seller sales letter opened by asking whether the reader hated their computer. It listed the frustrations, then made concrete promises (clear, specific offers) such as plain-English talk and a free thirty-minute consultation.

Calls to action (the step you ask the reader to take) and reply devices (tools that make answering easy)

2 min
  • Tell readers what action to take, and make it easy. Offer a coupon (a voucher for money off), a phone number or a simple reply method. Readers forget decisions quickly, so reminders built into the ad (advertisement) improve follow-through (doing what they meant to do).
  • Set the offer's terms (the rules of the deal) in the ad itself. Examples: the free sample, the deadline or the store where it can be picked up. Vague terms reduce response.
  • Include a reply card (a card the reader sends back to order) with spaces for name, address and the item wanted. Add a stamped return envelope (an envelope with postage already paid). Ordering then takes one action. Make the card easy to fill in and clearly marked as the way to order.
  • Include a prepaid (already paid for in advance) return card or envelope with each mailing (a letter or package sent out). Tell the reader to tuck a check (a paper payment) or dollar bill into the letter for a fast order. Make it easy to pay or order with the same enclosure (the item sent inside).
  • Many business readers skip to the last paragraph of a letter. They want to learn what it costs and what to do. So the hook (the line that tells the reader the action) must sit in that paragraph. A close (the ending of a letter) needs two parts. First, the persuasion: it shows the gain, the risk of delay and the guarantee. Second, the hook: it tells the reader what to do.
  • Write the close in this order:
    1. Tell the reader the exact action to take, using the reply card, the envelope or a specific phrase to send.
    2. Say what the reader gets by acting now.
    3. State the specific penalty (the cost of waiting) for not acting, such as a lost price, lost stock or a lost opportunity, with a date.
    4. Give the means to act at once, such as a stamped envelope or a prepaid card, so no step is left to the reader.
  • Letting a prospect (a possible buyer) say "tomorrow" or "later" is one of the largest causes of lost sales. A postponed buyer rarely returns. Give a concrete reason to decide now, such as a deadline or a limited quantity.

Small commitments (small first yeses) and trial steps (small test runs)

1 min
  • Most buyers resist a costly decision and want time to think. So a small commitment now makes the larger decision more likely later. Tell the buyer that the big decision can wait, and ask only for the small step.
  • Ask for a yes on a minor point first, such as color, size, delivery date or a sample. Agreement on a minor point carries the main offer along in about three cases out of four. Make the minor point a clear choice the buyer can make in seconds.
  • Supply the impulse (the urge to buy) in this order:
    1. Identify the motive that makes the buyer want the offer.
    2. Choose a small first action linked to that motive, such as requesting a free trial, a sample or information. Make it cost nothing and oblige nothing (lock the buyer in to nothing).
    3. Make the next step easier than stopping, such as delivering the product with a trial period.
    4. Ask for the purchase only after the buyer has used or handled the product.

Direct-response (built to get an immediate reply) sales letters

2 min
  • Four factors make a reader act immediately. They know and trust the sender. They like the product. They believe the urgency story (the reason to act now). They find the premium (the bonus or top-tier offer) exciting. Write these four factors on a card and check each one in every letter.
  • Fortune-telling formula (a pattern you can copy): make a specific prediction about the future of the buyer's market or situation. Then present the offer as the way to prepare for or profit from it. People are fascinated by predictions and remember bold, provocative ones.
    • Example (a niche is a narrow market; margins are the profit left per sale). Store owner: "Shipping costs in your niche will climb again next spring. Stores that lock in rates now will keep their margins while others cut prices. Here is how to lock in your rates before the increase."
  • Winners-and-losers formula (a pattern that sets two groups side by side): contrast two groups who started in the same place. One acts on the offer and one does not. Present the offer as the difference between them. It creates fear of being in the losing group and motivation to join the winners.
    • Example. Online course: "Two shop owners opened the same month with the same products. One reads the weekly pricing report every Monday and now holds a steady margin. The other guesses, and is still discounting to stay afloat."
  • Use a short question-based lead (an opening made of questions) for a recipient (the person who gets the letter) who is buying something they already own or fear buying. Phrase the questions around the frustration. Then state that you are on the reader's side.
  • Sales sequence (the steps in order) for a high-value product (an expensive item) sold by mail or in person:
    1. Send a series of informative circulars (printed mailings) that show the product's lifestyle benefit (how it improves daily life) without asking for a purchase.
    2. Invite the prospect to a special demonstration or trial with a set date and time. Include the spouse or partner.
    3. Let the prospect operate the product and point out its features during the trial.
    4. Offer a customized option (a version fitted to what they want) that matches the preference the prospect expressed.
    5. Ask for the signature (the sign-off that closes the sale) once the prospect has experienced the product.

Further details

1 min
  • Writing bullets (short, punchy selling points in a letter), five steps:
    • Step 1: Research the subject thoroughly.
    • Step 2: Write far more bullets than you'll need. Write at least 2-3x more than will appear in the final copy.
    • Step 3: Select the strongest, most intriguing bullets only.
    • Step 4: Arrange the selected bullets strategically. Place the most powerful ones at the beginning (front cover, headline area, the top of the page) and at the end (closing, call to action).
    • Step 5: Use themed groups of bullets (groups about one topic). Each group hits the same topic from 2-3 different angles, using different formulas.
