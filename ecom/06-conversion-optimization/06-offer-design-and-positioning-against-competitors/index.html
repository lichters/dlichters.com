Offer design and positioning against competitors

Section 6.6 · 6 of 22 in this chapter · 18 min read

In short

  • Find what your rival stores do badly, then do those things better. Their weak spots are your easiest wins.
  • Check your prices against the minimum price rule (MAP) and the lowest price in Google Shopping before you change anything.
  • Test your offer first. A clear, strong offer sells more than fancy design.
  • Keep choices few: three or four offers, a simple checkout, and a clear returns policy.
  • Do not win on price alone. Win on the whole offer.
More key points
  • A weak product page loses sales, even at a good price.
  • Competitors often have weak pages, so a better page wins fast.
  • Shoppers compare shops side by side. Your page must stand out quickly.

Finding the gap in competitor offers

1 min
  • Find your edge by checking what your top competitors (other stores selling the same things) do poorly. Then improve your store in exactly those areas. Their weak spots are your biggest chances to stand out.
  • Check your top competitors and your own store for these conversion weaknesses. Conversion means the share of visitors who buy.
    1. Low-quality or low-resolution product photos (blurry or small pictures).
    2. Generic or copied product descriptions (vague text, or text taken from others).
    3. Outdated product catalogs (the list of products on a store, not kept current).
    4. Missing product variants, such as sizes and colors (versions of one product).
    5. Unclear or missing shipping and returns policies (the rules for delivery and sending items back).
    6. Poor customer support or no live chat (a chat box where shoppers ask questions instantly).
    7. No promotional offers, bundles or bonus incentives (a bundle is several items sold together; a bonus incentive is an extra reward).
  • Weaker photos, fewer photos, weak descriptions, unclear shipping and returns policies, and poor support are weak spots on product pages (the pages that show one product). Fixing these costs little money but can raise conversion.
  • Common reasons shoppers buy from a competitor: better pricing, bonus offers, better product descriptions, more product photos, more information on the product page, and clearer shipping and return policies. Compare each of these with your store.
  • To decide what to fix first, study the policies, page structure (how the page is laid out) and product page layout of the future competitor with the most traffic (most visits). Traffic rank is a site's position by visits. Use the lowest rank number among your qualifying competitors as the highest-traffic one.

Competitor website review

2 min

Steps for a competitor website review:

  1. Open the top two or three competitor sites by traffic rank side by side.
  2. Compare the homepages (the front page of a site) for similarities and differences, noting free shipping, promotions, live chat, premium shipping options and services.
  3. Find the same product on each site: search by supplier SKU (the supplier's own product code) first, then by product name. If a product is missing on one site, note it as an outdated catalog.
  4. On the product pages, compare price (check for MAP, the minimum advertised price, which is the lowest price a seller may show), reviews, images, shipping and delivery estimate, price match policy (a promise to match a lower price), description and specifications (the technical details).
  5. Add the product to the cart (the shopping basket) and check which payment options are shown before checkout (the buying steps).
  6. Note what competitors do well that you must also do, and what they miss that you can do better.
  7. Prioritize: match the basics first, then build the differentiators (what makes you different) on top.
  8. Repeat the review about every two weeks.

Case: a chandelier niche (a small, specific market for light fixtures), analyzed with two top-ranked competitor sites from product research.

  • Homepages: both competitors showed free shipping on orders over $49. One had live chat and the other did not. One offered white glove delivery (careful in-home delivery and setup) for $150 and Room and Choice service (a paid service named by that site) for $99, with basic threshold delivery included. A threshold is the minimum order amount. Both ran supplier promotions with expiring dates.
  • Planned store: free shipping on all orders, an expiring coupon code (a discount code with an end date) in the header bar (the strip at the top of the site), and negotiated supplier deals (deals won by bargaining) of the same kind.
  • Product pages: a Calco product was missing from one site, which showed an outdated catalog.
  • For a Union matte black 12-light chandelier, both sites sold at the MAP price of $438 and had no reviews. One site showed free shipping with an estimated delivery date, live chat and a visible price match policy. The other did not.
  • Both used the same two supplier images and basic descriptions. One site said nothing about bulbs, while the other stated they were not included. Both hid payment options until checkout step one.
  • Planned response: match the better site's basics, add more images, show estimated delivery, offer bulbs as a free gift or as an add-on (an extra item sold with the main one), and show payment options in the cart.

Competitor product page review

1 min

Procedure:

  1. Pick a product page that receives traffic.
  2. Compare it side by side with the product pages of likely future competitors.
  3. Note where competitors are stronger and where the store has an advantage.
  4. Improve the weak points and keep the strengths.

Positioning against competitors

2 min

Positioning means how you present your store so it stands apart from others.

  • Find the strongest unclaimed position (a way to stand apart that no one uses) after competitive research has identified the standard market positions. Look for an angle with high emotional resonance (it strongly connects with feelings) that competitors ignore despite an obvious market need.
    • Example: every competitor in financial products for married men sells maximize your money, but none claims the check you choose is the check she lives on.
    • Identify which emotional truth (a real feeling buyers have) competitors systematically skip, then build bottom-of-funnel ads (ads aimed at people ready to buy) around it.
  • Compete with large retailers by offering more: better pre-sale product information, a more specific buying experience, service, and sometimes bonus gifts. The small share of sales a niche store takes from giants can still be a multimillion-dollar business.
  • A monoproduct store (a store selling one product) is not automatically a problem. It can be made to look more professional. Competitors in the same market may run general stores (stores selling many kinds of things), so compare yourself against them.
  • Niche-specific stores (stores for one small market) achieve higher conversion rates than multi-niche stores (stores covering several markets), because messaging is clearer and brand positioning as an expert in that niche is stronger. The conversion advantage justifies the extra $29 per month for a separate Shopify store (Shopify is a platform for building online stores).
  • Few competitors offer financing (letting buyers pay over time), so placing it prominently can serve as an extra value proposition (a clear reason to pick you) when a shopper compares two options.
  • Frame the product against competitors in the most favourable light, presenting the alternatives' weaknesses and the product's advantages side by side. Pair this with a social proof number (a figure showing others trust you), such as why do thousands of people choose us, to strengthen the comparison.
  • Competitor product sites with a plain layout, no unique offer and an intrusive pop-up (a box that jumps in front of the page) give shoppers little reason to choose them. A store that adds a clear extra, such as a free accessory or a free gift on the first order, can stand out against these sites.

Benchmark positioning formula

1 min
  • Compare the product against two familiar alternatives (a benchmark is a familiar reference point): one that is cheaper or more common, and one that is premium. Claim that it beats both on a specific dimension.
  • It works because the buyer understands the advantage immediately from known reference points.
  • Example 1, cordless vacuum: quicker to grab than a corded model, stronger than a budget stick.
  • Example 2, travel mug: keeps coffee warmer than a standard flask, lighter than a steel tumbler.

Comparison against the substitute

1 min

A substitute is what the customer already does instead of buying your kind of product.

  • Instead of comparing with other brands, compare with what the customer already does, such as a common alternative. Show that the product is faster, easier, more convenient or more ethical.
    • Example 1: single-serve pods cost more per cup than our bulk powder; our powder is quicker and costs less per cup.
    • Example 2: ready-made shakes contain added sugar; our powder lets you control sweetness and cuts out the extra sugar.

Comparison table against alternatives

1 min
  • Build a feature table (a grid that lists features side by side) with the brand in the first column and two or three competitor categories beside it. Use Yes, No, Sometimes, Usually no and Rarely to show differences.
  • Example layout for a new brand: rows for a unique technology, a durability guarantee and premium positioning, with competitor categories such as generic tools and budget tools.

Offer design

1 min

An offer is the deal you make to the shopper: what they get, for what price, under what terms.

Offer tiers and choice

1 min
  • Present offer tiers (levels of a deal) with minimal visual clutter (little extra on screen), so customers compare options quickly without becoming overwhelmed. Three to four distinct tiers convert better than excessive choice.
  • Presenting more than four offer variations at the same time overwhelms customers, slows decisions and reduces conversion rate compared with simplified choice architecture (the way options are laid out).
  • Keep the checkout simple, such as a minimum order quantity of three or a fixed price for a set, because extra steps make customers think harder and leave.

Friction and value

1 min

Friction is anything that makes buying or getting the result harder.

  • Most sellers focus marketing on the dream outcome (the result the customer wants) and the likelihood of success, but neglect minimizing implementation friction (the effort and hassle of getting that result). Reducing the time required and the effort or sacrifice toward zero multiplies perceived value (how valuable the offer feels) far more effectively than improving outcome promises alone.
  • The consumer's perception of value matters more than the real cost of the add-on (an extra item offered with the purchase). A free add-on bought for 10 cents to 1 dollar can be presented as a $20 value.
  • Conversion gains on a store come mainly from three visible elements: good offers, a strong money-back guarantee, and a free add-on or cheap upsell (a paid extra offered at checkout) presented as valuable. Each must be clearly visible on the site, and the copy (the written words) must read sensibly. FAQ videos on the site help too, but they are secondary.
  • A one-size-fits-all product removes sizing friction. There are no size charts, no confusion and less second-guessing, so customers buy faster and return less. Sizing friction is a major conversion killer (something that blocks sales) for apparel (clothing), and removing it is a strong advantage for testing.

Case: necklace store turnaround

1 min

Starting point (before March 17): earning 2,000 to 3,000 USD per month with a weak offer. Problems: a basic €29.90 necklace-only offer, 21 to 30 day shipping, weak copywriting (the written sales words), no PayPal (an online payment service), and a lengthy checkout.

Changes implemented on March 17 after the owner joined the mentorship and consulted on restructuring the offer:

  • Switched to a US-based supplier (the company that sends the product), reducing delivery from 21 to 30 days to 2 to 3 days.
  • Integrated PayPal one-click checkout (buying with one click, using saved details).
  • Rewrote product copy to emphasize style and heritage.
  • Created a bundled offer: €29.90 for a necklace, a second necklace free, a storage box, a style guide ebook and free shipping.
  • Installed automatic multi-buy discounts, with no codes required.
  • Added a one-click checkout app to skip the cart page.
  • Collected and displayed customer reviews as social proof.

Results: 700% growth in revenue rate (money earned over a period) from April 17 to May 11, and 60,000 USD total revenue over the 4-month period.

Learning: strong offer structure (bundled value, guarantees, social proof) and operational efficiency (fast shipping, a checkout with no hassle) drive sales more than product uniqueness.

Free gifts, bonuses and bundles

1 min
  • Bonus offers create a decisive advantage during comparison shopping. When customers evaluate the same product across several competitor sites, a meaningful bonus they cannot get elsewhere often tips the purchase in your favor.
  • Offering a bonus gift in ads and on product pages can raise both click-through rate (the share of people who click an ad) and conversion rate. Showing the bonus as a clear promotion helps the offer stand out against competitors.
  • A free gift should be profitable at the chosen threshold (the minimum order amount that unlocks it). Place a line such as free gift with this product in the description of best-selling products whose price already exceeds the threshold.
  • Free gift promotions have not caused Google Ads problems (Google's paid ad platform) for this seller. Bonus products are an accepted promotion for conversion rate and average order value (the average amount spent per order).
  • A landing page (a single page that ads send people to) built for one product can still convert well when a bundle of that product is offered, since customers can choose to buy more than one unit.
  • Stand out from competitors selling the same products at the same price by adding an offer they lack. Examples: a gift that costs about $30 to $50 but looks like about $100, expedited shipping (faster delivery for a fee), or white glove delivery. Test each option on customers.

Offer structures seen in live examples

1 min
  • Offer structures seen in live examples included a percentage discount on a first subscription purchase (a subscription is regular delivery on a set schedule), a free gift with a subscription, a bestseller tag on a product page, and a frequently bought with discount on a second item combined with an add-to-cart button (the button that puts an item in the cart). Use offer structures like these to test which offer tips visitors into buying.
  • Other offer and value examples: free shipping as a benefit, 20% off on our new joint care chew when you subscribe today as a hero offer (the main deal shown at the top of the page), and savings that scale with quantity (more units, bigger savings). Use a percentage offer at the top, then a call to action (a button or line telling the shopper what to do next) that scrolls to the product selection.

Offer framing

1 min
  • Move from a temporary promotion headline (the big title on a page), such as Up to X Off, which reads like a sale brand, to a value-anchored headline that names the outcome. Then list included perks (extras) such as free shipping, free gifts and a risk-free guarantee.
  • Example: Protect Your Hair While You Style, followed by a list of premium-quality claims and perks, with a risk-free trial line.

Quantity discounts

1 min
  • Quantity discounts raise average order value and conversion, because customers buy more units at a lower average price. Pairing the offer with a limited-stock message, for example only five left, adds urgency to complete the purchase.
  • Case: a fitness product (stretching band) sold at 29.99 USD with some sales but a weak conversion rate. Adding a free yoga mat to the same order raised the conversion rate sharply, at the same price.

Test offers, not just the product

1 min
  • Test offer variants (different versions of a deal) to find the conversion multiplier (the change that boosts sales most). Case: a home decor test.
    • Offer A, $29.90: failed.
    • Offer B, buy one get one free: failed.
    • Offer C, buy two get two free with an increased price per unit: succeeded, with consistent sales and high repeat customers (buyers who come back).
    • The winning offer had higher perceived value while maintaining margin (profit per sale). Takeaway: with the same creative (the ad design and words) and high CTR (click-through rate), run multiple offer tests to find the conversion multiplier.
  • Offer tests such as a free gift versus a price reduction can both produce sales, but compare the results. In one example, a £5 price cut with the gift removed also produced sales, so test which version converts better.

Sampling

1 min
  • A seller who does not let buyers sample a superior product before purchase loses sales. For products that clearly outperform alternatives, offer a small sample or a try-before-you-buy option (a trial before paying full price).

Differentiating on the same product

1 min
  • Improving conversion without price discounts, in this order:
    1. Ask your supplier for better pricing tiers or negotiating room, before anything else.
    2. Improve product pages with unique descriptions.
    3. Get unique photos and videos from the supplier, and add them to the product pages.
    4. Find competitor weaknesses on the same products and turn them into your strengths.
  • Stand out from competitors selling the same product at the same price. Procedure:
    1. Find the top stores selling the same product at the same price.
    2. Look for weak spots in their product pages.
    3. Fix those weak spots on your own product pages for the same products.

Pricing, price guarantees and MAP

1 min
  • Customers typically comparison shop (check several stores before buying) across three or more websites, searching for the lowest price. This creates decision paralysis (so many options that they stop and buy nothing). A price guarantee removes this friction by letting them purchase immediately while keeping the option to research competitors within 6 months, converting browsers (people who are just looking) into customers.
  • Dropship suppliers (companies that ship products for you, so you never hold stock) enforce Minimum Advertised Price (MAP) policy, requiring all authorized retailers to advertise at identical prices. This eliminates price-based competition between retailers and makes price-match guarantees sustainable with minimal refund payouts (small refunds paid out), since no authorized competitor can legally undercut your price (sell a little cheaper).
  • Secret shopper method for a dropshipping store (checking your own store as a hidden customer would):
    1. Google at least three of your own products and check whether your store appears in the results, without assuming it does.
    2. Use a VPN (a tool that makes your connection look like it comes from another country) set to the US when outside the US, so you see the ads a US customer would see. The example used Utopia.
    3. Compare your price with the lowest price shown in Google Shopping (Google's product price search) and match the lowest price instead of undercutting it.
  • How the two price rules fit together: where MAP applies, all authorized retailers advertise the same price, so there is no lower price to match or undercut. Where MAP does not apply and you find a lower price in Google Shopping, match the lowest price instead of undercutting it.

Small price and offer changes

1 min
  • Small changes can move conversion rate noticeably, for example a $2 price increase or decrease, a free gift, or a bundle saving. Test these small variations rather than relying on one price.
  • Once ads are running, test a new price or offer about every second day, and track conversion rate and customer engagement (how much shoppers interact with the store) on the store. Keep testing until the store reaches a conversion rate of at least 2%.
  • Keeping one price and one offer unchanged for a long period prevents finding the best-performing setting. Continuous price and offer testing is necessary after ads start.
  • If a store receives 150 to 200 sessions (visits) and shows poor conversion, with no add-to-cart events (shoppers putting nothing in the cart), the issue is likely the offer, not the page design. After implementing the page structure and copywriting, change the offer (discount percentage, guarantee, bundle) and retest.
  • Improve a landing page by testing prices, bundles and offers to find the combination that converts best while keeping a good margin. Start scaling (spending more on ads) only once the page turns clicks into sales, because a good CTR alone does not produce sales.

Guarantees, returns and shipping framing

1 min

The offer must be clear and strong

1 min
  • Roughly 40% of the chance that a visitor converts depends on how strong the offer is compared with the offers other sellers in the same market make. The 40-40-20 idea treats the offer as one of the main conversion levers (the things that drive sales).
  • A strong offer sells even with average presentation, while a weak offer cannot be rescued by a beautiful website. Improve the offer before investing in design.
  • The offer must be clear. Its elements must be easy to find on the homepage, product pages and about-us pages, and easy to understand within seconds. The customer should never wonder what something means, whether it applies to them, or how it helps them.
  • Remove fine print (small-print terms), caveats (warnings or limits) and exclusions (things not covered) from offer claims, because they make the offer look weak and reduce its perceived value. Regularly check customer questions to find places where the offer is unclear.
  • The offer does not need to beat the best offers in unrelated markets. What matters is being better on balance than direct competitors selling the same or similar products.

Returns policy as a visible advantage

1 min
  • Case: a seller of oversized home lighting, such as chandeliers, found that buyers hesitated because they could not judge size and fit from product photos. The seller introduced a free returns policy and promoted it strongly, and the change roughly doubled revenue in that first business. Competitors had return policies, but they were hidden in fine print and not shown on product pages, so buyers assumed none existed.
  • Procedure:
    1. Identify the main reason customers hesitate, for example worry about fit, size, or wrong product.
    2. Build a stronger guarantee or policy that removes that specific worry.
    3. Check what competitors offer and how visibly they show it.
    4. Present your policy in clear language on the product page, homepage, ads, about-us page and everywhere else on the site, not hidden in policy pages.
    5. Give the policy a short, distinct name, such as free and easy returns, and use that wording consistently so it becomes your own term.

Free-shipping framing

1 min

Three framings appear in the sources. Each is a different choice, not a conflict.

  • Label as Free Shipping on First Orders even when all customers receive the same benefit. This creates the perception of special treatment and increases order likelihood without changing actual costs.
  • Threshold framing, as used by competitors: free shipping on orders over $49.
  • Free shipping on all orders, as planned for the chandelier store, or bundled into a package offer as in the necklace case.

Product pages and competitive presentation

1 min
  • A product page must win on the total offer against competitors (rival shops selling the same thing). Price alone is not enough.
    • The total offer includes visuals (photos and videos), guarantees (promises that protect the buyer), bundles (several items sold as one deal) and value (what the buyer gets for the money).
    • Improving only the price or the look rarely fixes a low conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Competitors often have weak landing pages (the single page a visitor arrives on first).
    • Improving your page is a fast way to beat them.
    • Focus on high-quality images, a stronger offer and clearer copy (the written words on the page).
  • If a brand's own site (the website of the company that makes the product) is outdated or hard to use, build a better page on your own store and sell at the same price.
    • A better buying experience can win the sale even when the price is identical.
  • If your offer, price or product page is weaker than competing sellers shown beside you, your conversion rate drops.
    • Shoppers open several retailer tabs (browser tabs with different shops) and compare them.
    • Product pages must stand out quickly.
    • Product pages must clearly show the information shoppers compare.
    • Product page conversion work is therefore a must-have for Google Shopping (Google's product ads with photo and price), not an optional extra.
