Playbook › 6. Conversion optimization

Email and phone capture: pop-ups, opt-ins and lead magnets

Section 6.15 · 15 of 22 in this chapter · 34 min read

In short

  • Ask every visitor for an email address, and give something free and useful in return.
  • Put sign-up offers on every page, not only in the footer at the bottom.
  • Never show a pop-up the moment someone arrives. Wait until they scroll or stay a while.
  • Make the offer clear, and make sure the discount code matches what the pop-up promises.
  • An exit-intent pop-up appears when a visitor moves to leave. Offer a discount for their email there.
More key points
  • Run only one exit pop-up at a time. Two at once clash.
  • Keep pop-ups light and easy to tap. Check every one on a phone before publishing.
  • Don't show a sign-up pop-up the moment the page opens. Wait about 10 seconds or until the visitor scrolls.
This section has 9 parts. Tap one to open it.

Why capture contact details first

3 min
  • A free resource is something useful you give away, such as a guide or report.
  • It works best when it matches what the visitor really needs.
  • Capturing name and email turns cold audiences (people who do not know you yet) and warm audiences (people who know you a little) into prospects (people who may buy).
  • Research what the target audience genuinely wants (the people you want to sell to).
  • Then build a free resource, such as an eBook or report, that answers those needs.
  • Include company information and relevant offers next to the useful content. This introduces your business.
  • Older benchmark (older, written 2020, may be out of date): a new store with 100-150 qualified visitors per day performs acceptably. Qualified visitors are people likely to be interested.
  • Five email subscribers from that traffic is below target. It shows that email capture needs optimization (improvement).
  • Collect customer emails for follow-up, meaning emails sent later.
  • This includes abandoned cart recovery (emails to people who left items in their basket) and bounce-back campaigns (emails that bring people back).
  • Email follow-up significantly affects conversions. A conversion is a visitor doing what you want, such as buying.
  • A sign-up pop-up (a box that appears over the page) offers a small discount for an email address.
  • It can raise conversion and grow your list at the same time.
  • Run it from the start of the store.
  • An opt-in is when a visitor agrees to join your email list.
  • Ask every visitor for an email address in exchange for something of value, such as a free report or video.
  • Visitors unwilling to give an email address are unlikely to pay you later.
  • Example: 1,000 daily visitors (people who visit each day) at a 30% opt-in rate (30 in every 100 give an email) produce about 300 interested leads.
  • Few shoppers want another newsletter (a regular email you send).
  • But many will hand over an email address for something free.
  • An opt-in bribe (a free item given for an email address) makes the trade worth it.
  • A new brand selling only on pre-order (taking orders for products not yet ready) struggles to convert.
  • It lacks trust and authenticity (proof that it is real and honest).
  • Collecting sign-ups or notify-me requests is a softer way to capture demand (interest in buying).
  • Capture visitors' contact details.
  • Set up exit-intent overlays (a layer that appears as the mouse moves to leave the page).
  • Visitors who leave can be remarketed to, meaning shown ads again later.
  • An exit-intent overlay captures visitor emails at the moment they leave.
  • It is a core tool for building the list early on.
  • Even with a small ad budget, use an exit-intent overlay to collect emails from visitors who are leaving.
  • Captured emails feed the abandoned cart and promotion emails.
  • Combine a notification bar (a thin banner at the top of the page), an exit-intent overlay and email capture with an automated follow-up (emails that send by themselves).
  • This turns first-time visitors into buyers sooner.
  • Also set up abandoned cart emails.
  • Blog posts (articles on your website) are a good place to collect email leads.
  • Adding an email signup section to the blog posts template (the design file that controls how every post looks) helps build the list.
  • For paid traffic (visitors who come from paid ads), set the lead ad destination to a squeeze page. A squeeze page is a simple page with one job: get the email.
  • The squeeze page offers a free gift, a discount coupon or a popular product in exchange for an email opt-in.
  • Sell offers to opted-in people (people who joined your list) over time by email.
  • Enter the destination URL (the web address the ad sends people to) in the website URL field of the ad.

Where opt-ins go on the site

3 min
  • Every content page should carry a content-based opt-in (an offer tied to that page's topic).
  • Content pages are blog posts, the blog index (the list page of posts), the home page and collection pages (pages that group products).
  • Use a sidebar opt-in (a box on the side of the page).
  • Use an inline opt-in (a box inside the text) below the content on blog posts and collection pages.
  • Use an exit-intent pop-up on all pages except product pages (pages that sell one item).
  • Offer opt-ins on every page of the site. Not only in the footer (bottom strip of the page) or on the home page.
  • Use different opt-ins for different parts of the site.
  • Visitors at different funnel stages (steps from first visit to buying) get relevant offers.
  • The message is not repetitive.
  • Footer-only opt-ins are seen by very few visitors.
  • Only about 20 percent of visitors scroll to the bottom of a page.
  • Scroll-mapping software (a tool that shows how far people scroll) can show this on most sites.
  • Running only one opt-in offer across the whole site is a common error.
  • Visitors arrive at different stages of the buying journey. One offer cannot match all of them.
  • If an opt-in rarely gets sign-ups, it is usually the first thing to fix.
  • Blog layout: make the top third of each blog post a squeeze-style opt-in section.
  • In that section, the only available action is entering an email address.
  • Visitors from search, social or guest content (articles from other sites that send visitors to you) see it first.
  • This turns the blog into a conversion tool rather than only a content page.
  • Place an inline opt-in in the middle of a blog post.
  • An opt-in for a related downloadable guide on the same topic has a high opt-in rate.
  • The reader is already interested in that subject.
  • Place a related content opt-in below the author information at the end of the article.
  • Readers who reach the bottom have just finished the article.
  • They are most interested in the topic.
  • In the Manhattan theme (a ready-made Shopify store design), the email signup section on blog posts can be given an accent color (a highlight color).
  • The change is saved in the theme editor (the Shopify screen for changing the design).
  • It is visible after refreshing the page.
  • Place an opt-in bribe in the website footer, offering a free item in exchange for an email address.
  • Replace the default Shopify newsletter opt-in in the footer or sidebar with a giveaway offer.
  • Keep the same form.
  • Change the text to name a valuable prize.
  • State the entry rules.
  • Add an exit-intent overlay to the website.
  • It appears as a visitor is about to leave, to capture emails or promote offers.
  • Place the consultation or bribe offer next to the opt-in form on the static site (a simple website with fixed pages).

The bribe

2 min
  • A bribe is a free item that visitors already want.
  • You give it in exchange for their email address.
  • Email is easy to share.
  • Visitors only give it when they trust the offer and expect it to be respected.
  • Match the bribe to the stage of the visitor.
  • Someone who has not chosen a niche (a specific market to sell to) yet responds to a list of niche ideas.
  • Someone further along responds to a narrow piece of the paid product.
  • Pattern for a converting bribe: offer one narrow, specific snippet of the paid product (a small, specific part of it) as a free download.
  • It works because the visitor gets proof of value before paying anything.
    • Formula: [specific free piece] + [what it solves] + [quick result]
    • Example 1: A free one-page checklist that shows how to fix your morning routine to cut afternoon fatigue within three days.
    • Example 2: A free 15-minute video on the three fastest ways to lower your monthly phone bill this week.
  • Pattern for a bribe headline: [number of tips] + [specific fast result] + [timeframe].
  • Specific numbers and a time limit make the free offer feel concrete and quick to use.
    • Example 1: Seven budget meal prep ideas that help you cut 150 dollars from your grocery bill in four weeks.
    • Example 2: Five quick desk stretches that ease neck pain in ten days.
  • Physical bribes, such as a free product with a shipping charge, are more expensive.
  • They produce more valuable leads.
  • People who pay for shipping give their full name, email and shipping address.
  • This signals strong purchase intent (a strong sign they want to buy).
  • Case: a company marketed to preppers, people who expect a disaster and buy survival supplies.
  • It offered a credit-card-sized folding knife as a free bribe, and customers paid shipping.
  • The bribe was expensive, but the leads were very valuable.
  • They came with shipping details and a paid order.
  • A free consultation, such as a 10-minute video call on a specific topic, works as a bribe.
  • It works when you have expertise but do not want to create a digital or physical giveaway.
  • It costs your time per lead, so it suits low volumes (small numbers of leads).
  • The opt-in form collects a name and email address.
  • Pair it with a catchy, specific headline that names the free bribe.
  • Quizzes, surveys and giveaways are covered in their own section below.

Opt-in copy

2 min
  • Make every opt-in specific and valuable by naming the exact item the subscriber will receive.
  • Vague promises such as our newsletter and discounts attract almost no one.
  • The visitor cannot judge the value.
  • Each opt-in should solve one specific problem.
  • It should promise one quick win (a fast, visible benefit).
  • Use one call to action (the button or line that tells people what to do).
  • State the problem solved, the benefit and the time frame of delivery.
  • A lead magnet (a free gift that attracts leads) performs best when it solves one specific problem.
  • It needs a specific solution for a specific segment of the market (a defined group of buyers).
  • Name the segment in the opt-in copy so the right visitors recognize the offer as meant for them.
  • Formula: [specific asset the visitor receives] + [the problem it solves] + [how and when it is delivered]
  • Clear copy works because the visitor sees exactly what they get.
  • They can judge its value before entering an email address.
    • Example 1: Enter your email and get our one-page checklist for recovering abandoned carts, sent to your inbox today.
    • Example 2: Join the list and receive our 12-page guide to choosing the right running shoe width, delivered instantly.
  • Email signup headlines should match the brand voice, such as Join Shaka Nation or an equivalent branded call to action.
  • Select a color scheme that matches your global theme (the store-wide design settings) to keep the design cohesive (consistent).
  • Shorter content opt-ins often outperform long ones.
  • A checklist, how-to guide or infographic (a picture that shows information) that solves one problem is easier to accept than a 100-page ebook.
  • People's time is limited, and large downloads put them off.
  • Content opt-in types include guides, PDF downloads of existing blog posts (PDF is a file that looks the same on any device), infographics, ebooks, audio versions of content, checklists, cheat sheets, example collections, resource lists, inspiration files, recipe collections, calculators, spreadsheets, video courses, expert roundups and newsletters.
  • Most can be reused from content already produced for promotion.
  • Formula: take a piece of content, identify the problem it solves, and offer it in a different format as a download behind an email field (a box that asks for an email before the file opens).
    • Example for an outdoor grill seller: Get our top 10 ways to grill fish, with the recipes using our grill accessories, sent to your inbox.
    • Example for a homeware seller (household goods seller): Download 50 living room designs that feature the products we sell.
  • Sign-up success message: remove any line that tells the visitor to use the code to claim an offer.
  • State the code directly, for example with a line like here is your discount code.
  • Keep the confirmation line about subscribing.

1 min

    1. Choose distribution channels (places where you share your offer) for your free resource: blog banner ads, community sponsorships, Facebook ads, or partnerships with industry influencers.
    2. Build the free resource (eBook or report) that addresses your audience's stated needs.
    3. Create a landing page (a single web page built to get one action) to capture email addresses.
    4. Set up email automation in Klaviyo (an email tool) to deliver the free resource and begin the follow-up sequence (a series of emails sent in order).

  • Before a buyer's guide download, use an exit-intent overlay in Klaviyo that asks for an email before the visitor leaves.
  • Set the overlay text to invite the visitor to enter an email for the buyer's guide.

  • A squeeze page is a simple opt-in page that asks for an email address in return for a free report or video.
  • It removes ads, navigation tabs and every other distraction, so the visitor either subscribes or leaves.
  • Its single goal is to convert visitors into subscribers.

Fixed amount versus percentage

1 min
  • A fixed-amount discount (a set dollar value off, such as $5) tends to convert better than a percentage discount for sign-up offers.
  • Shoppers can see the exact value they receive.
  • This is the author's experience, not a measured result.
  • A sign-up pop-up can use a fixed dollar amount instead of a percentage, such as save $5 with your first order.
  • Use it when a fixed amount feels like a clearer and bigger reward to the visitor.
  • The matching discount code must be created in Shopify (the online store software) as a fixed amount with the same value.
  • Otherwise, the promise on the pop-up will not work at checkout (the payment step).
  • Example: a 5-dollar discount for an email address.
  • The headline changes to a question such as Do you want 5 dollars off?
  • The button reads Sign up to get the deal.
  • The coupon is then created in Shopify Discounts (the discount section of Shopify) as an Amount off order fixed amount of 5 dollars.
  • Percentage offers used in practice: 10% for an email sign-up and 15% for an SMS sign-up (a text-message sign-up).
  • Sources give no measured comparison between fixed and percentage offers.
  • Use the fixed amount where the reward is clearer that way.
  • Make sure the Shopify code matches the promise.

Channel-specific and first-purchase offers

2 min
  • Use different discount offers by channel within one pop-up: 10% for email sign-ups and 15% for SMS sign-ups.
  • Name the campaign so the offers are visible in the campaign name.
  • This helps when comparing results later.
  • Discount opt-ins should be used only on product pages.
  • They should be used only for the first purchase.
  • Keep them time-limited, with windows such as 48 hours, 36 hours or one week.
  • Do not make the window too long.
  • Offer a meaningful to exceptional discount and deliver it by pop-up or exit-intent pop-up.
  • Example: a discount opt-in on a product page shows a 15% off next-order offer with an email field.
  • It also shows a Be the first to know about new products line and a No thanks option.
  • Welcome offer only for new leads: filter for Amount spent equals zero dollars (a rule that picks customers who have not spent money).
  • The welcome offer goes only to new leads, not previous customers who already know the brand.
  • Example: a merch store (a shop selling branded clothing and items) set the pop-up to display after 60 seconds.
  • It offered discount code save 10.
  • It automated welcome emails to new subscribers.
  • It automatically excluded customers with abandoned cart tags (labels on customer records) and prior purchase history (past orders).
  • An exit-intent overlay should offer something different from the header discount (the discount banner at the top of the page).
  • Do not repeat the same percentage in the overlay.
  • Visitors already see it and have no reason to enter their email.
  • A vague wording such as enter your email for a special discount also works.
  • The Coupon Pop app for Shopify (an add-on that shows pop-ups) can show an expiring discount pop-up, such as 10 percent off, to collect email addresses.
  • The same offer can stay in the store header.
  • The pop-up gives visitors a reason to leave their email instead of exiting.
  • Older rule (2020): an exit-intent overlay that offers a fixed discount or coupon (a code for a set saving) in exchange for an email address is standard on large e-commerce sites.
  • Giving a $50 coupon for signing up to a mailing list is legal.
  • Offer must be stated clearly in the headline.
  • A pop-up with a clear offer converts better than a prettier pop-up without one.

Timing, triggers and frequency

3 min
  • Never show a pop-up or any overlay on page load (the moment the page opens).
  • Do not show an email pop-up immediately on page load.
  • Older rule (2021-22): do not show intrusive pop-ups or interstitials (overlays that cover part of the page) within the first 30 seconds of a visit.
  • This includes discount or newsletter pop-ups.
  • Triggering immediately is penalized by Google (Google ranks such a site lower).
  • Discount pop-ups should be shown only to visitors who show real interest in the offer.
  • Google can see page code loaded by pop-ups and interstitials that cover a significant part of the screen when it crawls a page (when Google reads a page).
  • Google may deprioritize such a page (rank it lower).
  • Such overlays also reduce the chance that visitors stay.
  • An interstitial is any overlay that covers part of the page.
  • It includes discount or newsletter pop-ups and intrusive chat widgets (chat boxes) that open as large bubbles.
  • Keep both kinds off the screen during the first 30 seconds of a visit.
  • Avoid showing the pop-up at the very start of the visit.
  • Showing the pop-up immediately when a visitor arrives annoys them and makes them leave.
  • Let them scroll or stay on the page first.
  • A scroll-depth rule (a rule based on how far down the page someone scrolls) works as an engagement filter (a rule that shows the offer only to interested visitors).
  • A visitor who has read about half the page is more likely to want a discount or need reassurance.
  • So the offer appears when interest is already shown.
  • Pair the scroll rule with a delay of about 10 seconds.
  • Show the pop-up only when all selected rules are met.

Exit-intent overlays

18 min
  • Exit-intent overlay (a pop-up that appears when a visitor moves to leave) catches visitors who would otherwise go without signing up or buying.
  • A bounced visit is when someone lands on the site but does nothing within a set time (a "bounce" is a quick exit). They may be distracted, on a call, or have the site open in a background tab. Interrupt them with a pop-up that trades a discount code for their email address. This gives you another way to contact them before they leave.
  • Set the email pop-up to show after 60 seconds of visitor inactivity on the same page (no clicks or page changes). This catches them before they close the tab.
  • Page coverage conflicts between sources:
    • Course A-style setup: the email pop-up shows on all page types: home page, blog posts, collection pages (lists of products) and product pages. Use this when the goal is maximum capture across the store.
    • Older rule (2021-22): exit-intent on all pages except product pages. Use this when the product page should stay free of overlays.
  • Bounce visits setup: set up an exit-intent overlay that captures visitor email addresses before they leave the store.
  • Setup procedure in Source E's pop-up tool:
    • Go to Trigger (the setting that decides when a pop-up shows), then Add new trigger.
    • Choose On exit-intent (When a visitor is about to leave your site).
    • Set Device to Desktop or Both devices.
    • Exit-intent catches visitors who leave without buying or signing up. It works on both mobile and desktop.
  • Use an exit-intent pop-up as the last chance to catch a visitor who is about to leave. Show it only when the visitor moves to leave.
  • An exit pop is a last-chance pop-up that appears when a visitor tries to leave without subscribing. It can include a special offer for subscribing. Departing visitors rarely return, so being a little intrusive here is acceptable.
  • Do not run two exit-intent pop-ups at once, such as one from the theme (the store's design template) and another from Klaviyo (an email marketing tool). Keep one and turn off the other. (2023)
  • Manual follow-up for opt-ins (an opt-in is a visitor agreeing to get your emails): if no exit-intent overlay is running, set one up. Then manually email everyone who opts in yourself (not automated messages) with the following text. It builds the relationship and recovers sales:

### Pop-up design

#### Visual and layout rules

- Baseline case: a starting pop-up with a **0.4%** conversion rate (the share of visitors who take the action) and 16,000 views produced only 71 email submissions. This baseline shows the value of a stronger visual pop-up design.
- Use a lighter pop-up tool than the Klaviyo embed. An embed is a piece of code that adds a tool to the page. OptiMonk (a pop-up tool) was chosen for this reason. Heavier pop-up scripts slow page load time, and **page speed** (how fast the page loads) affects conversion.
- Make pop-up buttons and inputs (the boxes people type into) very clickable:
  - Use **display manual** (a setting that lets you set the size yourself) with a fixed width of about **400 px** (pixels, screen units).
  - Set the height to **50 to 70 px**.
  - On mobile, keep buttons larger than inputs.
- Match the pop-up button and input shape, color and shadow to the store's own product-page buttons. The pop-up then feels like part of the site.
- Make the floating product image and text legible and eye-catching. A floating image that breaks out of the pop-up frame attracts attention. A **text shadow** (a soft dark edge behind letters) improves readability on busy backgrounds.
- The image on a sign-up pop-up should relate to the **niche** (the market your store serves) or the product, or show a gift. It sits beside the discount offer and supports it. Replace the default image in the **Styles** section (the design settings) before you publish the form.
- **Check every pop-up on mobile view.** Adjust sizes separately for desktop and mobile, because layout, button size and font size often need different values on each device.
- **Check the sign-up form on a mobile preview before publishing.** Most visitors in a mobile-heavy store arrive from phones. A form that looks fine on desktop can be hard to use on a phone.
- Pure black on a pop-up background can look fake and digital next to a website. A **dark charcoal** tone (a soft near-black grey) blends in and looks more natural. Pick the shade by eye beside the store's own colors.

#### OptiMonk settings

- **Layering** (which item sits on top): give the email input fields and the Reveal Code button a higher **z-index** (the layer number; higher means on top) of about **10**. The floating image gets about 1. This stops the overlay image from capturing clicks.
- **Padding** (empty space inside a box):
  - First, zero out any leftover column spacing.
  - Then open **Column > Advanced**. Set left and right padding to **0**, and top and bottom padding to **50 px**.
  - If the result looks cramped or too loose, test **100 px** on top and bottom. Keep the value that looks more even.
- **Text shadow**: set it under **Style > Text shadows**, using a **medium** shadow on all text blocks. A light shadow looked too weak on a sandy background. The same shadow everywhere keeps readability consistent.
- **Hero image** (the large main background picture): for a full-width hero background, OptiMonk's image fit options are under **Style > Image**. They are:
  - **cover** (fills the box, may crop the picture)
  - **contain** (shows the whole picture, may leave gaps)
  - **original** (keeps the picture's own size)
  - An alignment choice can also be adjusted. In the tested pop-up, the **original** fit looked most natural. Compare fit modes visually before you settle on one.
- **Container shadow**: give the whole pop-up container (the outer box) a soft shadow with a medium size and about **30%** opacity (how solid the shadow looks). This lifts it off the page. Keep the opacity low, because a heavy shadow makes the pop-up look dated.
- **Mobile layout procedure:**
  1. **Switch the OptiMonk editor to mobile view** and review the pop-up before changing anything.
  2. Select the floating image, go to **Display > Position**, and set it to **Top**.
  3. Select the column and set left and right padding to **0**. Then add back about 25 px if the content touches the edges.
  4. Select **Main page** and set its size to **95%** of the screen.
  5. Set mobile inputs to a fixed width of about 400 px and a height of about 50 px. Set the button to about **60 to 70 px** high.
  6. Raise any headline or button text that is too small on mobile. The tested pop-up used sizes around **125 to 150**.
  7. Adjust the desktop and mobile layouts separately, because they often need different values.
  -
- **Preview caveat**: a pop-up preview may not show on some themes. An OptiMonk pop-up also does not appear until the **app embed** (the switch that turns the tool on) is enabled in the **Shopify** theme settings. Shopify is the online store platform this store runs on. **Check that the embed is on** before assuming the pop-up is broken.

### Pop-up tools and platform setup

#### Choosing a tool

- Tools for pop-ups, exit-intent forms, inline forms (forms placed inside the page text) on content pages and sidebar forms (forms in a side column) include OptinMonster and OptiMonk. Other options include Sumo and Just You Know.
- OptinMonster is used for all pop-ups, including exit-intent, timed, inline forms and opt-ins. It integrates with Shopify, meaning it connects directly to the store.
- The key criterion when choosing a **provider** (the company that supplies the tool) is its **impact on page speed**. Some scripts (small pieces of code that run on the page) load slowly and hurt site performance. Privy is named as an example of a slow tool with problematic script loading. **Avoid it or switch away from it.**
- Built-in forms from email platforms such as Klaviyo are weaker than dedicated form tools. Email software is not built around forms and pop-ups, so those features feel like an afterthought. **Prefer a separate specialized form tool.**
- Conflict: the 2021-22 advice prefers a dedicated tool. The Source C method (a named set of steps) builds the sign-up pop-up in Klaviyo. Use **Klaviyo's sign-up form** when it meets the page-speed test. Use **OptiMonk** when the Klaviyo embed is too heavy.
- Pop-ups can still collect email addresses if used carefully. However, ad blockers (browser tools that hide ads) and some ad networks (companies that place ads) reject ads that send traffic to pages with pop-ups. Newer pop-up formats are harder to block than older ones. **Check the advertising platform's policy** (its rules) before relying on them.

#### Klaviyo sign-up form

- Choose **Pop-up**, not Full page or Flyout (a box that slides in from the edge). Pop-up has the highest conversion.
- Choose **Email only** as the form's channel. Select the **Newsletter** list so new addresses land in the newsletter list.
- **Teaser** (a small tab that stays on screen after the pop-up closes): keep the teaser **off** by default. A teaser that stays at the bottom of the screen annoys mobile visitors. Turn it on only when the sign-up form conversion rate drops.
- **Targeting** (who sees it and when): under **Show based on rules**, show the pop-up after **10 seconds** on the page. Add the rule that the visitor has scrolled **30 to 50 percent** of the page. Set the condition to show only when **all** selected rules are met.
- Creation procedure:
  1. **Click Create**, choose the email-only option and select the Newsletter list.
  2. Choose the **Pop-up** form type and click **Next**.
  3. **Set the teaser to off**, unless the sign-up rate drops.
  4. Choose a **static coupon** (one fixed code for everyone) for the incentive. Enter the code created in Shopify (for example `WELCOME10`).
  5. Open **Edit form** and adjust the layout. Check the mobile version, because most visitors come from mobile devices.
  6. Open **Styles** to change the image, text and styling options.
  7. Open **Targeting** and set the rules (see the targeting setting above).
  8. Edit the copy: headline, email field text, button text and decline link (the "no thanks" link).
  9. Open the **success step** (the message after signing up). Remove the line about claiming the offer. Add a line that gives the discount code directly.
  10. Change the status from **Draft** (not visible to visitors) to **Live**. Then click **Exit**.
  11. **Preview the form on a mobile device** before finishing.
  -
- Mistake: showing the sign-up pop-up the moment a page loads is too aggressive and annoys many visitors. A delay of **about 10 seconds** combined with a scroll condition gives better results.
- Coupon pop-up copy: use the Klaviyo success message and the Shopify code as described above.

#### Targeting, segments and reuse

- **Targeting**: under **Select who should see the pop-up**, choose a predefined segment (a ready-made group of visitors). For example, `Non-subscribers` stops people already on the list from seeing it. Other predefined options include Now visitors, Active shoppers, All visitors, Engaged visitors, New home page visitors, Just purchased and Custom targeting. Saved segments and Copy from previous campaign are also available.
- In the targeting step:
  - **Load segment** opens your saved segments.
  - **Save as segment** stores the rule you built so it can be reused in other pop-up campaigns.
  - **Copy from previous campaign** reuses targeting from an existing pop-up.
  - Save a segment when the same audience rule is needed across several pop-ups.
- Welcome pop-ups can be limited to new visitors only. This requires a custom code addition (code you write into the theme). (2023)

### Shopify automation after capture

- Set up a **Shopify Email automation** (emails sent by themselves, built in Shopify). Trigger it by bounced-visit pop-up submissions. It automatically sends captured contacts a coupon code. At the same time, it forwards their contact information to your email for manual follow-up.
- The bounce visits setup is the exit-intent overlay that feeds this automation.
- Captured emails feed **abandoned cart** (a cart left unpaid) emails and promotion emails.
- Over time, feed contacts from exit-intent overlays into **automated flows** (automatic email sequences).
- **Remarketing** (showing ads or emails again to people who left): visitors who leave can be remarketed to once their contact details are captured.

### Phone capture and multi-step forms

- An extra incentive, such as an additional **5% off**, can be offered in exchange for a phone number. This works because the visitor is already looking for a better deal when they try to leave.
- **Trojan Horse Method** (a way to collect email and phone number in one pop-up):
  1. **Ask for the email address first.**
  2. Once the email is captured, offer the visitor the option to upgrade by also adding a phone number.
  3. If the visitor adds a phone number, give them the upgraded offer. It is a discount **double** the basic one.
  4. If the visitor declines, keep only the email. Send the basic offer they signed up for.
  -
- Asking for the email first guarantees the email capture. The optional phone upgrade lets the visitor choose their own reward path. The basic sign-up is never blocked by the phone request.
- **Two-step form** (a form split across two screens):
  - Step one collects a name and email address. This qualifies the visitor as a subscriber even if they leave.
  - Step two collects credit card details to cover shipping in a **free-plus-shipping** offer. The product is free, and the buyer pays only shipping.
- Case: a Klaviyo pop-up on a store with about 16,000 views produced only 71 email sign-ups. That is a conversion rate of about **0.4%**. The rebuild used:
  - an OptiMonk pop-up with a floating product image,
  - a **10%** email discount,
  - a follow-up **SMS** (text message) upgrade to **15%**,
  - TCPA-style consent text under the button. TCPA is a US law about text messages. Consent text is the line where the visitor agrees to get texts.
  
  The reported result was over **30,000 USD** in under 10 days. This is a self-reported figure and not independently verified.

### Quizzes, surveys, contests and giveaways

#### Quizzes

- A **pre-frame** (a warm-up step before the offer) quiz engages visitors with personal questions that point toward the offer. Use **three to four** numbered questions, such as `Step 1 of 4`, to show progress. Some successful quizzes run to 20 questions. Requiring an email address to see the results combines the pre-frame with **subscriber qualification** (sorting real, interested people into your list).
- A quiz can **segment** the audience (split visitors into groups) by answers. Asking whether a visitor owns a dog or a cat splits the list. A dog owner can then be asked how old the dog is and shown answers by age range. The next ad can then show a dog and recommend food suited to that dog's age.
- Quizzes and surveys can run on any page: home page, product pages, collection pages and blog posts. They can appear as pop-ups or as fixed items such as sidebar or inline forms. Home, collection and product placements suit pop-ups. Collection, blog and blog post pages suit sidebar items. **Each quiz or survey should have its own list.**
- Quizzes and surveys work because they are fun or engaging. They carry no pressure to buy, so they can reach very high opt-in rates. The quiz does not have to be about the products. It only has to relate to the niche and interest the visitor. Example: a surfboard seller can use `Which world surf pro are you?` and later move these subscribers toward surfboard products.
- Tools:
  - **Typeform** was used to build quizzes and surveys.
  - **Qzzr** (Quizzer) is an alternative for quizzes.
  - **Google Forms** (a free tool from Google) is a simple alternative for surveys.
  - Quizzes run on the page or in a **lightbox** (a box that opens over the page). The visitor enters an email address at the end to see the results.
- **Quiz formula**: `Which [recognizable figure or category from your niche] are you?` Follow it with a short quiz that the visitor finishes to see the result. The question invites **self-identification** (seeing yourself in the answer) and fun. Visitors give their email to see the answer, even when the quiz does not mention products.
  - Example 1: `Which camping style matches your next trip?`
  - Example 2: `Which coffee brewing method fits your morning routine?`
  -
- Keep a quiz short. Use only a few simple inputs before the email step, so visitors finish quickly. The email request should come **after** the quiz has already delivered some engagement.
- A quiz works as a **lead magnet** (a free thing given in exchange for an email) when the results are revealed only after the visitor enters an email address. This turns the quiz into a fast way to grow the list with people who searched for related products.
- A quiz built to capture emails can be linked from several places on the store. Examples are collection pages and the home page, not only from ads. For **paid search** (ads shown on search results), use a dedicated quiz page as the **final URL** (the page the ad link opens). Then the ad headline and the **landing page** (the page a visitor arrives on from an ad) match.

#### Surveys

- A survey asks visitors questions about their industry and preferences. Turn the results into a content piece such as `We surveyed 100 people in this industry`, which is likely to be shared. Send each **respondent** (person who answered) the results **before publishing**, so they see how their views compare.

#### Contests and giveaways

- Small contests and giveaways generate large numbers of opt-ins. The prize does not need to be the main product. Cheap items that sell for around $100 can be bought and given away. Offer contests on any page through pop-ups, exit-intent pop-ups or sidebar forms. Promote them on social media to grow entries.
- Giveaway prizes can be set so that one prize is awarded for every set number of opt-ins. For example, **one prize per 1,000 opt-ins**. Even a 500 dollar prize can remain profitable under this rule, because collected emails are cheap to market to later.
- Giveaway opt-in text for the newsletter box. Replace the placeholders in square brackets:

Enter your email to win [PRIZE, for example a 200 dollar gift card to our store or our best-selling product] at [STORE NAME]. We will give away one [PRIZE] for every [NUMBER, for example 1,000] people who opt in. Full rules: [LINK OR TEXT WITH RULES].


- **Replace the default newsletter opt-in** with a giveaway offer. Name a valuable prize, such as a 200 dollar gift card or your best-selling product. State the entry rules.

### Sales event and other capture formats

- Sales event opt-ins offer **presale** (early buying before the sale opens) or exclusive access to a big sales event. Examples are Black Friday, Easter or Veterans Day. They can appear as pop-up or exit-intent on any page. They can also appear as sidebar items on collection, blog and blog post pages. Each sales event should have its own list. **Show them only in the one or two weeks before the event.**
- **Squeeze pop** (a pop-up that asks for an email before the visitor can move on): a button placed in blog posts or articles opens a squeeze-style pop-up when clicked. A visitor who enters an email address moves to the next page. Squeeze pops work anywhere a button can be placed. This includes places where normal opt-in forms would not fit.
- **Free account**: gives visitors access to a membership or a lite version (a smaller, free version) of your software in exchange for signing up. Free accounts are similar to squeeze pages. They collect more personal information, such as a physical address and phone number, while keeping conversions high. Use them for software and membership products.
- **Webinar registration** (signing up for an online talk, live or recorded) is a natural way to collect email addresses. Registrants expect follow-up emails with details. Automated webinars let people pick the day they want to watch. After entering an email address, they become subscribers. Registrants are also **primed** (ready) to look for your emails.
- Consultation as a bribe (a free offer used to win an email): see the bribe section above.

### Page speed and technical considerations

- Choose the pop-up tool by its effect on **page speed**, not only its features.
- Use a lighter pop-up script than the Klaviyo embed where page speed is a concern. Page speed affects conversion.
- **Check that the pop-up embed is enabled** before assuming a pop-up is broken.
- Overlays that cover a significant part of the screen can be seen by Google. They also reduce the chance that visitors stay. **Keep overlays off the first 30 seconds** of a visit.
- Pop-ups and overlays can be blocked by ad blockers and some ad networks. **Check the ad platform's policy** before sending paid traffic to pop-up pages.
- Measurement: use **Lucky Orange** (a screen-recording app that shows how visitors use the site). Watch visitor sessions to find the **average time on site** needed for pop-up timing.
- **Do not add Google reCAPTCHA** (a tool that checks whether a user is a robot) to an email sign-up form to block spam. It can reduce opt-in rates. Instead, monitor new sign-ups and delete obvious spam manually. (2024)

### Compliance and consent

- Give a fixed-amount or percentage coupon only where the **platform terms** (the platform's rules) and local rules allow it. Giving a $50 coupon for signing up to a mailing list is legal.
- **SMS upgrades** carry TCPA-style consent text under the button.
- Ads and pop-up policies: **check the advertising platform's policy** before relying on pop-ups in paid campaigns.

### More rules worth knowing

- Portugal candles brand: **€1,000** total Black Friday ad budget for the France market. The brand used a list-building strategy with landing page opt-ins. It achieved **€2.89 cost per lead** (the price paid for each new contact). After €3,000 total investment, it was still acquiring leads at this low cost. This shows that list building remains profitable even when **CPCs** (cost per click, what each ad click costs) spike to **€20+**.
- Set the pop-up targeting to **Show based on rules** with two triggers:
  - **Exit intent** (when the visitor is leaving the page).
  - A **delay of 10 seconds** after the page loads.
  
  The delay gives visitors time to scroll before the offer appears.
