Playbook › 6. Conversion optimization

Prioritizing and running the optimization plan

Section 6.20 · 20 of 22 in this chapter · 22 min read

In short

  • Start conversion work when the store makes its first sale, or before that once it gets about 40 visitors a day.
  • If sales are slow at 40 visitors a day, fix the store before you spend more on ads.
  • Work on the product pages that already get visitors first. Start with the top 20 percent.
  • Change one thing at a time, test it, and keep it only if the numbers improve.
  • Turn off an ad that spent $10 or more with no add-to-cart or sales in its first 24 hours.
More key points
  • Spend more on ads only when each sale still makes a profit after ad costs.
  • Before you pay more per click, first raise the share of visitors who buy.
  • Once Google Shopping stops being profitable, you can only improve it so far. Then focus on other channels.
  • If ads bring visitors but few buy, fix the store first, then spend more.
  • Fix big store problems first, then run many small tests.

Entry conditions: when conversion work starts

1 min
  • Conversion work means changing the store so more visitors buy. The conversion rate is the share of visitors who buy.
  • Start as soon as the store makes its first sale. That sale shows the checkout works and customers want the product.
  • Before any sale, start once the store reaches about 40 unique visitors per day. Unique visitors are different people, counted once per day.
  • At 40 unique visitors per day, the store should make at least one sale every other day.
  • If it does not, fix the conversion problem before you spend more on traffic. Traffic means visitors who come to the store.
  • Run these basic checks first:
    1. Confirm Google Product Listing Ads are running. These are paid Google ads that show your products with photo, name and price.
    2. Confirm Amazon Product Listing Ads are running. These are paid product ads on Amazon.
    3. Confirm the store has at least 50 products.
  • Action plan, in order:
    1. Check traffic and confirm at least 40 unique visitors per day.
    2. If traffic is low, confirm Google Product Listing Ads and Amazon Product Listing Ads are running.
    3. Set up free bonus gifts for the top three products.
    4. Make the changes. Brainstorm on paper first, or log into the shop and make them.

Order of operations

1 min

Work in this sequence. Each step points to the section that holds its full detail.

  1. Confirm entry conditions. Traffic, basic ad checks and the 50-product minimum from the entry section come first.
  2. Audit the store as a paid visitor sees it. Run the cold-visitor review first. Then run the full-funnel audit at about one and a half months in. A funnel is every step from the ad click to the paid order.
  3. Choose where to focus. Rank products by traffic. Work on the products that already get visitors.
  4. Build the first product page, then duplicate it. Build the page structure on one product page first. The add-to-cart ratio is the share of visitors who press "add to cart." Once that ratio meets the platform minimum (the lowest level the platform expects), copy the page for the other products.
  5. Run the conversion setup in order. Follow the setup sequence.
  6. Roll out the marketing message in stages. The marketing message is the words and offer that sell a product. Start with best-sellers, test, then extend.
  7. Build the test backlog and run tests continuously. A test backlog is a to-do list of tests waiting to run.
  8. Hold the weekly routine.
  9. Change scale or strategy only on evidence. Scaling means spending more on what works. Cutting means stopping what does not. Maintenance means regular upkeep. Use the scaling, cut and maintenance rules.

Cold-visitor review

1 min

Older (2021-22)? No. Source: Source A, undated.

A cold visitor is someone who has never seen the store. Review the store the way a cold visitor from a paid ad sees it:

  1. Note the product and offer promised by the ad that brought the visitor.
  2. Open the homepage and check within a few seconds whether it shows the same product type. Check whether a stranger can tell what the store sells.
  3. Open a product page and check these points:
    • The featured image (the main photo) shows the product clearly.
    • The description sells it with benefits, features and dimensions.
    • Reviews, stock count, delivery estimate and live chat link are visible. A live chat link is a button to chat with support.
  4. Check the tabs or sections on the product page for the description, the shipping policy and any price match policy. A price match policy promises to match a lower price elsewhere.
  5. Scroll to the footer (the bottom of the page). Check that these are present and point to relevant pages: get-in-touch details, support hours, support email, live chat link, social media links and policy links.
  6. Check that the social media accounts exist and show recent activity.
  7. Write down every point where you would hesitate to buy. Fix those points first.

Full-funnel audit at about six weeks

1 min
  1. Audit the whole store once it is built and ads are running, around one and a half months in.
  2. Go through every page and every step of the funnel, from the ad click to the completed order. Include minor details that tutorials may not cover.
  3. Look for small problems that cause drop-off (people quitting at a step), such as a missing element or an unclear step. Do not wait for someone to report low conversion or many abandoned carts. An abandoned cart is a customer who added items and left before paying.

Standing assumption

1 min
  • Assume your store has issues that need constant fixing. Even large companies have teams fixing bugs and reports every day. Review your store regularly with fresh eyes, and check trends in your data.

Conversion setup, in order

1 min

Shopify is the online store platform used in these steps.

  1. Install an urgency app (an app that shows pressure to buy now) and a review app (an app that collects and shows customer reviews) on Shopify.
  2. Modify the content page templates. Templates are page layouts.
  3. Create Facebook and Instagram business pages.
  4. Install Klaviyo on Shopify. Klaviyo is an email and messaging tool.
  5. Set up automated emails and actions for bounced visits (visitors who leave right away) and abandoned carts.
  6. Apply for a financing app (an app that lets customers pay in installments).
  7. Complete all remaining conversion action tasks.

Marketing message rollout

1 min

Source B.

  1. Start with the best-selling products, not the whole catalogue.
  2. Test the new descriptions against the old ones. A split test shows two versions to different visitors and keeps the one that sells better.
  3. Optionally, run conversion rate tests and compare the results.
  4. If the new approach works, implement it across the rest of the products.

Seven store optimizations for a winning product with no sales

1 min

Run these in this order when a winning product (a product with strong demand) gets traffic but no sales:

  1. Split-test the landing page design or theme. A landing page is the page ads send visitors to. A theme is the store's design template.
  2. Test prices.
  3. Test lead magnets (free gifts that draw people in).
  4. Add automatic discounts.
  5. Improve copywriting. Copywriting is the written sales words.
  6. Improve the product description and trust signals. Trust signals are things that build trust, such as images, GIFs (short looping animations) and reviews.
  7. Break down ad data by placement (where the ad showed), gender, age and country before relaunching. Relaunching means starting the ads again.

Store optimization sequence (alternative order)

1 min

Source: Source C. No trigger stated. See the conflicts section.

  1. Test different themes.
  2. Test different bundles. Bundles are products sold together as one package.
  3. Test different prices.
  4. Add a clear sense of urgency.
  5. Improve the copywriting on the store.
  6. Once the conversion rate is good, keep scaling the product.

Mistake to avoid: Do not leave the store unchanged from day one while only adjusting ads. Working on the store is part of testing every product.

Product pages first

1 min
  • Improve product pages where you already get traffic. This raises conversion rate without extra ad spend. It also improves ROAS (return on ad spend: money earned from ads divided by money spent on ads). This is the highest-leverage starting point, meaning the step with the biggest effect for the effort.
  • Improve product pages for products that already convert before you scale their traffic. Better pages turn the same clicks into more sales.
  • Optimize the top 20 percent of product pages first. They usually produce about 80 percent of sales. Then work through the remaining products.
  • Optimized product pages support free Google traffic and Google Ads clicks and conversions. Google Ads is paid Google advertising. So optimizing products that already get traffic gives the fastest return.
  • Start on the pages that get the most traffic, since these produce the fastest gains. Check Google Analytics (a free tool that tracks visits and sales) for your most visited pages before you decide what to change.

Ranking products by traffic

1 min

Two cut-offs appear in the sources. Both are kept here. The sources do not say which applies to which store size.

  1. Look at the product analytics (the numbers showing how each product performs) and rank all products by traffic.
  2. Identify the top 100 products that actually get visitors.
  3. Focus optimization on those product pages, rather than trying to perfect every page.
  • Focus optimization effort on the top 20% of products that receive traffic. These are likely to be the first products that make money. Other products can wait until traffic data shows they matter.

80/20 across pages and channels

1 min
  • Use the 80-20 principle (the idea that about 20 percent of things bring about 80 percent of results). Apply it to SEO (search engine optimization, making pages easier for search engines to find). Start with the top 20% of pages by traffic. Optimize these first to boost conversions, then slowly expand to other pages. Do not try to optimize the entire catalog on day one. Older (2022).
  • Use an 80/20 approach when deciding where to focus. For an established store of about three to four years, paid search (ads shown in search results) and organic search (free search results) are almost always the largest traffic sources. So conversion work has the most impact there. Compare sources in Google Analytics to see which converts and which does not. Older (2021-22).
  • Focus on the top 20% of products by traffic and conversion first. (Derived from the two rules above.)

Representative analysis

1 min
  • Do not change website elements at random based on personal opinion. That wastes effort. Changes should follow evidence of what customers need to see before they buy. Judge that with empathy, and compare with competitor pages. Older (2021-22).
  • When a competitor comparison reveals a problem on one product, the same problem usually exists on other products in the store. Analyse a few representative products in the comparison matrix (a table comparing your pages with rivals) instead of checking every product separately. Older (2021-22).

Traffic versus conversion

1 min
  • If traffic is high but conversions are low, improve the product page and retarget visitors. Retargeting means showing ads again to people who already visited. If traffic is low, first focus on getting relevant traffic.
  • Conversion levers are things that push visitors to buy. They include social proof (signs that others trust the product, such as reviews) and compelling offers (strong deals). Retargeting drove most of the owner's sales.
  • When traffic already arrives but sales lag, improve the conversion rate before buying more traffic. A store's conversion rate is never finished. Keep improving it. Older (2019).
  • On-site conversion changes (changes made on the store itself) produce small, incremental gains, meaning small steps forward. They do little if the traffic is poorly matched to the offer, or if the offer itself is weak. Fix the audience match and the offer first, before you spend time on these details. Older (2021-22).

CRO action checklist (full sequence)

2 min

CRO means conversion rate optimization: changing the store so more visitors buy.

Source B. Google Optimize and Neat are named as in the source; check that each tool is still available before use.

  1. Set up a CRO tracking system to log every conversion test. Options include a Trello board, a spreadsheet, a Google Sheet, Asana or another project management tool. Trello and Asana are online task boards.
  2. Connect a CRO testing tool to the e-commerce site (Google Optimize or the Neat A/B testing tool on Shopify), and test with one or both. An A/B testing tool shows two versions and measures which sells more.
  3. Fill the first column of the tracking system with a list of future CRO tests. Base the list on what you think needs testing after reviewing the material.
  4. Review your competition analysis, covering competitors' offers and USPs. A USP (unique selling point) is the reason a buyer should pick you.
  5. Compare your current offer with competitors. Judge where you stand in your market.
  6. Improve the offer toward a strong C.U.R.V. offer where you are behind, and record it in the curve offer builder. C.U.R.V. is the name the source gives its strong-offer model.
  7. Show the curve offer on the home page, product page and About Us page at minimum.
  8. Review product descriptions against the marketing message parts.
  9. Write a marketing message for one or more current bestsellers, or for high-traffic products with low conversion rates. Share the mechanism (the reason the product works) with any external copywriter.
  10. Implement the review multiplier call system (a way to get more reviews through calls). Share the script with customer service, and schedule post-purchase calls (calls after the sale).
  11. Add video review requests to every review request. Image reviews are the second option.
  12. Set up the off-site customer survey (a survey outside the store, sent by email). Send emails inviting visitors, opt-ins (people who gave their email), pre-purchase prospects (people who have not bought yet) and existing customers to take it. Choose questions from the template list.
  13. Choose and implement one on-site survey mechanism, such as an online chat widget or Hotjar. Hotjar is a tool that shows what visitors do on the site and can ask them questions.
  14. Identify other on-site CRO tests, plan them and start testing.
  15. Review the list of smaller on-site factors and add the promising ones to the test plan.
  16. Keep the surveys and testing running continuously.

On-site factors procedure

1 min

Older (2021-22).

  1. Check whether your store already does each factor.
  2. For each factor you do not have yet, or are unhappy with, add it to a conversion test backlog (a to-do list of tests). A Kanban-style board works. Kanban is a board with columns that move tasks forward, such as Trello.
  3. Decide when and how each test will run, then implement and measure it. Not every factor has to be implemented.

Checklist of easy wins

1 min
  • If visitors come to the store but do not buy, work through a list of 18 easy-win conversion tips before making deeper changes. Easy wins are quick fixes. Older (2018).

Test backlog and test design

1 min
  • Keep at least one conversion test running at all times. Treat testing as a permanent item on the to-do list. Even small tests add up to long-term change. Always collect new test ideas. Older (2021-22).
  • Conversion rate optimization is an incremental process, built from many small, repeatable improvements. Waiting for one rare change that doubles conversion rate is not a plan for growth. Older (2021-22).
  • Make many small changes to the store, such as offer placement and wording. Test them one after another rather than waiting for one large redesign. Each small change can noticeably move results, so check the effect of every change.
  • Store design tests make sense only after the store already makes sales. Before that, use design changes only when the offer and the traffic are both confirmed to be working.
  • Rewrite the marketing message first for products that already sell well. Then prioritize products with high traffic but low conversion rates, since they offer the biggest conversion upside. Older (2021-22).
  • Add the smaller on-site factors that may improve conversion to the test plan. Test them after the larger tests are planned. Use the tracking system for this list too. Older (2021-22).
  • Many popular conversion tips and hacks change very little on their own. The larger gains usually come from fundamental changes to the store, offer and purchase path (the steps from product to paid order). Prioritize fundamental changes over minor tweaks. Older (2021-22).
  • Conversion improvements fall into two kinds. Quick actions can be taken immediately. Ongoing processes take weeks or months to build and pay off. Plan both. Treat the ongoing ones as continuous work inside the business. Older (2021-22).
  • Conversion optimization is a foundational system, meaning the base everything else sits on. These 11 tasks form that system. It needs continuous revisiting and refinement beyond the initial setup.

Quick wins versus tests

1 min
  • Split the roadmap into two lists:
    • Quick wins: fixes to do now, such as making a guarantee, free shipping or free gifts visible.
    • Testing ideas: hero positioning (the big top section of the homepage), risk reversal (taking the risk off the buyer) and comparison modules (blocks that compare your product with others).
    • Tag each item with the metric it should move. A metric is a number you measure.
  • In an example 30-day roadmap, the first block (days 1 to 3) was labelled a quick win with high impact. The days 1 to 5 block included:
    • Rewriting the homepage hero to explain the premium tool, its technology and its benefits (less damage, less frizz, less styling time).
    • Adding a comparison section against standard hot tools near the homepage and product page.
    • Making the guarantee, free shipping and free gifts visible, which was rated low effort.
    • The metrics to improve were homepage conversion rate, product page engagement, add-to-cart and checkout start.

Weekly cycle

1 min
  • Repeat the full optimization process every week, for example every Monday, instead of doing it once. A store either grows or slips backwards.
  • Conversion optimization is an ongoing process. After the initial setup, keep checking how ads and pages perform over time. Review each optimization area again. Older (2020).
  • Monitor ad spend every two weeks (self-auditing, checking your own spending). Identify ads and products not generating profitable sales. Verify ROAS is stable or improving. Older (2020).

Reading trends

1 min
  • A small, short-term dip in traffic or sales is not always a problem. Check whether it continues into the next weeks before concluding something is broken. Older (2020).
  • Slow sales periods are a good time to work on conversion rate optimization, adding products and suppliers, improving content and expanding sales channels. The aim is to be ready when orders return at a higher rate. Older (2021).

Measuring compounded gains

1 min
  • Improving each funnel step by 10% compounds. Compounding means each gain multiplies the last one. 1.1 x 1.1 x 1.1 = 1.331, so three separate 10% gains give about 33% total, not 30%.
  • Funnel steps to optimize include:
    • The visit to next-page rate (how many visitors go on to another page).
    • The add-to-cart rate.
    • The checkout click rate.
    • Abandoned-cart recovery (winning back customers who left with items in their cart).
    • Pre-purchase emails (emails sent before a sale).

Dashboard organization

1 min
  • Organize the dashboard (your screen of key numbers) with decision metrics on the left, top-of-funnel metrics in the middle, and bottom-of-funnel metrics on the right. Top-of-funnel means early steps. Bottom-of-funnel means late steps near the sale.
  • This layout lets you see everything that drives daily decisions without scrolling.
  • Then use the funnel metrics to find where drop-offs occur in the customer journey. These are reach (how many people saw your ads), impressions (how many times ads were shown), link clicks, add to carts, checkouts and purchases.
  • Everything you need to make decisions should fit on one screen.

CRO agency success and pay

1 min
  • CRO agency success is harder to measure than paid media (ads you pay for), because many factors influence conversion improvement. Structure pay as a base plus incentive, tied to incremental revenue (extra sales) or conversion rate improvement.
  • Beware revenue share percentages that favor the agency. A revenue share means the agency takes a cut of sales.

First 24 hours (ad level)

1 min
  • Within the first 24 hours, evaluate individual ads, not the ad set level. An ad set is a group of ads that share one budget.
  • Turn off any single ad that has spent $10 or more, has a cost per unique link click (CPC, the cost of each click) of $3.50 or higher, and has generated no add-to-cart events or purchases.
  • The reason: such a CPC is not sustainable long-term.

Product break-even (product level)

1 min
  • A product with no sales in its first two days can still become profitable. Profitable means earning more than it costs. This can happen after the landing page or offer is fixed and the ads have collected more data.
  • Cut a product at the pre-set break-even limit, meaning the sales level where it just covers its costs. Do not cut it on the first disappointing day. Change one weak funnel stage at a time.
  • Change the ad budget at product level: monitor, cut, redirect and verify. See the reallocation procedure below.

Reallocating budget from losers

1 min

monitoring every two weeks.

  1. Monitor ad spend every two weeks (self-auditing).
  2. Identify ads and products not generating profitable sales.
  3. Cut spend from underperforming products entirely.
  4. Redirect that budget to products that are converting or have historically converted.
  5. Verify return on ad spend (ROAS, money earned per dollar spent on ads) is stable or improving.

Recovering a store that almost breaks even

1 min

Older (2022). To improve a store that is almost breaking even after several months of ads, meaning it earns about as much as it spends:

  1. Review all ad and analytics data to see which ads and products lead to the best results.
  2. Identify losing products because of low margin (little profit per sale), supplier issues or high ad cost.
  3. Stop advertising the losers, renegotiate with the supplier, or focus only on organic traffic (visitors who come without paid ads) for them.
  4. Reinvest the cut budget into the top 20 percent of products. Optimize those product pages for higher conversion.

Launch budgets

1 min
  • Pouring large budgets into untested campaigns is a mistake. Start with small budgets to see if a campaign is profitable before scaling.
  • If results are poor, delete and restart the campaign rather than modifying it extensively. Older (2020).

Scaling and changing strategy

1 min

Scaling means growing what already makes money.

Scaling checks

1 min
  • Cost structure example: a £15 cost on a £45 sale. That is 33% of revenue (money coming in from sales) spent on ads.
  • Use this ratio to decide whether to double the budget (spend twice as much on ads) during 4-hour scaling checks.

Raising bids

1 min
  • A bid is the most you pay for one ad click. To pay more per click and still keep your target profit per sale, you must first raise the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Confirm that the new bid is still profitable at your current conversion rate before you increase it. Older (2021-22).

Google Shopping ceiling

1 min
  • Google Shopping is Google's product ad listings. Once you reach the profit limit there, raising the conversion rate is the only lever left in that channel. A lever is something you can change to get different results.
  • That lever also has a ceiling, a top point it cannot pass.
  • After that, keep the account profitable in maintenance mode (running it without growing it), and focus on other channels. Older (2021-22).

Low conversion on paid traffic

1 min

Traffic means visitors. Paid traffic means visitors you pay for through ads. Older (2021-22).

  1. Check that the traffic targets the right audience (the right type of people).
  2. If the ads look good but conversions (sales from visitors) are low, reduce ad spend.
  3. Focus effort on the website and raise the conversion rate.
  4. Increase ad spend again only once the website can support the cost of the traffic.

Scaling a product

1 min
  • Build the page structure (the layout of the page) on one product page first. Duplicate it only after its add-to-cart ratio (the share of visitors who click "add to cart") meets the platform minimum (the lowest number your shop software asks for).
  • Improve product page optimization (making the page work better) for products that already convert (already make sales) before you scale their traffic.

Where sources disagree

2 min

Different experts give different advice. Here is how to choose.

  1. Design changes before or after sales. Store design means how the shop looks and works.

    • Source C says store design tests (trying two designs to see which sells more) make sense only after the store makes sales. Before that, run them only when the offer (what you sell and at what deal) and traffic are confirmed to work.
    • Source A says to start conversion work (changes that get visitors to buy) at the first sale, or before any sale at about 40 unique visitors per day (different people visiting, each counted once per day).
    • Source C also says not to leave the store unchanged from day one.
    • Apply the design rule to design tests.
    • Use the pre-sale rule (the rule about starting before any sale) for general conversion work, once traffic reaches about 40 unique visitors per day.

  2. Order of first store tests.

    • One Source C sequence starts with the landing page (the first page visitors see) or theme (the store's visual template). Then comes prices, lead magnets (free offers that collect email addresses), discounts, copy (the words on the page), product description, and trust signals (proof that the shop is real and safe to buy from). Last comes ad breakdown (checking each ad's results separately).
    • Another starts with themes, bundles (selling several items together), prices, urgency (reasons to buy now, such as a timer), and copy. Then comes scaling.
    • The first sequence has a stated trigger, a clear starting signal: a winning product gets traffic but no sales.
    • The second has no stated trigger.
    • Use the first sequence when that trigger applies.

  3. Many small changes versus fundamental changes.

    • Source B says small tips change little. Fundamental changes to the store, offer, and purchase path (the steps a buyer takes to pay) come first.
    • Source C and Source B both recommend many small tested changes (changes you measure with real numbers) as an ongoing process.
    • Use fundamental changes when the offer or purchase path is weak.
    • Once those are planned, run small changes continuously.

  4. Cut-offs for product focus. A cut-off is a limit that decides which products to focus on.

    • The top 100 trafficked products (the 100 products with the most visitors) is one cut-off.
    • The top 20% of trafficked products is another.
    • Both are kept above.
    • Use the top 100 as the working list, and the 20% rule to rank within it, if the sources' stated numbers are applied to the same store.
    • (Derived; the sources do not say which applies to which store size.)

  5. Ad cut level. A cut means stopping or lowering spending.

    • Ad-level cuts happen within the first 24 hours at $10 spend and $3.50 CPC (cost per click, what you pay each time someone clicks your ad).
    • Product-level cuts happen at the pre-set break-even limit (the point where you neither make nor lose money), not on the first disappointing day.
    • These apply at different levels and do not conflict.

Further details

1 min
  • Product recommendation and discovery tests (for example, "you may also like" boxes) need strong product categorization (sorting products into clear groups) and merchandising (how products are arranged and shown) to get their full lift (extra sales).
  • Before you run tests on product recommendations, out-of-stock alternatives (products shown when an item sells out), or quick navigation links (shortcut links), audit and optimize your product catalog structure (the list and layout of all your products) and categories.

More details
