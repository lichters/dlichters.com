Playbook › 6. Conversion optimization

Customer research, feedback and behavior analytics

Section 6.4 · 4 of 22 in this chapter · 24 min read

In short

  • Learn why people buy before you build the store. Their feelings decide what your page must show.
  • Ask customers and visitors directly. Trust patterns across many answers, not one opinion.
  • Check the numbers before you change anything. Start with bounce rate, the share of visitors who leave after one page.
  • Keep surveys short, with three to five questions. Wait for 50 answers per question before you trust a pattern.
  • Start with one or two feedback methods, not all six.
More key points
  • A CRO audit finds why visitors do not buy, so you can fix it.
  • Use one AI project with two sources: the audit rules and your store facts.
  • Run each of the three prompts in its own chat.
  • Start with text only. Add screenshots only if the AI's answers are wrong.
  • Copy your theme before big changes, so you can go back if something breaks.

Why people buy and why they leave

2 min
  • Study why customers buy before you design the store and the offer.
  • Their psychology (what they feel and need) decides what your page must show.
  • Market research (finding out what buyers want) and emotional framework analysis (listing the feelings that drive buying) improve:
    • pre-landing pages (the pages a visitor sees before the main sales page)
    • bottom-of-funnel ads (ads aimed at people close to buying; the funnel is the path from first visit to purchase)
    • The website
    • overall funnel performance
  • Scaling (spending more on ads to grow sales) works best when every message speaks to the real psychological needs of your target customer.
  • Customer knowledge improves conversion (the share of visitors who buy).
  • The offer and messaging then match the points customers need to see before buying.
  • It also shows which traffic channels (places visitors come from) to use. Focus on the places where your ideal customers are most often online. **
  • The first customers reveal the real buyer profile (a description of who actually buys).
  • A niche (a narrow market group) with a specific buyer group can justify building a page for that group.
  • Designing the store around the first customer type can open a repeat business channel (a way to sell again to the same people).
  • Monitoring visitors online works like watching customers in a real shop.
  • Track how many enter, how long they stay, what they examine, and what they buy or avoid.
  • Then improve based on patterns you can see.
  • Improvements based on what one visitor tells you benefit all future visitors.
  • So conversion gains compound (build on each other) over time.
  • Objections (reasons not to buy) come from visitors who looked but did not buy.
  • Find them in complaints, comments and feedback. They show the real hesitations that stop purchases.
  • Use them to guide page copy (the written words on the page), FAQs (frequently asked questions) and ad messaging.

Example of a main customer desires list for a financial product (example: social security). A desires list shows what buyers want, how strongly (urgency, from 1 to 10), and why.

Not to be the fool who left money on the table 9/10 Loss-aversion-driven (people feel losses more strongly than gains); stronger than the gain-framed version (a version that focuses on what you win)
Certainty that the irreversible decision is right 10/10 Peaks sharply (spikes hardest) in the 90 days before filing (submitting the claim)

Diagnosing from the traffic side and from visitor signals

1 min
  • When it is hard to see the cause of low conversion from the inside, ask visitors and customers directly.
  • Owners often cannot judge their own site objectively. They are too attached to it.
  • Customer answers replace your guesses. **
  • Collect feedback to see recurring patterns across many answers.
  • Do not react to a single opinion.
  • One response never justifies a site change.
  • A problem named by a meaningful share of respondents deserves a plan. **
  • Asking customers where they found the site, or why they contacted support, is a useful signal.
  • But few stores do it, so it is too thin to diagnose a low conversion rate on its own.
  • Do not wait for that feedback. Start the diagnosis on the traffic side and work through the funnel. Older advice (2021-2022).
  • Both sources apply together. Ask customers when you have enough volume to hear them.
  • Start on the traffic side when feedback is thin.
  • In Google Analytics (Google's free visitor statistics tool), go to Behavior and open the page as a landing page (the first page a visitor sees).
  • Check the bounce rate (visitors who leave after one page), time on site, time on page, the number of visitors who continue to other pages, and the number who exit from that page. **
  • Average session duration (how long one visit lasts) above 15 seconds indicates customer interest in the product.
  • A session under 15 seconds means the customer left early because of weak images, offer or messaging.
  • A high bounce rate on a Google Shopping product page can be normal. (Google Shopping shows product listings in Google search.)
  • These visitors already know what they want.
  • If the page answers every buying question, check session recordings (records of what each visitor did on the page) for frustrations. Do not assume the page is at fault.

Self-testing the store

1 min
  • Take a week away from the store.
  • Then open it as a real customer and try to buy a product, for example pants.
  • Note what stops you at each step.
  • Note what demotivates you: slow loading, unclear prices, missing shipping information, or too many pop-ups (small windows that cover the page).
  • Ask a good friend who will be honest to buy a product while thinking out loud.
  • Watch where they hesitate.
  • Do not rely on family members for honest feedback.
  • Make a purchase yourself on your own store.
  • If you would not buy, note why and change it.
  • If you could not buy, note why and fix it.
  • If the store looks perfect and you still see no problems, get an outside view.
  • Ask a friend to go through the buying process while thinking out loud.
  • Then you hear their hesitations.

Choosing a feedback method

3 min
# Method Channel
1 Post-purchase survey (a survey sent after buying) Email, sent a set time after purchase (separate from the review request)
2 Customer service survey Phone or chat support, asked before the call ends
3 Exit-intent survey (shown when a visitor is about to leave) Pop-up when a visitor is about to leave
4 On-site survey (a survey shown on the page) Single question sliding onto the page, or an on-site survey tool
5 Online chat survey Automated chat that asks questions or links to a survey
6 Browse abandonment survey (for people who looked but did not buy) Email triggered by a browse abandonment event (a visitor who viewed products and left)
  • The method you choose depends on order volume (how many orders you get) and traffic (how many visitors you get).
  • At around 100 customers per day, asking existing customers about the site is practical.
  • Without customers, the business must reach visitors who have not bought yet. **
  • Introduce feedback collection gradually.
  • Start with one or two methods. Add more over time.
  • Running all six at once is unnecessary.
  • Sites with different order volumes and traffic levels will find different methods work best. **
  • Post-purchase survey answers come only from people who already bought.
  • So they cannot show why non-buyers left.
  • Their suggestions for improvement still help make future visitors more likely to buy. **
  • Hotjar is a behavior tracking tool (it records what visitors do).
  • It also offers on-site surveys that appear on the page.
  • It is the recommended tool for on-site surveys. **
  • Google Forms is free for off-site surveys (surveys sent outside the page, for example by email).
  • Paid alternatives are Typeform, RightMessage and SurveyMonkey.
  • Typeform can be embedded directly on a Shopify page (Shopify is a store platform), so the visitor stays on your domain (your web address). **
  • Keep surveys and testing running as an ongoing process.
  • Stopping once the first round is done wastes insight into customer friction (anything that slows or blocks buying).
  • So conversion work should be a constant improvement process. **

Setting up the off-site customer survey (older advice, 2021-2022):

  1. Create emails that invite visitors, opt-ins (people who gave you their email), pre-purchase prospects (people who have not bought yet) and existing customers to complete the survey about their experience on your site.
  2. Choose questions from the survey template list.
  3. Build the first survey with these questions.
  4. Use the survey in the emails.

Implementation steps for customer feedback (older advice, 2021-2022):

  1. Set up one off-site survey (for example Google Forms or Typeform).
  2. Add the survey email and a reminder into the post-purchase thank-you email sequence (the series of emails sent after buying).
  3. Choose one on-site survey method and implement it. Options: Hotjar survey, exit-intent pop-up, on-site pop-up, or online chat survey.
  4. Test variants (different versions) later to improve the response rate (the share of people who answer).
  5. Task the customer service team to ask key questions during calls, especially pre-purchase calls.
  6. Set up a Q&A tracking tool (a tool that records questions and answers) and give the right people access.
  7. If browse abandonment tracking is used, set up a browse abandonment feedback email (an email to someone who looked at products but left without buying).

Survey design rules

2 min
  • Off-site surveys should be short: three to five questions, never more than five.
  • Three is a good number.
  • State clearly that the survey takes only a couple of minutes. **
  • Collect at least 50 responses per question before drawing conclusions or hypotheses (guesses you test).
  • Aim for 100 responses per question for conversion-related decisions. **
  • Make each survey question specific enough that every possible answer leads to a clear conclusion.
  • Use yes/no or 1-to-10 rating questions where they are enough to decide whether a problem exists.
  • Use open-ended questions (questions where people write their own answer) where nuance is needed to find a solution. **
  • Recommended pattern: if 10 out of 50 respondents name the same problem, treat it as a pattern worth acting on.
  • Plan an improvement for that area. **
  • On-site surveys should ask one single question at a time.
  • The question appears as a slide-in (a box that slides onto the page).
  • Rotate the question over time. Keep one question for a few weeks, then switch to another after it has run for a while. **
  • Exit-intent or timed pop-ups for feedback can be shown when a visitor leaves, or after about 30 seconds to a minute on the site.
  • Ask for a short survey of about two minutes, and explain its purpose.
  • Expect only a minority (a small share) of visitors to respond.
  • The survey must run long enough to build useful data. **
  • Asking visitors why they did not buy with an exit-intent pop-up: only about 5% of visitors usually answer.
  • But the answers can reveal issues such as unclear shipping regions or missing information.
  • Fix these in the FAQ or on the page.
  • For an automated chat survey, the chat can trigger (start) on specific pages or after a set time.
  • Then it runs a series of questions in the chat. Each answer triggers the next question.
  • The answers appear in the chat software.
  • The alternative is a chat message that links to an external survey. That sends the visitor away from the site. **
  • Survey length depends on where the survey appears.
    • Off-site surveys: three to five questions (older advice, see above).
    • Single questions fit moments of hesitation, such as the cart (the basket of items) or checkout (the payment steps), and the last abandoned checkout email (sent to someone who started to pay and left).
    • Sources differ on the count. So match the count to the channel: one question at the point of hesitation, three to five in a full off-site survey.

Template for a cart or checkout feedback poll (one short question, asked at the moment of hesitation):

Place a feedback poll on the cart or checkout pages that asks: What's stopping you from completing this purchase? Keep the question short so visitors answer it at the moment they hesitate.

Post-purchase and order confirmation surveys

2 min
  • Add a short survey to the Shopify order confirmation page (the thank-you page shown after buying).
  • Use an app, or a free Google Form.
  • Send the responses to Google Sheets (a free online spreadsheet).
  • Customers are most willing to answer right after buying.

Post-purchase survey for the order confirmation page (answer fields can be multiple choice or short text):

1) Why did you buy from us?
2) What was the most important factor for you when choosing a [niche product]?
3) Are you looking to buy any other [niche products]? If yes, which ones?
4) Which websites, podcasts and YouTube channels do you follow?
5) How did you hear about us?
Replace the bracketed parts with your niche. Use the answers to plan monthly offers, find new products to add, and find new advertising channels.
  • Use a small prize draw (a random winner) on the survey, for example one monthly cash winner.
  • This gives buyers a reason to answer.
  • State on the survey that answers are anonymous (your name is not attached to them).
  • On the first screen of a post-purchase survey, state the reward clearly.
  • For example, a monthly cash prize of $1,000 for one respondent per month.
  • Add a note that all answers are anonymous.
  • A chance to win costs the buyer nothing.
  • So the prize is a reason to answer, not a payment per answer.
  • Survey invitation formula: reward + time needed + number of questions + anonymity note.
  • It works because it lowers the perceived effort (how hard answering feels) and the risk of answering.
  • That raises the completion rate (the share who finish) for post-purchase surveys.
    • Example 1: Tell us why you bought and get 10% off your next order. Three quick questions, under 60 seconds, and all answers stay anonymous.
    • Example 2: Two short questions about your results, about 45 seconds, and one buyer each month gets a $50 gift card.
  • Post-purchase survey rewards in the Source D example differ from the older Source A case.
  • In the older case, a 30 to 50 USD gift card was offered for about 30 minutes of time in a pre-launch interview (an interview before the store opens).
  • Choose the reward by the effort asked.
  • Short surveys fit a small prize or discount.
  • Long interviews need a clear payment.

Abandoned checkout and exit follow-up

1 min
  • Add the survey question to the last abandoned checkout email.
  • Send it after the other emails have addressed price, offer and trust.
  • Ask what stopped the customer from buying.
  • Send a simple plain text email, not an image-heavy one.
  • Split test (send two versions to different groups and compare) the survey in the last email against the first email.
  • Compare the replies.
  • Surveys are less useful at the start of an abandoned checkout flow.
  • Customers stop for different reasons, and each email must address them all.
  • Once the flow already answers trust, price and offer objections, a survey in the last email shows what remains unsolved.
  • Call customers who abandoned checkout to ask why they did not complete the order.
  • Record the answers as qualitative data (descriptive answers in words, not numbers) for improving the store.
  • Do not call on Sundays, since many customers do not welcome calls then. **

Session recordings, heatmaps and device checks

2 min

  • Start the Lucky Orange free trial (a tool that records visits and shows heatmaps) only after active traffic is coming to the store, not during development.

  • You can cancel within the first six days to avoid being charged.

  • Use Lucky Orange to record individual sessions and heatmaps (color maps showing where people click).

  • Recordings show where visitors click, hesitate and exit.

  • Analyze drop-off points (the spots where people leave) after 150 to 200 sessions.

  • Then decide whether to improve images, offer, trust factors (proof you can be trusted) or product fit (whether the product suits the buyer).

  • Recordings show whether people add to cart (put a product in the basket), click the bundle (a set of products sold together), or leave at the product page.

  • This helps diagnose a funnel problem (the place where buyers get lost on the way to purchase).

  • Lucky Orange helps find clicks on non-clickable elements (things that look clickable but are not) or actions you do not want.

  • Use those recordings to make optimizations (improvements) to product pages.

  • Heatmap review, page by page:

    1. Open Lucky Orange and review heatmaps page by page to identify where visitors click most (deep orange areas).
    2. Verify whether areas with high click activity are actually interactive or meant to be.
    3. For non-clickable areas receiving heavy clicks, redesign that section to be clickable or clarify its purpose.
    4. Check your Add-to-Cart button (the button that puts an item in the basket) specifically. It should display as deep orange (most-clicked area).
    5. If the Add-to-Cart button is not deep orange, enlarge it, change its color, or increase its visual prominence (make it easier to see).

  • Use session recordings to find device-specific bugs (errors that happen on one device only).

  • Example: a feature works on desktop but fails on one particular Android phone.

  • Check the recordings for that device. Then fix the layout or function.

  • Test it on the same device again.

  • Online visitor monitoring works the same way as observing customers in a physical store.

  • You track entries, time spent, what they examine, and what they buy or avoid.

Live chat as a signal

1 min
  • Questions in live chat (a chat box where visitors ask in real time) are a signal of conversion problems.
  • When customers ask for variants (versions, such as different sizes or colors) that are not available, the variant structure may be confusing.
  • Splitting variants into separate landing pages can clarify what is on offer.

Competitor and customer research

2 min

Competitor product-page analysis, step by step (competitors are rival stores):

  1. Find your previously best-selling product pages in your store.
  2. Search each product name on Google.
  3. Open the product pages of the top competitors that appear in the results.
  4. Check whether they changed anything compared with last year. Identify the changes that help them overcome obstacles you also face.
  5. Repeat this regularly. Competitors keep adapting, so the process becomes an ongoing back-and-forth.
  • Find your most visited product pages in your store analytics (your store's visitor statistics).
  • These are the products receiving the most clicks from ads.
  • Search each product on Google. Review the results as a shopper would.
  • Compare competitors on price, product photos, product descriptions and shipping speed.
  • Ask why a shopper would choose the competitor over your store.
  • Then adjust your product page so it beats the competitor on those points.

Analyze competitors at product-page level, step by step:

  1. Pick competitor stores in your niche.
  2. Open their product pages side by side with your own (next to each other).
  3. Note what competitors do well and where they are weak.
  4. Turn each competitor weakness into a strength on your own product pages.
  • Before comparing a product across two stores, make sure both listings are the same variant.
  • For example, a 12-light model against a 12-light model.
  • Load both product pages with the same SKU (a unique stock code for each product) or name.
  • Then the comparison of price, images and policies is fair.
  • Competitor research findings should directly inform conversion rate optimization (CRO, the work of raising the share of visitors who buy).
  • They should not stay as a standalone document.
  • Review the research before making any conversion-related change, so changes respond to the market. **
  • Customer research case: a skincare startup (a new small company) selling its own sustainable cosmetics (eco-friendly beauty products).
  • It used user feedback tools to form hypotheses before redesigning its website.
  • It spent about 3 months building the new design, then about 3 more months tweaking it (making small adjustments).
  • Before launch, it asked its top 10 customers for feedback. It offered a 30 to 50 USD gift card for about 30 minutes of their time.
  • After the redesign, the store reached a 5% conversion rate for new visitors and 8% for returning customers.

AI-driven CRO audits: principles and scope

2 min
  • Run an AI-assisted audit (a check done with help from AI) of the full customer journey (every step from first visit to purchase).
  • Do this before hiring an agency (an outside company that does the work for you) or buying complex CRO testing tools.
  • This fits brands doing roughly $500K to $2 million a year that have not yet completed the basic best-practice review of every page, from homepage to checkout.
  • The target store profile for this audit: a brand doing roughly $500K to $2 million in annual revenue (sales per year).
  • That brand has not yet done the foundational best-practice review (a basic check against proven good practice) of its full customer journey.
  • ChatGPT Projects (a folder feature in ChatGPT) can hold the audit instructions and input metrics as sources.
  • Each prompt is run in its own chat. Claude can be used the same way.
  • Projects are a container that keeps all three audit prompts and their outputs together.
  • The audit should cover five customer journey pages:
    • homepage
    • product page
    • collection page (a page listing several products in one category)
    • cart page
    • checkout
  • The criteria (what it checks) are: clarity, trust, friction (anything that slows buying), offer, product page conversion, homepage orientation (how clearly the homepage guides people), mobile experience, objection handling (answering reasons not to buy), and conversion prioritization (ranking fixes by impact).
  • Audit instructions should demand specific, not generic, recommendations.
  • Prioritize customer clarity and trust over cleverness.
  • State that the output must explain the commercial reason (the money reason) for each recommendation.
  • The output must be usable in call notes, Slack recaps (summaries in the Slack chat app) or a CRO checklist.
  • Include that the AI should assume most traffic is mobile (on phones), and flag small-screen friction.
  • When metrics, screenshots or copy are provided, the audit assistant should first ask only for the most important missing information.
  • Then it proceeds with the audit using the data given.
  • Input metrics (numbers you give the audit) to supply:
    • conversion rate
    • average order value (AOV, the average amount per order)
    • traffic by channel
    • returning customer rate
    • add-to-cart rate
    • checkout completion rate
    • Meta ROAS (return on ad spend on Meta, which covers Facebook and Instagram ads)
    • MER (marketing efficiency ratio, how much sales your total marketing brings in)
  • The example brand's checkout completion rate of 54 percent was flagged as a key leak (a big loss point).
  • The audit uses input metrics to decide which opportunities come first.
  • Fictional or approximated metrics (made-up or rough numbers) can test the audit flow.
  • For real results, the input metrics should match the actual store as closely as possible, including the URL (web address) and real copy (the real written words on the site).
  • Feed screenshots of the homepage or product page into the audit whenever AI output hallucinates (makes things up) or is inaccurate.
  • This lets it give deeper, more precise feedback.
  • Run the audit prompts one section at a time. Rewrite these separately rather than all at once:
    • homepage hero (the large top section of the homepage)
    • above-the-fold messaging (the words visible before scrolling)
    • product page headline
    • benefit bullets
    • FAQ and objection handling
    • offer framing
    • shipping and returns clarity
    • trust badge placement (where small trust logos go)

AI-driven CRO audits: setup procedure and prompts

2 min

A CRO audit (conversion rate optimization: finding why visitors do not buy, then fixing it) can be run with an AI tool. You use three separate prompts inside one project.

Steps:

  1. Create an AI project. Name it 'CRO website audit'.
  2. Add the first source: the audit instructions.
    • This is a "system-style brief": standing rules that tell the AI its role.
    • It holds the audit criteria, meaning what the AI checks, and the rules it must follow.
  3. Add the second source: an input-metrics document.
    • This is a file with your store facts and numbers.
  4. Fill the input-metrics document with these items:
    • The homepage headline, the big title at the top of your home page.
    • The subheadline, the smaller line under the headline.
    • The primary CTA (call to action: the main button that asks for a sale).
    • The key homepage messaging themes, the main points your home page repeats.
    • Promotional banner examples, the sale or offer strips at the top of the site.
    • Social proof: signs other people trust you, such as reviews and testimonials.
    • Collections, which are your product groups or categories.
    • The hero product, your main featured product, and how it is positioned (how you present it to buyers).
    • The average price range.
    • The metrics: conversion rate (share of visitors who buy).
    • AOV (average order value: average money spent per order).
    • Traffic by channel, meaning where visitors come from, such as search, social or ads.
    • The returning customer rate (share of buyers who buy again).
    • The add-to-cart rate (share of visitors who add an item to their cart).
    • The checkout completion rate (share of people who start checkout and finish it).
    • Meta ROAS (return on ad spend on Facebook and Instagram ads: sales per ad dollar).
    • MER (marketing efficiency ratio: total sales divided by total ad spend).
    • The top customer objections, the reasons people hesitate or do not buy.
    • The main promotional offer and example bundles (products sold together as one deal).
    • The primary target customer, described as specifically as possible.
    • A secondary audience, and their psychographics (attitudes, values and habits).
  5. Open a separate chat for each prompt:
    • Prompt 1 is the customer journey diagnosis.
    • Prompt 2 rewrites weak sections. One section at a time is recommended.
    • Prompt 3 builds the 30-day roadmap (a step-by-step plan for the next month).
  6. If the AI output is inaccurate, send it screenshots of the homepage or product page. Then ask for a deeper look.
  7. Before major theme or website changes, duplicate the theme (make a copy of your store design). If something breaks, you can go back to the copy.
  8. If you have a team, turn the roadmap into delegated tasks (tasks handed to each person). Give each person one clear job.

Prompt 1: customer journey diagnosis

1 min

Open a new chat and paste this prompt. It asks the AI to act as a first-time visitor and find where people leave.

Take on the role of a seasoned senior CRO specialist for ecommerce and review this store as someone landing on it for the first time. Pinpoint the largest places where visitors drop off, covering clarity, credibility, the deal itself, the product page, the mobile version, points of friction, handling of hesitations, and what pushes someone to buy. Rank these problems by how much revenue they likely cost, and justify each ranking.

Audit assistant instructions: the auditor brief

2 min

This is the first source you add in step 2. It sets the rules the AI follows in every chat.

You are a compact, AI-driven assistant for ecommerce conversion rate optimization and customer journey auditing. Your job is to examine the path a buyer takes through an online store so it becomes easier to follow, more believable, less effortful, and more likely to convert. Review these areas:
1. Clarity: does a visitor immediately grasp what the brand sells and why that matters?
2. Trust: is there enough confidence from reviews, social proof, press coverage, the founder's story and similar cues?
3. Friction: where does the shopper have to work too hard?
4. Offer: is the deal attractive and simple to understand, including bundles, discounts and free shipping?
5. Product page conversion: does the product page clearly lay out what the item is, its advantages, its use cases and its objections?
6. Homepage journey: does the homepage orient visitors quickly, build credibility and explain the product category?
7. Mobile experience: assume most visits come from phones and point out anything on a small screen that could slow people down.
8. Objection handling: what must a customer know before purchasing, and does the site answer that plainly?
9. Conversion prioritization.
The five pages in scope are the homepage, product page, collection page, cart and checkout.

Guidelines:
- Be concrete, not generic. Do not hand over a list of stock suggestions.
- Put customer clarity and trust ahead of cleverness.
- Rank recommendations by impact, effort and confidence.
- Do not suggest A/B testing by default if the brand probably lacks the traffic to reach a meaningful result.
- If a change is obvious and low-risk, label it a 'fix' rather than a 'test'.
- Explain the commercial reason behind each recommendation.
- Keep advice workable for a small, lean ecommerce team.
- Aim for a higher conversion rate, higher average order value, stronger customer confidence and more completed purchases.
- Write in plain language that can go straight into call notes, a Slack recap or a CRO checklist.
- Rewritten copy should convert while staying true to the brand voice.
- If information is missing, say what you would need to sharpen the audit.
- When screenshots, website copy or metrics are provided, first ask only for the most critical missing item if one is needed. Otherwise, carry on with what was supplied.

Output layout: open with an executive summary. Then give the customer journey diagnosis with an impression of each core area, the biggest trust gaps, the friction points, copy and messaging improvements, and finally a prioritized action plan.

AI-driven CRO audits: example result

1 min
  • In one example, the store sold premium hair tools (high-end, expensive products) priced at $149 to $299.
  • Its biggest leak was price skepticism: doubt that the price is worth it.
  • First-time visitors did not see why the product was better than well-known branded tools or marketplace tools (cheap tools sold on big online shopping sites).
  • The highest-impact fixes were:
    • Make the value clear above the fold (the top part of the page you see before scrolling).
    • Add stronger science and proof for the core technology and damage reduction.
    • Add direct comparison content against branded premium tools, marketplace tools and standard hot tools (regular heat styling tools).
    • Improve objection handling on the product page.
    • Make the offer easier to understand.
    • Reduce checkout hesitation. This was flagged because checkout completion was only 54%.
