Playbook › 6. Conversion optimization

Matching traffic to awareness stage and page type

Section 6.5 · 5 of 22 in this chapter · 34 min read

In short

  • Match the page to the buyer's step. A person who does not yet know their problem needs a page that explains it first, not a buy button.
  • Only about 10 percent of the market is ready to buy at any time. A page built only for ready buyers reaches few people.
  • Visitors who do not know you need a longer bridge (the steps that lead them to the offer), with education before the offer.
  • Keep the ad and the landing page saying the same thing, so visitors do not leave at once.
  • Use several traffic sources. One source is not enough.
More key points
  • Do not send paid visitors to your homepage. Send them to a product page or collection page.
  • Visitors who do not know you yet need a page that warms them up before the product page.
  • Start with one standard landing page. Build more pages only as daily sales grow.
  • The less a visitor knows, the more the page must explain before it asks for the sale.

Awareness stages and the customer journey

1 min

The five-stage model and the pyramid

1 min
  • ** Buyers pass through five awareness stages before they buy. An awareness stage is how much a person already knows about their problem and the fix. The five are Unaware, Problem Aware, Solution Aware, Product Aware and Ninja Aware (also called Most Aware). Each stage is one mental step, from first being in the market to deciding on one specific product.
  • ** A four-stage version of the same model runs problem aware, solution aware, product aware and most aware, in that order. A product page converts visitors from an earlier stage only if it walks them through all the missing stages. A price or buy button means little to someone who has not yet accepted the problem or the solution.
  • ** The whole potential market fits into the awareness pyramid (a picture that shows the stages as layers). The Unaware group is the largest segment, at the base. The segments get smaller toward the top. Ninja Aware buyers are the fewest, at the top.
  • ** The customer awareness pyramid is also called the customer journey (the path a buyer takes to a purchase). Marketers use the two terms interchangeably, but they are not exactly the same. The sales funnel (a funnel picture) shows the same stages turned upside down. Its top of funnel is at the pyramid's base. Its bottom of funnel is at the tip.
  • ** The awareness pyramid runs from completely unaware prospects (people who might buy) at the bottom. It runs through problem aware and product aware, up to ninja aware buyers at the top, who are ready to purchase now. Traffic (visitors who come to your site) and landing pages (the page a visitor arrives on after a click) should be matched to the stage each prospect is in.

What each stage looks like

1 min
  • Unaware: ** Unaware people match the profile of a future buyer. They do not yet know they have a problem or desire that the product solves. Reaching them requires messaging that first makes them see the problem.
  • Problem Aware: ** People feel a pain and start looking for a solution. Pain is caused by problems. People either run away from pain or chase pleasure. So the pain is the entry point for messaging.
  • Solution Aware: ** People know a broad type of solution to their problem. They need to learn which option fits them, possibly among several solution types. This is the largest group in the market at any time. Most of them are not ready to buy, because they still need education (teaching) to feel safe buying.
  • Product Aware: ** People know several specific products that solve their problem. These include your product and competitor products (products sold by rivals). They have not yet chosen one. They are still comparing products before a final decision.
  • Ninja Aware: ** People have learned their options. The desire for a solution has been built. They know the exact product they need. They are ready to buy at this stage.

Why the stage decides the destination

2 min
  • ** Roughly 10 percent of the total market is ready to buy at any given time. That is only the Ninja Aware segment. The earlier awareness stages hold much larger shares of the market.
  • ** Product pages that only target Ninja Aware visitors reach a small pool of people. Visitors at earlier stages land on those pages, find nothing that speaks to their situation, and leave without buying.
  • ** Product Aware and Ninja Aware visitors already know the solution. For them the decision mostly comes down to price, the best offer and the best deal. Simple copy (the written sales words) with a clear deal and a bit of urgency works only for this group.
  • ** Serving people with good copy and the right product information at each awareness stage increases the likelihood of purchase. This raises the store's conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
  • ** Buyers choose the type of solution before they compare specific products. For example, a beginner who needs a surfboard first decides between renting and buying, then compares products. Product pages and copy should answer the solution-level question before the product-level comparison.
  • ** The landing page a visitor reaches must match the awareness stage that visitor is in at that moment. Sending problem aware or completely unaware traffic to a hard-sell product page (one that pushes hard to buy now) ignores the buyer's need for education. It usually yields few sales.
  • ** Under the 40-40-20 principle, the first 40 percent is targeting. So the audience you target decides which awareness stage must be served on the landing page.
  • ** Modern version of the rule: the first 40% depends on reaching the right audience in the right place, at the right time, with the right destination. The destination must match where the visitor sits in the customer journey. So cold social media traffic should not land on a high-ticket product page (an expensive product) with a direct buy request.
  • ** Cold, top-of-funnel (at the start of the journey) social media visitors are unaware of the solution. If they go straight to a product page selling a 3000 USD product, an extremely low conversion rate is expected. The traffic may match the market. But the destination does not match the buyer's awareness stage. The visitor needs to be moved toward readiness first.
  • ** A product page converts cold traffic only if it educates visitors. The education runs from problem awareness through solution awareness and product awareness to readiness to buy. When visitors who are still problem-aware land on a product page without that education, the mismatch is usually a bigger cause of low conversion than the page design.

Worked example: the awareness ladder for one product

1 min
  • ** Example for a home office worker with back pain from long sitting. The person is problem aware of the pain. Then they are solution aware that a standing-capable desk could help. Then they are product aware of the electric height-adjustable desk type. Ninja aware means they have chosen one exact model. Only then do they decide which retailer to buy it from.

Matching the headline and page to the stage

1 min
  • ** Match the ad headline (the big title of your ad) exactly to the primary promise on the destination landing page. If sending to a product page, use the primary promise from product copy. For blog or sales pages, match the page headline. Mismatches cause high bounce rates (visitors who leave at once) and wasted ad spend (money paid for ads). Users expect immediate visual confirmation of what they clicked.
  • Message match (the ad and the page say the same thing) matters. If the ad shows one thing and the link goes to a clearly different product or topic, visitors leave at once.
  • Keep the product page visually close to the ad. A GIF (a short looping animated picture) of the product in use in the ad is followed by the same GIF on the page. This one-to-one match reduces confusion for the visitor.
  • Match your headline to the prospect's awareness of the product. A product-aware prospect should see the product named first. A desire-aware prospect should see the desire first. A problem-aware prospect should see the problem stated specifically. Where a prospect stands on that scale decides how the copy begins.
  • Relevance example: a golf-targeted ad for sports leggings should lead to a page that talks about golf performance. For example, how the leggings support stance (how you stand) and swing. Not generic sportswear benefits. A visitor who sees their own activity named on the page feels understood and is more likely to buy. Build this kind of audience-specific variant (a version made for one audience) only after the first landing page test shows a winner.

Diagnosing a low-converting page

1 min

** Steps to fix a product page with a low conversion rate:

  1. Identify the awareness stage of the traffic arriving on that page. The traffic can be mixed. Problem aware visitors can be recognized by search traffic about their problem.
  2. Check whether the page moves visitors from their current stage to the next one.
  3. If it does not, segment your marketing (split it by group) so problem aware visitors first reach a more suitable page. Or add elements to the existing page that help visitors move through the stages.

Sequencing problem-aware and unaware visitors

1 min

** For a problem aware or completely unaware audience, the sequence is:

  1. Send the visitor to a content page first. It explains the problem and why it exists. It shows how to solve it and what solutions exist.
  2. Move them through email, retargeting (showing ads again to people who visited before) or another page that introduces your products as solutions.
  3. Finally, lead them to the product page.
  • ** Problem aware visitors typically arrive from Google searches about their problem. They also arrive from social media posts that promote a product solving that problem. So traffic from these sources should usually land on an educational content page, not a product page.

Traffic temperature and bridges

1 min

Hot, warm and cold traffic

2 min

Traffic temperature means how well a visitor already knows you.

  • Hot traffic: people who already know you, such as email subscribers, podcast listeners and blog readers. Speak to them personally. Share stories, opinions and details of your life, because they already trust you. Such personal messages can generate large revenue from hot traffic.
  • Warm traffic: people who reach you through someone else's relationship, such as affiliates (partners who sell for you and earn a cut) and joint venture partners (partners who share a deal with you). A personal endorsement (a recommendation) from a trusted source raises response greatly. A lift letter (a letter of recommendation sent with an offer) is one example. In classic direct mail (printed letters sent by post), list owners and sellers often split profits 50/50.
  • Cold traffic: people who do not know you, such as Facebook or pay-per-click (you pay each time someone clicks your ad) visitors. These prospects need the longest bridge. Most sellers fail here, because they speak in insider language (jargon only insiders understand). Plan a separate path for cold traffic instead of sending it straight to the offer.
  • Bridge length scales with coldness. A bridge is the page or steps that link the visitor to the offer. Hot traffic needs only a quick link or email. Warm traffic needs an endorsement from a trusted person. Cold traffic needs the longest bridge that educates the visitor before the offer. Choose the bridge from the traffic temperature.
  • Not all clicks are equal. The context a visitor came from decides how they respond to your page. A visitor who has just read a glowing recommendation converts far better than one who has just read a hostile warning about you. Control the frame your traffic arrives through wherever possible.
  • Visitors from cold traffic often need several steps before they buy. A path through trust-building landing pages can come before the purchase page. Plan the journey so each page answers one question the visitor has.
  • Traffic from different sources arrives at different awareness levels. So one landing page often cannot serve all of it. Plan up to three landing pages, one each for hot, warm and cold traffic. Send each traffic source to the page that matches its temperature. This setup is worth the extra effort.

Pre-frames

1 min
  • A pre-frame is the mindset you set up before a prospect moves on to the next stage. Every stage of a funnel serves as the pre-frame for the next one. That is why each should be tuned for both selling and credibility (trust that you are honest). Visitors who arrive with a strong frame convert at a much higher rate than those who arrive with a weak one.
  • The introduction a speaker receives strongly affects how much is sold at the back of the room. A bland introduction lowers energy and sales. A strong pre-sell (selling before the main offer) lifts them. Take control of the introduction with a short, prepared intro video that features endorsements and success stories. Play it just before going on stage.
  • Sell testimonials as pre-frames. Pay customers or students to write blog posts about their results. Or ask them to publish video reviews that include links and keywords (search words). Then drive traffic (send visitors) to those pages. Visitors arriving through someone else's endorsement convert much better.

Cold bridge pattern

1 min

Pattern: a cold bridge page translates a visitor's problem into the vocabulary of your offer. Start with a broad question about something the visitor wants. Then explain the jargon (special words) in plain words. Finally, show how the offer solves the problem with the new term.

Why it works: visitors only convert when they understand the terms the offer uses. So the bridge closes that knowledge gap.

  • Example 1: "Want a free website that earns money?" followed by "To earn from it, people must visit, and visitors are called traffic. Here is how to bring them in."
  • Example 2: "If you wake up with stinging feet at night, the usual cause is nerve damage called neuropathy. Here is what to do about it."

Traffic sources and their fit with awareness stages

1 min

Traffic foundation

2 min

  • A single traffic source is not enough for a reliable e-commerce traffic base. E-commerce means selling online. A business should combine several sources to reach prospects across the whole awareness pyramid.

  • ** A full e-commerce traffic foundation rests on five components:

    • paid search ads billed per click (you pay each time someone clicks)
    • unpaid search engine visibility
    • retargeting campaigns
    • building an email subscriber list together with email marketing
    • marketing through social media platforms

  • ** Traffic should reach prospects at every stage of the customer awareness pyramid, not only at the most aware stage. Each stage needs a page and message that matches where the prospect currently stands.

  • ** Targeting only the most aware buyers is a mistake. That group is small, the most competitive, and often hard to convert. If you are absent when they reach that stage, a competitor can take the sale.

  • ** The goal is omnipresence (being available at every point of the journey). Prospects should be able to find you at any point in the awareness pyramid. This keeps the market's attention on your brand throughout their decision.

  • ** The earlier a prospect is reached and given useful help, the longer they have to experience the business. That early exposure makes the brand more likely to be remembered when the prospect reaches the most aware stage.

  • ** The industry average is about seven touchpoints (each contact with a business) before a purchase in e-commerce. This is only an average. The real number varies a lot between businesses. Some need more, some fewer.

  • ** Prospects who have already interacted with a brand several times are more likely to buy when they reach the most aware stage, because trust has been built. A brand that appears only at the last moment must rely on having a clearly better offer than the alternatives the prospect has already seen.

  • ** Each traffic source must be matched with how the website engages visitors from that source. The content and landing experience should fit the awareness stage the traffic comes from.

  • ** Some businesses can take a prospect from first visit to purchase in a single session. Many e-commerce businesses cannot. Plan for multiple visits, because the commonly cited industry average is about seven touchpoints before a purchase.

Platform-specific matching

1 min
  • Google Shopping and paid search text ads point to product-level listings. Search text ads convert best when the landing page matches the search term closely. Usually that is a single product page for a specific term, or a narrow collection page (a page listing a group of products) for a broader term. A collection with hundreds of products makes the offer hard to find and lowers the chance of a sale.
  • Broad or long-tail keyword traffic (visitors from wide search terms, or long, specific search phrases) often converts poorly at first. The visitor must be guided through several steps of value before buying. Expect a lower immediate conversion rate after a traffic boost ends.
  • Smart links let traffic from specific ads or influencers (people with many followers who promote products) land on a customized landing page built for that source. Matching the page to the visitor's entry point is meant to improve conversion from the first moment on the site.
  • For influencer campaigns, keep the landing page simple. Use pictures or GIFs of the influencer using the product. Add customer reviews. Offer a coupon code (a discount code) that the influencer shares with their followers.
  • A website with only product pages engages only shoppers who are already ready to buy. Reaching people earlier in the customer journey, across the customer awareness pyramid, requires content (helpful pages such as articles).

Display and retargeting audiences

1 min
  • Use website visitor audiences (lists of people who visited your site) because they show prior intent (they showed interest before). Cold traffic on Display (banner-style ads on other websites) is nearly impossible to make profitable. So only run Display and Discovery (a Google ad format) to people who have already visited your store.
  • Retargeting is where the most-aware visitor is served the offer directly. See the ad-type table above.

Page types: product page, landing page, homepage and collections

1 min

Definitions and when each fits

3 min
  • A product page is a short page for one product. It shows a picture, a description, a price and an add-to-cart button (the button that puts the item in the basket).
  • A landing page is the page an ad sends visitors to. It has many sections, such as a header (the top area), a banner, bonuses, icons, features and reviews.
  • A hybrid page mixes both. It removes one step before checkout (the payment screen). But it is not always better.
  • Products that need explaining, such as health products (for example for back pain), often sell to more visitors (convert better) with an advertorial first. An advertorial is an ad written like a news article.
  • The advertorial explains how the product works before the product page. This adds one step.
  • Impulse-buy products (things people buy on a whim because they look nice) need the shortest path to the buy button.
  • The product page must do the selling. It uses its description, product pictures and product text (the copy).
  • A call to action is the button or words that ask the visitor to act. On a product page, the main call to action is to buy.
  • The offer is shown on the website, not in the ad.
  • A product page should get visitors who already know the solution (a fix exists) or the product.
  • Visitors who do not know there is a fix are "problem aware" (they know the problem) or "unaware" (they do not know the problem).
  • Explain the topic to them first. Then link them to the product page.
  • Traffic (visitors) must match the page.
  • A product page only fits visitors close to buying. Visitors close to buying are "hot" (ready to buy). Visitors who are just looking are "cold" (they have never heard of you).
  • Send broad search terms from the top of the funnel (the start of the buying path) to content or collection pages.
  • High-ticket buyers (people buying expensive items) rarely buy on impulse. Expect a longer gap between click and sale.
  • Plan for retargeting (showing ads again to people who already visited).
  • A website sells only if the offer and page match what the buyer needs at that point.
  • A product page cannot teach the whole market.
  • If a broad search term has no good fit, write content that compares options. Then link it to the product page.
  • Dropshipping means a supplier ships the product for you.
  • For direct response dropshipping (selling fast to people who respond to an offer), the main focus is the product or sales page at the buy stage.
  • It must ask for the sale quickly.
  • Funnels (a step-by-step path of pages) and retention pages (pages that bring customers back) come later, as the business grows.
  • Lead capture pages offer something free, such as an ebook, a white paper (a short report) or a webinar seat (a spot in a live online talk). In return, visitors give their contact details.
  • A long form sales page is a long page. It suits higher-priced single products that need more explaining before buying.
  • About 95% of paid visitors who land on a product landing page decide there. They either buy or leave.
  • Only about 5-10% look at other products.
  • So the landing page is the most important part of the funnel after the ad click.

Homepage versus product page

1 min
  • Do not send paid traffic to the store homepage (the front page of the shop).
  • Visitors cannot see the product right away. They cannot add it to the cart. Buying becomes harder.
  • Send them to a collection page (a page that lists related products, such as a bestseller collection) or straight to the product page.
  • A product page can be a better landing page than the homepage, when most traffic goes to it.
  • Test first: does direct-to-product traffic sell better?
  • Only then build a separate landing page for each product.

Digital products and collections

1 min
  • A digital product is a file or online item with no shipping.
  • Send traffic from one digital product to a product page. It should have bullet points, a screenshot or mockup (a picture showing the product in use), testimonials (customer reviews) and instructions for use on a tablet or phone.
  • Send traffic for preset-type products (ready-made photo editing settings) to a collection page.
  • Collection pages can serve as landing pages for new ad angles (the reason to buy), such as a feature customers care about.
  • You do not need a new page for each angle.
  • The same pages also work for email segmentation (sending different emails to different groups) and Google Ads campaigns.

Variants and separate landing pages

1 min
  • A variant is a version of a product, such as a different color or size.
  • Separate listings for each variant work well when the variants look clearly different. An example is vibrant colors that are easy to see.
  • Send traffic to a collection page that shows these separate listings.
  • A dedicated landing page works better when the product has emotional angles, such as love or mother and father gifts.
  • It also works when it targets one specific group of people.
  • Use GIFs (short looping animated pictures) and images to add emotion to the copy.
  • When variants confuse shoppers, or do not match what they expect, give each one its own landing page.
  • This makes clear what is available.
  • It usually raises conversion (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Case: A store had no currency settings (prices showed in the wrong money). It also had confusing variants, such as one product page with mixed sizes or colors.
  • Its conversion rate was 0.4% to 0.5%.
  • It created separate landing pages for each variant group.
  • It set up Shopify Payments (Shopify's built-in payment system) with local currency checkout (payment in the buyer's money).
  • The conversion rate rose to about 1.2%.
  • A separate variant page also helps when customers ask for options you do not have.

Learning which page sends the most traffic

1 min
  • Check each landing page's share of a brand's ads. This shows where its funnel really sends traffic.
  • In the example, one product page got 125 ads (81.2%).
  • A second page got 8 ads (5.2%).
  • The Top Landing Pages list shows the share of a brand's ads that link to each page.
  • In a dropshipping store, the top landing page got about 3% of traffic.
  • In established branded stores (known, long-running brands), one landing page can get around 30%.
  • A landing page that gets a large share of a brand's traffic is probably its best page.
  • Checking which page a competitor (another seller) sends most traffic to helps you find a proven landing page.

Pre-sell pages: advertorials, listicles, editorials and presells

1 min

A pre-sell page warms up visitors before the product page. The types below are all pre-sell pages.

Gaps in competitor funnels

1 min
  • If a competitor sends paid traffic straight to product pages, the gap is an opportunity.
  • It has no listicle (a list-style article, such as "7 ways to..."), advertorial or pre-sell page.
  • Build landing pages, advertorials or listicles that warm up cold visitors before the product page.
  • If a competitor sends paid traffic straight to its product page, an advertorial can beat it.
  • The advertorial agitates the problem (makes it feel more painful) before it shows the product.

Advertorials and editorial funnels

3 min
  • An editorial funnel is a pre-sell page placed before the product page.
  • It mixes article-style content with advertising.
  • It turns cold visitors into warm ones who are ready to buy.
  • The reader gets useful information first. The product pitch comes last.
  • An advertorial is an ad styled as an editorial (a news-style article).
  • It does not ask for the sale right away.
  • It warms up cold traffic. It teaches about the problem and the product, and it builds trust.
  • Then it collects data, such as an email, so the audience can be retargeted.
  • An editorial widens your market.
  • It answers objections (reasons people say no) and builds trust before any pitch.
  • This turns people who would otherwise leave.
  • It also reduces dependence on luck in ad targeting.
  • The page itself raises awareness of the problem. It explains why the product is the best fix.
  • Use the 70/30 rule.
  • About 70% of the page gives information and story.
  • Only the final 30% introduces the product and the buy option.
  • The reader must read the whole problem and fix before the pitch.
  • Then they are warm when they click.
  • Build the editorial on the AIDA sequence. AIDA means four steps: attention, interest, desire and action.
  • Each stage moves a cold reader one step closer to buying.
  • Skipping a stage leaves the reader unconvinced at the pitch.
  • In the desire stage (the part that makes the reader want it), handle objections.
  • Explain why existing solutions on the market fail to work well.
  • Explain why the new approach is different.
  • The product can be shown as a new method, without naming the brand early on.
  • Social proof (evidence that other people got results) in the desire stage should include:
    • a customer's photo and review
    • a results timeline (for example after one week and after two weeks)
    • graphs or data
    • a count of other customers who had good results
  • These elements make the claimed benefit believable.
  • In the action stage, show the product in the final 30%.
  • End with a clear call to action that leads to the product page. Examples: "check current availability" or "choose your package".
  • The earlier stages must already have built agreement that the problem is real.
  • Putting the product image, price and buy button at the top of a page for cold traffic makes visitors feel pushed.
  • The page then does not build the trust needed to convert them.
  • Lead with the problem and story instead.
  • Pitching the product before the reader agrees on the problem wastes the editorial.
  • The reader has no reason yet to care about the fix.
  • Always begin with the shared problem.
  • A salesy tone (pushy sales language) at the start makes the reader leave.
  • The page stops feeling like useful content.
  • Keep sales language for the final part.
  • Editorial funnels are an advanced step.
  • Add one only when your current funnel already converts well.
  • Use it to win the group of buyers that product pages miss.
  • Do not use it to fix a weak product page.
  • Case: A sleep-pain product store sent paid Facebook traffic to a story-style page, not to its product page.
  • The headline promised a surprising fix for pain-filled sleep.
  • The story introduced a character who fits the ideal customer (the buyer you want most).
  • She had a car accident years earlier. Now she has poor sleep and constant pain.
  • The problem was agitated with photos.
  • Then she visited a doctor, who recommended a foam pillow.
  • She tried it, and her sleep improved over time.
  • The page showed other customers with similar results.
  • The product was never pitched as the brand's own.
  • The final button said "check current availability" and led to the product page.

Presell pages and third-party pre-frames

2 min
  • A pre-frame is content that prepares a cold visitor before the offer.
  • A presell page educates before the sales page. It is a longer article that gives background or explains a concept.
  • For example: what email marketing is, and why it grows businesses.
  • Affiliates (people who sell other companies' products for a commission) use presell pages to warm up prospects.
  • Then they send them to a partner's sales page.
  • Articles on third-party sites (other people's websites) work as pre-frames for cold traffic from banner ads (picture ads on websites).
  • Article types include a case study (a real example story), a product explanation, or a piece that frames the problem without mentioning you.
  • Add the call to action as an inline link (a link inside the text), a button at the bottom, a linked photo, or a mix.
  • News-style pre-frames borrow attention from current events.
  • Linking your message to a timely story, such as an election or disaster, lifts attention while the story is hot.
  • The effect is short-lived.
  • A page that looks like a news page, with a headline bar or sidebar (side column), can boost credibility.
  • It must stay truthful.
  • Blog posts are a pre-frame tool for almost any topic.
  • A guest post (an article you write for someone else's blog) on a high-traffic blog in your target market works better than your own blog.
  • Publish it under the blog author's name.
  • Pair it with a link to your offer. Send targeted traffic (visitors who match your buyer) to the post.
  • Pre-frame videos work well for testimonials and for teaching.
  • Publish them on someone else's channel.
  • Place calls to action in the description or comments.
  • You can also use pre-roll (a video shown before the main video), post-roll (a video shown after it ends), or annotations (clickable notes on the video).
  • The video should show a problem or teach a process that creates desire for your fix.
  • Email pre-frames use solo ads (paid emails sent to another person's list) or joint venture partners (business partners who share a deal).
  • The partner endorses you to their list.
  • The partner's endorsement borrows their credibility (trust).
  • The call to action is a link in the email.
  • In direct mail (printed letters), the same role is played by a lift letter (a letter from the list owner).

Educational content and the one-page versus many-pages choice

1 min
  • Educational content, such as guides and how-to articles, helps customers choose the right product.
  • A site with strong guidance turns visitors into buyers.
  • Study content-rich stores that guide customers.
  • Adapt their approach to your niche (your small market).
  • ** Education-based marketing walks a prospect through the whole awareness pyramid (the steps from not knowing the problem to being ready to buy).
  • This can be done on one long page. But it needs strong copywriting (persuasive writing).
  • Several linked pages are usually easier, because each page needs less text.
  • A short quiz on the home page can ask a few questions about the visitor's situation.
  • Then it recommends the right product or funnel stage.
  • This helps undecided visitors who do not know which option fits.
  • A Learn section has short decision guides.
  • They cover how to choose, how to understand the product, and how to avoid costly mistakes.
  • They answer questions before the buyer leaves.
  • Each guide should link to the matching product or kit (a bundle of products).
  • Finish the section before ads point to the site.

Quizzes and free entry points

1 min
  • Quizzes with personalized results (results made for each person) can be an entry point (the first step a visitor takes).
  • This suits a high-ticket or education-heavy product.
  • In the example, the quiz ran on its own domain (its own web address): neurotest.me.
  • It took about 20 to 25 minutes.
  • It ended with custom results and product recommendations.
  • Step: Free digital course as an entry point.
  • Build the funnel in ClickFunnels (a tool for building sales pages and funnels).
  • The short course promises one clear result. In the example, the result is ending procrastination (putting things off) in three days.
  • It has three parts and one bonus part from a partner.
  • End the course with product offers.
  • Link the offer to a Shopify checkout (the payment step of a Shopify store) by using direct add-to-cart (a link that puts the item straight into the basket).
  • Fulfillment (packing and sending the order) then stays on one system.
  • For high-ticket items, enter the fulfillment details manually instead.
  • Free trial entry points can be scaled only with your own fulfillment.
  • Third-party logistics providers (outside companies that pack and ship for you) charge for each small task.
  • That makes sample or trial packets expensive.
  • A co-packer (a company that fills and packs products for you) can assemble trial packets from bulk product.
  • The trial sign-up asks only for an email address.
  • Test free-plus-shipping (the item is free, you pay shipping) and free-only versions.
  • See which converts better.

Blog posts and content CTAs

1 min

  • Blog posts that educate but end with no next step waste the traffic they already get.

  • Add an on-topic call-to-action block or an email capture (a sign-up box for an email).

  • Readers can then continue into the funnel.

  • Choose a call to action based on the reader's funnel stage.

  • Do not pick it just because a product button seems expected.

  • Readers of top-of-funnel articles have near-zero buying intent (almost no wish to buy yet).

  • So a high-priced product in an informational article will rarely convert.

  • Procedure: Map existing article content to funnel stages and CTAs. (An embed is a product box placed inside the article.)

    1. Ask the AI to classify each article by awareness stage, for example problem aware, solution aware or product aware, or by top, middle and bottom of funnel.
    2. Create a CTA for each stage, matching the reader's intent at that point.
    3. Map each stage to an email segment, so readers receive messages suited to where they are.
    4. Map each stage to product embeds or offers, placing products only where the reader is ready to consider them.

  • Example: A top-of-funnel article about a niche animal topic can carry an embedded signup for a topic newsletter (a regular email).

  • The promise is practical tips on the subject.

  • This captures interested readers, instead of asking for a sale they are not ready to make.

  • On top blog posts, pairing an on-topic product block with an email capture works better than a generic store banner (a general ad strip for the whole shop).

  • The product should match the article's subject.

  • For example: apparel for an ocean-themed article.

Funnel design and pre-frame structure

1 min

Funnel principles

2 min

  • Revenue of six to eight figures (hundreds of thousands to tens of millions of dollars) depends mostly on one thing.

  • That thing is whether you understand each phase of your funnel and can monetize each point along it (earn money from it).

  • Products, traffic sources, copy, website design and launch methods matter. But they are not what separates the levels.

  • Focus on designing the path each customer takes.

  • Problem-based marketing beats product-based marketing.

  • Do not sell product features, such as "take this pill for focus".

  • Instead, follow three steps:

    • Top of funnel: make the audience aware a problem exists. Share content about it.
    • Middle of funnel: show how life looks without the problem, and how to overcome it.
    • Bottom of funnel: present your offer as the solution.

  • Find problems with Google Trends (a free Google tool that shows what people search for), not products.

  • Each stage of a funnel is the pre-frame for the next one.

  • Use the seven-phase procedure below to structure the path:

    1. Determine traffic temperature (hot, warm or cold) and the awareness level of the prospect.
    2. Set up a pre-frame bridge matched to that temperature.
    3. Qualify (sort) subscribers with an opt-in offer that delivers something of value.
    4. Qualify buyers immediately after opt-in with a low-cost or free-plus-shipping offer.
    5. Identify hyperactive buyers (buyers who keep buying) through upsells (a bigger extra offer) and downsells (a smaller, cheaper offer) on the following pages.
    6. Age and ascend the relationship over time, moving customers up the value ladder (from cheap offers to bigger ones).
    7. Change the selling environment for high-ticket offers by moving to phone, direct mail or live events.

  • There are 23 interchangeable funnel components (parts you can swap).

  • They are sorted into four stages:

    • The pre-frame bridge
    • screening subscribers
    • screening buyers
    • spotting hyperactive buyers

  • You can combine them in any arrangement to fit a given offer.

  • Assemble the setup, run a test, change a single element, then test again.

  • Send all paid traffic to a squeeze page (a single page with one goal).

  • The visitor either gives an email address or leaves.

  • Remove menus, ads and any other links so nothing distracts from the opt-in.

  • Every paid click should become owned traffic (visitors whose contact details you have, so you can reach them again).

  • Track traffic and offers by the stage they are at:

    • A squeeze page captures an email.
    • A pre-sell or advertorial page warms cold traffic toward a sale.
    • A product page closes a visitor who is already ready.

  • Ad-level funnels should match the path.

  • A problem ad leads through educational content.

  • A solution ad leads to a landing page.

  • Retargeting leads straight to the offer.

Pre-frames within the funnel

1 min
  • The seven-phase procedure above treats each prospect's temperature as the first decision.
  • Stage-matched copy and page choices reinforce the pre-frame.
  • A visitor who arrives with a strong frame converts at a much higher rate.

Ad creative as a funnel stage

1 min
  • If a Kalodata rip campaign is saturated or not working, do not pause everything. (A rip campaign uses a winning TikTok product found with Kalodata, a TikTok product research tool. Saturated means people have seen the ad so often that they stop responding.)
  • Test bottom-of-funnel image ads instead.
  • You may capture sales from an audience that already knows the solution but is tired of watching videos.
  • Bottom-of-funnel image ads are cheaper to test (quick to make) than new video production.
  • Some stores build a separate product page per persona (a buyer type) or angle.
  • They keep the same layout and change only the copy.
  • Track each page separately to see which angle converts.
  • When one product sells to several audiences, keep the landing page structure identical.
  • Change only the copy and headline for each angle.
  • Then split-test (show two versions to split the traffic and compare) the variants to see which angle converts best.
  • The landing page must match the ad's angle.
  • With Page Fly (a Shopify page builder app), create one page theme per angle.
  • With other themes (page designs), create separate product pages and see which converts best.

Testing and scaling landing pages

1 min

Testing order

1 min
  • Test landing pages against each other after a first test.
  • Build more relevant variants for specific audiences.
  • Relevance between ad, audience and page is a key driver of performance.
  • Start with a standard original landing page.
  • Build listicles, advertorials and whitelist pages (a type of pre-sell page) only once daily revenue reaches roughly $500 to $1,000.
  • Below roughly $200 in daily revenue, do not spend time on listicles, advertorials or whitelist pages.
  • Focus on getting first sales with the original landing page.
  • Do not build a pre-lander (a page placed before the main page to warm visitors up) or pre-sales page until a single day has produced at least $500 in revenue.
  • Before that threshold, pre-landers add work without enough sales data to justify them.

Where the thresholds apply

1 min

The revenue thresholds apply at different stages and do not conflict:

Daily revenue What to build
Below roughly $200 Only the standard original landing page; no listicles, advertorials or whitelist pages
Roughly $500 to $1,000 Listicles, advertorials and whitelist pages may be built
At least $500 in one day A pre-lander or pre-sales page may be built
  • Plan up to three landing pages, one each for hot, warm and cold traffic.
  • Do this when several sources of different temperature are already running.
  • An editorial funnel is an advanced step that comes after the current funnel converts well.

Test steps for a page

1 min
  • Create at least two variants of a landing page and split-test them.
  • Use the same page structure. Change only the parts that differ between angles.
  • Test whether direct-to-product traffic converts better before building a separate landing page for each product.
  • For each ad set (a group of ads with the same audience and settings), record its awareness level and the page it lands on.
  • Then compare conversion by page.

Further details

1 min
  • Send cold traffic to the single simplified kit page, not to a page with many options.
  • One clear problem and one clear solution convert best for people who have never heard of the brand.
