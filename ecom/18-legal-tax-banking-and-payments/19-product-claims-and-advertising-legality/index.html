Product, claims and advertising legality

Section 18.19 · 19 of 24 in this chapter · 10 min read

In short

  • Check each product's legal status in every country before you build a store or ads.
  • Never promise cures, guaranteed income or results you cannot prove.
  • Never copy reviews or invent testimonials, numbers or doctors.
  • Check the official rules of every country you sell to, and keep your proof on file.

Restricted and prohibited products

1 min
  • Start every product decision with this filter.
    • Products with legal concerns face obstacles.
    • Payment processors (the companies that handle customers' card payments) are reluctant to approve them.
    • Big traffic and ad platforms restrict promotion. That limits how far you can grow.
  • Do not sell or list products that need a doctor's prescription (a written permission from a doctor). Customers may send fake prescriptions. You could be held legally responsible if the wrong person gets a prescription-only item. Avoid these products entirely.
  • Do not sell these categories: firearms, other weapons, knives, swords, adult products, vehicles, drug-use equipment, tobacco products or gambling products. Google, Shopify (a store-building platform) and Facebook ban promoting them. That blocks most of your marketing.
  • Fringe products (unusual or risky products) such as vaporizers, e-cigarettes and many supplements make it hard to keep a payment processor. PayPal and Shopify Payments can cut off your account when you sell them.
  • The source does not claim to know whether these products are legal in every market. Check the legal status yourself before choosing a product.
  • Paintball guns, bowhunting equipment, archery equipment, scuba diving tanks and hyperbaric chambers (sealed pressure chambers used for medical treatment) can fall under weapon, pressure-vessel (tanks that hold high pressure) or medical-device (health-care equipment) rules in some countries and on some marketplaces. Check the legal status and platform policy (the rules of the site you sell on) in the target market (the country you sell to) before building a store around them.
  • Self-defense products, such as a keychain, can count as weapons in the UK and US. Check the weapons laws of every target country before listing. A product that is illegal to sell in a market cannot be marketed there. Verify this before building ads.

Certifications, licensing and regulated categories

2 min

Compliance sequence for a regulated product (compliance means following the rules; regulated means the government sets rules for it):

  1. Confirm the required certifications (official approval papers) for your country and category.
  2. Work with your supplier, or hire a China-based testing agency (a company that tests products) to handle compliance testing.
  3. Check licensing (official permission to sell) or extra requirements on official government sources in your country. Do not rely on forum guesses for licensing questions.
  4. Verify legal compliance for every target country before launch (the first day of sales).

Additional rules:

  • When moving from dropshipping (selling products that others make and ship for you) to manufacturing branded products (products with your own brand name), secure FDA approvals (permission from the US Food and Drug Administration) for regulated product categories. Also create instruction booklets, and verify legal compliance for all target countries.
  • Child-related products, such as car seats, cribs, bassinets (small baby beds), playpens, high chairs, toddler beds and baby rockers, are usually subject to child safety standards (required safety rules). For example, federal crib and car seat rules in the US. Check the required certificates and labeling (the printed safety information) for the target country before listing them.
  • Product claims for DIY (do-it-yourself), building or safety-related projects must be checked against local building codes (local construction rules) and safety standards before publication. Add a disclaimer (a short warning statement) that projects must be confirmed with local regulations before work starts.
  • Google and Meta restricted-category policies show which products need extra steps, such as disclaimers or policy agreements. Many restricted products can still be sold after those steps.
  • California Proposition 65 warnings (a California law requiring warnings about certain chemicals) may be required on product pages. Ask your suppliers which warning wording they recommend. A warning can be shown as plain text on the site, or through an app (a small add-on for your store) that offered a single free warning. (Older advice, 2025 source; no age mark applies.)

Health, medical and supplement claims

3 min

Claim rules:

  • Health or pain-relief claims, such as 'remove pain', 'make leg straight again' or other medical promises, will eventually trigger account suspension (your account gets blocked). This happens even if sales occur at first. These claims violate platform policies.
  • Avoid bold health claims in ad creative (the pictures, words and video of an ad), captions and voiceover (spoken narration). Examples include saying a product will make you lose weight or cure a condition. Use softer phrasing that describes relief or benefit instead.
  • Ad copy (the sales words) must not claim results such as 'clinically proven' (proven by proper clinical tests) unless there is evidence to back them. Unsupported medical or health claims in ads can get the ad rejected or create legal risk.
  • Verify health or science claims with laboratory tests or qualified review (checks by qualified experts) before using them. Unverified claims can be refused by respected media or challenged. Keep the test records on file.
  • Health-related product claims may need legal disclaimers. Ask the manufacturer (the company that makes the product) for its compliance documents rather than adding claims without legal backing.
  • Copying health or supplement claims from competitor pages (other sellers' pages) creates regulatory (government rule) and advertising risk, especially when the claims cannot be proven. Publish only health claims you can substantiate (prove). Remove any claim you cannot back.
  • For health and finance products, avoid health or financial result claims in headlines and page copy. Legal rules on what can be promised differ by country. Regulators can act against non-compliant claims.
  • Products in the health space allow fewer claims in copy. So the wording of benefits must be checked against those limits. Mapping still works for unfamiliar, technical or expensive products, but health-related benefits must be phrased with restraint (carefully, with few promises).

Safer wording:

Avoid Safer direction Ids
Detoxifying the body, releasing toxins, healing tissue Neutral wording such as 'a relaxing experience'; or have evidence ready and check each country's ad rules
Losing weight, curing a condition, removing pain Softer phrasing that describes relief or benefit ,
'Clinically proven' without evidence Use only with real evidence on file

Supplements:

  • Verify each product's ingredients (the substances inside it) with third-party identity testing (a separate lab checks what is really inside) to confirm the active ingredients (the main working substances) are present. This is harder for botanicals (plant-based ingredients), but it is stronger proof than a label alone.
  • For US supplement ads and product pages, add a disclaimer that the statements have not been evaluated by the FDA and that the product is not meant to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. Place the same wording on ad creatives and landing pages (the page a visitor reaches after clicking an ad) before launch.
  • Health-related ebooks and supplement gifts should carry a note that the content is educational and does not replace advice from a qualified healthcare professional. Keep product claims soft and framed as support, not as a fix.
  • Do not build character pages (pages built around an invented persona) with fake medical authority, such as an invented doctor, for medical treatments, supplements or health claims. This can imply endorsement (approval) of unapproved treatments, may be illegal and may breach platform terms. Non-medical authority, such as a parent or an experienced hobbyist, is much safer.

Income, finance and results claims

1 min
  • Income claims cannot be guaranteed. Promising returns (money earned back) to customers or prospects (people who may buy) is not allowed. Describe only results you can measure.
  • For Meta ads (ads on Facebook and Instagram) promoting financial products to prospects aged 55+, the following create FTC liability (legal responsibility to the US Federal Trade Commission):
    • Fabricated testimonials (made-up customer praise), unverified star ratings, review counts and unattributed social proof (praise with no named source).
    • Use only real, verified social proof (praise and signs that other people trust) with documentation, or leave social proof empty.
    • Fabricated evidence is an FTC violation, not just a weak ad idea.

Reviews, testimonials and endorsements

1 min
  • Never copy reviews from a competitor's website, a competitor's supplier site, Amazon or any other online store. This is not allowed and can lead to legal trouble.
  • Reviews imported from a supplier's marketplace (a site where many sellers offer products) and shown as your customers' reviews can breach consumer-protection rules (rules that protect buyers) against fake reviews, such as the US FTC rule in force since October 2024, and platform policies. Show only genuine reviews, or clearly label imported reviews as coming from another source.
  • Attribute quotes, reviews or expert bylines (the name shown as author) only to real people who agreed to them. Keep every claim consistent with what the product actually does. Invented doctors or fake testimonials count as misleading advertising in most markets. The same applies to fabricated statistics (made-up numbers) or news-style sources.
  • Do not publish invented testimonials, made-up customer counts or fabricated guarantees, such as '2,000,000+ happy customers'. These claims can mislead buyers and create legal risk under consumer-protection and advertising rules. Use only reviews and counts you can verify.
  • Every specific claim must be true and provable. Readers take specific facts at face value, and the advertiser is accountable for them. Publish only figures that come from real tests or measurements.

Comparative claims and patent language

1 min
  • Comparative claims (claims that compare you to another product), such as 'produces more than the closest competitor', need test results or documented evidence behind them before they go into product copy or ads. Keep the test report on file.
  • Use the label Patent Pending (a note that a patent application is filed) only while a patent application for that feature is filed and not yet granted. Do not describe a feature as patented (legally protected as an invention) until the patent has actually been granted.

Fake news sites, advertorials and disguised mailings

1 min
  • Use legitimate articles on legitimate websites. An advertorial is an ad written to look like an article. Never create fake news sites or fake blogs to disguise advertising. Regulators such as the FTC have acted against this practice, and it damages business credibility. Ethics and honesty protect the long-term business.
  • Disguised mailings (letters that hide that they are ads) annoy some readers, especially in small markets where people talk to each other. Limit disguised mailings to targeted prospects or customers who will be interested once they open the mail. This reduces annoyance and raises response (how many people reply or buy).
  • Before writing any legal consequence into a letter, check the laws in your own country: debt collection (rules for chasing unpaid money), consumer protection (rules that protect buyers) and fraud (rules against cheating). Historical letters threatened criminal charges that may not fit current rules. Describe only actions you are legally allowed to take. (Older advice, 2019-2022 letters.)

Giveaways, discounts and subscriptions

1 min

Giveaways (free prize contests):

  1. Write giveaway terms and conditions (the official rules) that satisfy the legal requirements of each country where entries are accepted. Adapt the terms per country.
  2. Award the advertised quantity of product. The landing page and the ads must state that about nine out of ten entrants will lose, and the same disclosure must appear in the terms and conditions.

Discounts:

  • Before launching any discount, check that it complies with the consumer-law rules of each market you sell in. For Australia, this means the ACCC requirements (rules of Australia's consumer and competition regulator). Treat this check as part of launch preparation, not an afterthought.

Subscriptions:

  • Undisclosed (not clearly stated) subscription terms on a product page lead to unexpected recurring charges (repeat payments), one-star reviews and refund requests. State the subscription, billing frequency (how often you charge) and cancellation rules clearly before checkout (the payment step).

Shipping promises

1 min
  • Mention fast, insured shipping (shipping with insurance) in product or search copy. In many countries, including Australia and the US, the seller must replace a shipment that goes missing, even if the customer did not pay for insurance. The seller therefore carries that insurance cost anyway.

General rule for every market:

  • Verify the import, licensing and tax rules for your specific product and country before selling.
  • Check the legal status of each product in each target market before building ads or a store around it.
