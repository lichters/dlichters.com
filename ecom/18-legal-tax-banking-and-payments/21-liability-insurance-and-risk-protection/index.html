Liability, insurance and risk protection

Section 18.21 · 21 of 24 in this chapter · 6 min read

In short

  • Get several quotes before you buy any insurance, because prices differ a lot.
  • Most lawsuit risk comes from your own business: rules for customers, legal compliance, brand ownership and repeat payments. Fix these first.
  • If your supplier does not cover you, you need your own product liability cover.
  • An LLC protects you only when you keep business and personal money separate.

Insurance types for e-commerce

  • The main e-commerce insurance types are Product Liability Insurance, which pays if your product hurts someone, and D&O (Directors and Officers) Insurance, which protects company leaders. The source wrote "DNO (Direct Officer)", but D&O is the correct name.
  • Recall insurance covers the cost of pulling products back from customers. It exists but is very hard to get.
  • Product liability insurance is optional. If you want it, compare quotes from several insurers.

Where lawsuits come from

  • As a successful business, you will be sued. The claim may be unfair. It can come from an accident, a competitor or a baseless claim. Expect it, and prepare by keeping legal reserves, which is money set aside for legal costs.
  • Most lawsuit risk in e-commerce comes from the business itself:
    • Terms of service, the rules customers agree to on your site.
    • Compliance, following the laws that apply to you.
    • Intellectual property, which means brand names, logos and designs that belong to someone.
    • Subscriptions, where customers pay repeatedly.
  • It does not mainly come from events you cannot control. Find and fix the risks you can control.
  • Lawsuits against a small e-commerce business are rare, but the money cost can be severe. This is a low-chance, high-impact risk. Choosing the right business structure protects you against it.
  • Customers in the US can sue almost anyone. Whether to buy general liability cover (insurance for injury or property damage claims) depends on your risk tolerance, meaning how much risk you accept.
  • If a product could cause injury, a lawsuit may name every party a lawyer can find. This includes the seller and the supplier, the company that makes or sends your products.
  • People who sue, called plaintiffs, often sue every party in the chain, including the store.

Supplier protection

  • Suppliers do not protect you from product liability claims.
  • If you white label through a manufacturer, you sell its product under your brand. The manufacturer will often cover your product liability under its umbrella insurance policy, a large policy that also covers others.
  • If the manufacturer does not cover you, you need your own product liability insurance. It is usually not very expensive.

Product liability and product safety

  • Assess product liability by asking every way the product could injure someone, including misuse. Also consider every person who might use it, not only the buyer.
  • Print all required warnings on the product to protect your company.
  • Products sold in plastic bags need a suffocation hazard warning printed on the packaging.
  • Confirm the exact legally required wording. Do not rely on memory. Budget money to get all required warnings right.
  • Avoid products that could be dangerous. If that risk worries you, consider a general liability policy.

Business structure and asset separation

  • Business liability insurance depends on three things: the business entity (the legal form of your company), the expected liability and your risk tolerance. Consult a professional before buying policies, because brokers are salespeople. A broker is a middleman who sells policies.
  • Choosing a business entity is similar to choosing insurance. The owner decides.
  • Run the business through an LLC (limited liability company, a structure that separates owner and business debts) or an S corporation (another US business structure). Keep business and personal expenses separate.
  • Consider general liability insurance once the business holds meaningful assets, meaning valuable things it owns. Confirm the approach with a professional.
  • An LLC or S-corp limits personal liability only when assets are kept separate. Consult an accountant once the business earns money.

General liability insurance: is it required?

  • General liability insurance is optional for a US e-commerce business. Some suppliers require it before they will work with you.
  • It is optional and depends on supplier requirements.
  • Most suppliers do not require business insurance.
  • If a gold supplier requires it, request general liability quotes from the insurer you already use for home or auto cover. A gold supplier is a supplier that pays for a premium listing on a supplier marketplace.
  • It is optional. It depends on risk versus reward, meaning how much you have to lose.
  • It is a personal decision. The business can run without it, though it may help depending on the risk.
  • It is optional and depends on where you operate.
  • It is recommended for peace of mind.
  • It is useful as the business grows.
  • Business insurance is not required for dropshipping in the US. For the UK, the source knew of no requirement. Check a local answer.

  • The $30 figure applies to a small store. The $100 figure applies to providers offering coverage for large sales volumes. Both are older figures.
  • Set coverage size according to revenue, which is your total sales income. The insurer will ask about it.

Buying without brokers

Sources differ on which channel to use. They agree on two things: avoid small or unvetted brokers and compare several quotes.

  • Use an independent quote-comparison website. Choose the lowest price for suitable coverage. A higher price does not mean better protection.
  • Buy from a large direct insurer rather than through a broker. Commissions, the fee the broker keeps from your payment, make broker policies expensive.
  • Get general liability quotes from the same insurer that holds your personal insurance, such as auto or home. This usually gives better rates and less risk of being taken advantage of than online small business brokers.
  • Ask the insurer you already use for home, auto or life policies for a business policy. Compare it with other quotes.
  • Start with a large insurer you already use for other insurance.
  • Shop through major agencies such as Geico for Business, or ask connections in insurance.
  • Avoid small brokerages and use major agencies.
  • Be careful with brokers who charge high premiums, meaning high prices for your policy. Compare multiple quotes before buying.
  • Brokers can overcharge heavily for weak coverage.
  • Agents may overcharge for policies you do not need.
  • Buy business insurance without overpaying:
    1. Get a general, catch-all business policy first, since it covers the basics.
    2. Ask several insurance brokers for quotes, not only one.
    3. Be careful with agents who offer every policy they can sell. Many coverages may be unnecessary.
    4. Review coverage again as revenue grows. Insurers may audit the business, and premiums can rise.
  • Start with a small general liability policy. It is a cheaper starting point than a bundle of policies. More coverage can be added later. Pick coverage based on actual risk.

Premium audits and renewals

A premium is the price you pay for a policy. A premium audit is when the insurer checks your sales later and may bill you extra.

  • Insurance audits can happen retroactively, meaning they look back at past sales, based on revenue growth. They can result in bills above $10,000 for past premiums, even if no claims occurred.
  • A revenue audit on a broker-sold policy can lead to tens of thousands in extra premiums.
  • Do not buy general liability through a broker. A revenue audit after the policy starts can produce large extra premiums.
  • A carrier or broker audit after the first year can produce a large retroactive premium bill when sales grow. Here, the carrier is the insurance company. Check how audits and premiums are calculated before signing.
  • Before buying, ask the provider how year-end premium audits adjust the price.
  • Check the premium once revenue grows. Low-price quotes can produce large bills at audit.
  • A cheap first-year rate can increase sharply later.
  • Quotes can rise sharply at renewal. Renewal means extending the policy for another period. Compare quotes from several insurers.

Shipping insurance

  • Shipping insurance is important for expensive products. It covers damage claims, so you can resolve them without a full return.
  • Check the carrier's policy before deciding who pays. Here, the carrier is the shipping company. Carrier shipping insurance policies decide whether a damaged item in transit is refunded along with the shipping costs.

Licensing for food and special categories

  • Before selling food or beverage products, check the licenses and regulations that apply. They add steps beyond normal drop shipping, which means selling products that a supplier ships directly to the customer.
