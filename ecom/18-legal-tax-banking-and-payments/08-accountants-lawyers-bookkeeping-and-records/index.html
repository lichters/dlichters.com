Accountants, lawyers, bookkeeping and records

Section 18.8 · 8 of 24 in this chapter · 12 min read

In short

  • Ask a professional, not a mentor, for tax and legal questions. Course notes are only a starting point.
  • Hire an accountant once the store earns money. Check that they know e-commerce and your state or country.
  • Keep clean records from the very first sale. Save every receipt, payout and cost.
  • Use separate bank accounts for separate money. This keeps tax money from being spent by mistake.
  • Ask a lawyer for a phone call if their email answer is vague.

Working with Lawyers

1 min
  • Get legal and tax advice from an attorney who specializes in e-commerce, taxes, and policies.
  • Ask for referrals. Do not rely on a general family-friend lawyer.
  • Legal advisory subscriptions with unlimited chat are not needed for a typical startup.
  • A business owner may only need to speak to a lawyer about once every few years.

Lawyer communication process

  1. Outline your current situation and questions to the lawyer in bullet points.
  2. The lawyer will reply via email listing risks and disclaimers, because they hedge via email to protect themselves.
  3. If it is unclear after the email, demand clarification and push back on vague language.
  4. The lawyer will typically call to give actual advice, because the phone call creates a record of their liability for accuracy.
  5. Keep large email chains as documentation.
  • If a lawyer hedges (gives vague, safe answers) and you remain unclear, push back and demand clarity.
  • Vague risk language is not actionable guidance. Actionable means you can act on it.
  • Get a phone call where they state their actual advice.
  • If you pursue a lawyer for malpractice (bad professional work), they carry professional liability insurance. This is insurance for mistakes at work.
  • The insurance claim is your remedy, which means the way to get compensation.
  • That makes the written advice important documentation.

Cash vs Accrual Accounting

1 min
  • Cash-based accounting records income and expenses when money physically enters and leaves your accounts.
  • Accrual-based accounting records income when it is earned, and the matching expenses in the same period.

Separating Entities and Stores

1 min
  • Keep store income separate from other businesses, in a different entity (a separate company).
  • Mixed bookkeeping makes it hard to show clean numbers when you later sell a store.
  • Store owners who plan to sell should keep the company and bookkeeping together, if the stores are related.
  • Separate accounting, such as one QuickBooks account per store, only makes sense for stores that would be sold on their own.
  • Speak with an accountant before choosing a business structure.
  • The best entity type depends on the country and the owner's situation.
  • An accountant can also take over bookkeeping later.

Record-Keeping and Receipts

1 min
  • Bookkeeping serves the owner first and the tax authority second.
  • So clean records are needed from day one.
  • Records should make profit and expenses clear at any time.
  • Poor bookkeeping from the first sale can lead to large unexpected tax bills later.
  • Keep proper accounting records from the very first sale, not fixing them later.
  • Keep the receipts for all business software, courses and tools.
  • An accountant can check whether they are deductible in your country.
  • Such costs are often tax-deductible business expenses in most countries.

Reading the Financial Statements

1 min
  • Clearing accounts hold money from payment providers such as Shopify, Klarna (a payment service) or PayPal.
  • That money is earned but has not yet reached the bank.
  • Show clearing accounts as current assets. Current assets are things that turn into cash soon.
  • Track them, because they should turn into cash within days.
  • Owner draws (money the owner takes out) and profit distributions (profit paid to owners) are financing activities.
  • They are not operating expenses.
  • They appear in the financing section of the cash flow statement. This report shows real money in and out.
  • Suppliers and advertising are often paid on credit cards.
  • Card balances rise as orders come in and fall when the cards are paid off.
  • The financing section shows this build-up and repayment.
  • A large positive financing figure means more borrowing than repayment.
  • A PPP loan is a COVID-era government stimulus loan in the US. Stimulus means government money to help the economy.
  • A PPP loan appears as a liability (money you owe) on the balance sheet. The balance sheet is a snapshot of what you own and owe.
  • Any government stimulus or grant funds received should be tracked as liabilities or equity (the owner's stake) until their treatment is settled.
  • Interest expense on loans and card balances appears in operating expenses in the example.
  • Review it alongside the debt balances to understand what is being paid on borrowed money.
  • In the example store, merchant account (card processing account) fees of 46,733 were about 2.6 percent of gross sales (all sales before costs).
  • Those fees were about 3 percent of total income.
  • Card fees are part of cost of goods sold (COGS, the direct cost of the product sold).
  • So card fees reduce gross profit (sales minus COGS), along with product cost and shipping.

Profit First Account Allocation

2 min
  • Profit First is a method that splits income into set buckets. Allocation means dividing money into buckets.
  • Keep a separate bank account for each allocation bucket.
  • This way, money for suppliers, taxes, owner pay, staff and operating costs cannot be spent elsewhere.
  • A single mixed account defeats the system.
  • Some buckets, such as profit, can be savings accounts instead of checking accounts.
  • In this system, the tax account holds money for the owner's personal income tax on the business's earnings.
  • So the business sets that money aside before it is spent.
  • Set the tax percentage at every allocation, so the bill can be paid in full when it is due.

The seven-account Profit First setup

Account Purpose Money comes in from Paid out from it
Income Receives all sales payouts Shopify, PayPal, Klarna deposits Transfers to the other accounts only
Cost of goods (COGS) Supplier costs COGS percentage of income Supplier invoices and supplier cards
Profit Reward for owning the business Profit percentage of real revenue Distributed quarterly
Owner pay Regular salary for the owner Owner pay percentage of real revenue Paid to the owner at every allocation
Taxes Tax reserve Tax percentage of real revenue Tax payments when due
Staff Payroll Staff percentage of real revenue Payroll
Operating expenses All other business costs Operating percentage of real revenue Operating cards and bills

Procedure: beginner setup

  1. Open three accounts: an income account, a COGS account, and a profit account.
  2. Send all payouts from Shopify, PayPal, and other payment sources to the income account.
  3. Start by moving 1% of income into the profit account. This should be easy to keep without missing it.
  4. Move 80% to 90% of income into the COGS account as a starting estimate. Adjust it based on what you observe.
  5. Add the owner pay, taxes, staff, and operating expenses accounts as you get comfortable with the system.
  • A starting profit allocation of 1% should be easy to keep.
  • If it cannot be kept, the business likely has a serious underlying problem.
  • The 80% to 90% COGS starting range is an experiment, not a fixed rule.

Outsourcing Accounting

  • Accounting is a strong outsourcing candidate (a task to hand to someone else).

  • The owner can still review bank balances and financial reports.

  • Do not use a home-made spreadsheet for this. Real software is the first step of professional bookkeeping.
