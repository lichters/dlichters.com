Privacy, cookies, GDPR and marketing consent

Section 18.17 · 17 of 24 in this chapter · 9 min read

In short

  • Find out which countries your customers live in. Each country has its own privacy rules.
  • Cookie banners and consent matter most for EU customers. Ask a qualified lawyer about each country.
  • Never send texts or emails without clear permission. Never buy email lists.
  • Your privacy policy must match the tools you really use. Never publish the default text unchanged.
  • Keep your terms and billing rules clear, and make your site usable for people with disabilities.
This section has 12 parts. Tap one to open it.

Start With the Regions You Sell To

1 min
  • If you sell to customers in the EU (the European Union, a group of European countries), cookie consent and GDPR apply. Cookie consent means asking visitors for permission before tracking them with cookies (small files that follow visitors around). GDPR is the EU's strict data protection law. Check the requirements for each target market (each country you aim to sell to) before you decide anything else.
  • Regional compliance (following each region's rules) varies widely. For subscriptions (customers who pay on a repeating schedule) and data collection (gathering customer information), check US state by state, the EU, Australia and other markets, because lawsuits target this area.
  • For each country you sell to, check the legal requirement with a qualified professional. This material is not legal advice.
  • Apps such as Recart, SMSBump and Klaviyo (tools that send messages and manage customer lists) give country-specific guidance for legal text (the wording of legal pages). One account with a common privacy policy section that includes each country's requirements is usually enough, rather than separate accounts per country.
  • GDPR is a real requirement for stores that handle personal data (any information that identifies a person, such as a name or email address) of EU visitors. Shopify (online store software) and Google Ads (Google's paid advertising) terms cover part of this, but the store must still meet the remaining obligations in its own policies.
  • GDPR covers stores that serve European customers.
  • CCPA (California's consumer privacy law) applies to California customers. Check whether it applies to your business.

Shopify Customer Privacy Settings

1 min
  • Shopify's Preferences page (the settings page of your online store) has a Customer privacy section with two options.
    • Limit tracking for customers in Europe: requires consent under GDPR before tracking.
    • Limit the third-party sale of California customers' data: applies under CCPA. Third-party sale means selling data to outside companies.
  • Enable the setup like this (source from 2021-22, marked older):
    1. Go to Online Store > Preferences and find Customer privacy.
    2. Enable the EU option (Limit tracking for customers in Europe) if you sell to Europe. GDPR requires consent before tracking. **
    3. Enable the California option (Limit the third-party sale of your California customers' data) if you sell to California. CCPA requires an opt-out (a way for people to say no to their data being sold). **
  • Under Settings > Customer privacy, the current Shopify settings include:

Data sales opt-out page Active for certain states (a page where people can say no to their data being sold) California, Colorado and 6 other states
Data storage hosting location Set here (the country where customer data is kept on computers) United States

  • Shopify calls its default privacy settings recommended. These settings are a starting point, not legal advice. The store owner stays responsible for complying with the privacy and marketing laws that apply to customers' countries.

Cookie Consent Banners

1 min
  • A cookie consent banner is a pop-up at the bottom of the page where visitors accept cookie tracking. It matters most when you sell into the EU, where cookie opt-in (visitors must say yes first) is required. For other markets, check the requirement with a lawyer.
  • Banners are mainly required under EU law.
  • Cookie consent pop-ups and tracking disclosures (notices that say what is tracked) come from privacy regulations such as GDPR.
  • Enable cookie consent in the Shopify theme settings (the design settings of your store) if you sell outside the US, especially in the EU. It is optional for a US-only demo store (a test store for the US only).
  • The cookie consent toggle (an on/off switch) is in the footer settings (the settings for the bottom of every page). It switches the consent banner on or off. Switch it on when the business operates out of the United States.
  • A cookie consent app was not used for a US-based store. Cookie consent tools (apps that show the banner) are usually required by laws in other countries, so check your jurisdiction (which country's laws apply) before you skip them.
  • Do not switch off the banner before you check the requirement for each target market.
  • For an older theme (the design of your store) that has no banner option, ask Shopify support how to add one. Do not build it yourself.

Privacy Policy Content

1 min
  • The privacy policy (the public page that explains how you handle customer data) must explain:
    • what customer data is collected,
    • how it is used and stored,
    • cookies (small files stored in the browser) and third-party services (outside tools that handle data),
    • how customers can request access to or deletion of their data.
  • The privacy policy must be fully compliant.
  • Monitor Robert Frude, a lawyer on Twitter, for subscription lawsuit patterns. This shows which compliance failures lead to litigation (legal cases).
  • Do not publish the generated Shopify privacy policy as it is.
    1. Compare the generated text with the store's real tools: remarketing (showing ads again to past visitors), Google Analytics (Google's visitor statistics tool) and cookies.
    2. Add any missing sentences that describe these tools.
  • If you run remarketing campaigns, the privacy policy must disclose that you:
    • use cookies,
    • work with third-party vendors (outside companies) that gather data from cookies,
    • use cookies to personalize ad experiences.
  • If the store runs remarketing ads, the personal data paragraph must end with a sentence saying that personal data may be used for remarketing. The default template may not include it, so check the generated text before you publish.
  • Place privacy text about cookies or consent under the section that describes information collected or how it is used. Check the template Shopify provides, because the wording changes over time.
  • The privacy policy must include the behavioral advertising section (a part about ads based on what people do online) when Google requires it, or when the compliance rules in this handbook say so.
  • Use this security statement as a template (a ready-made text to copy). Insert it as written.
Our systems apply industry-standard encryption and safeguards so personal data stays away from unauthorized access, changes or disclosure. Card and payment details are kept safe and can be reached only by approved staff.

Meta, Facebook Pixel and Data Sharing

1 min
  • Customers can stop their website activity from being shared with Meta (the company behind Facebook and Instagram). They do this in the Off-Facebook Activity settings of their own Facebook account. The store cannot switch this off for them, so pixel data (visitor information sent by the Facebook pixel, a small tracking code) is limited for those customers.
  • Choose the dataset category (the type label for your data set in Meta) that matches your business most accurately. The category decides what data may be shared with Meta. Depending on the category and location, data sharing can be limited or blocked. Never send sensitive user data in events (signals sent to Meta about customer actions).
  • use the customer privacy setting that allows the highest level of data sharing, so that Facebook tracking captures the most data. The Shopify Facebook pixel must be enabled under Online Store preferences. **
  • Conflict: the 2022 advice maximizes data capture. The 2026 advice limits sharing by category and location and forbids sensitive data in events. Follow the 2026 advice where the two conflict. The 2022 setting is only the older route to the pixel.

Email and SMS Consent

1 min

SMS

  • SMS (text messages sent to phones) sales messages need consent. Show the consent in two places:
    • in checkout, next to the phone number field, with a short note on how the number will be used,
    • in the privacy policy and terms.
  • Keep SMS abandoned cart messages (texts sent to customers who left before paying) disabled until SMS consent is set up correctly. Sending SMS without consent can lead to fines.
  • A pre-ticked SMS consent checkbox (a box already ticked by default) is not valid consent under GDPR. Leave the phone marketing opt-in unticked so the customer must actively agree.

Email

  • The pre-chat form (the form visitors fill in before a live chat) can include a checkbox that opts visitors in (signs them up) to marketing emails. Checking it adds them as email subscribers. In one 2023 setup, the default setting for this opt-in was left unchanged. Review that default before you go live.
  • Never add purchased or rented email lists to a store's marketing. They hurt email deliverability (whether emails reach the inbox) and sender reputation (how much email providers trust you). They also make it harder for legitimate opt-in subscribers to receive messages.

Anti-Spam Requirements

1 min
  • Automated marketing emails must show a valid business address. Anti-spam law (laws against unwanted messages) in the US, Australia and the EU requires it. Drip or Klaviyo (email tools) insert the address from the Shopify account. **
  • Removing the tawk.to app (a live chat tool) triggers a request that erases customers' personal data held by tawk.to after 48 hours. **

Subscription Compliance

1 min
  • Subscription compliance is critical. If subscriptions are a substantial part of revenue (income) and non-compliant (not following the rules), such as with unclear billing terms, difficult cancellation or poor disclosure (unclear information), one lawsuit can destroy the business.
  • Check your billing terms (how and when you charge), cancellation path (how customers cancel) and disclosures against the rules of each region you sell to.

User-Generated Content

1 min

User-generated content means anything customers create for your store, such as reviews and photos.

  • Add a user-generated content clause (a rule in your terms and conditions) to the terms and conditions. **
  • The clause states that any content a customer provides becomes the business's property once they buy. This covers:
    • reviews,
    • images,
    • video,
    • survey responses,
    • social comments. **
  • The clause also protects the business if a customer later disputes (challenges) the use of their content. **

AI Tools and Customer Data

1 min
  • Assume that any data you put into an LLM (large language model, the AI behind chatbots) carries some risk, because AI data handling is ambiguous (unclear). Claude is currently among the safest options because of Anthropic's commitment to data protection. Understand and accept this risk before you proceed.
  • Before you paste any data into an AI tool:
    1. Anonymise the brand name if needed (remove details that identify it).
    2. Remove confidential information (secret details).
    3. Know your own data handling rules first. Data sent to an AI tool leaves your account.

Call Recording

1 min
  • Recording customer calls without informing the caller can be illegal in many places. Check local law before you record calls.
  • This section describes a recording service with unreliable quality, and it does not recommend it.

ADA Accessibility

1 min
  • ADA (a US law on accessible websites for people with disabilities) lawsuits target websites that cannot be read. Companies may receive compliance letters (formal demands for fixes), which force expensive responses, or they may be sued directly.
  • Invest in website accessibility (making the site usable for everyone) and in terms of service (the rules customers agree to) that people can read.
