Selling the business: legal, tax and transaction matters

Section 18.23 · 23 of 24 in this chapter · 6 min read

In short

  • Keep clean, complete financial records from day one. Buyers often want four years of them.
  • Choose before listing: asset sale (you sell parts of the business) or stock sale (you sell the whole company). Asset sales are simpler.
  • Use escrow (a neutral holder keeps the money until the transfer is checked) to protect both sides.
  • A broker's fee is 15% on stores under $1 million. A private sale avoids that fee, but you lose the broker's buyer list and protections.

Preparing the Store for Sale

  • Keep accurate, complete books (the record of all money in and out) from the start. Brokers and marketplaces usually require several years of complete financial records. The example given was four years. Older guidance from 2021 to 2022 gives the same requirement.
  • Store all supplier invoices (bills from your product suppliers) in Google Drive, so they are easy to show during buyer due diligence (the buyer's own checks before buying) and verification.
  • Turn off WHOIS privacy (the setting that hides your name in the public record of who owns a web address) in the last month before listing. Your own name must show as domain owner. Buyers look up WHOIS to confirm ownership.
  • Make sure you can be verified as a seller: real social profiles with real friends, and a LinkedIn profile (a professional networking site) with genuine connections. Sellers without an online presence may be asked for ID, a passport or a social security card, which makes the sale harder.

Asset Sale or Stock Sale

  • Decide before listing (putting the store up for sale) whether you will do an asset sale (specific assets transfer) or a stock sale (transfer of ownership of the entire company).
  • Asset sales are simpler. Stock sales require deeper buyer due diligence.
  • On this marketplace, more than nine out of ten sales are asset sales. Websites, files and domains are transferred, but the LLC (a company type that separates the owner's personal money from the business) stays with the seller.
  • A stock sale of an LLC requires much deeper due diligence, because the buyer must consider lawsuits arising from earlier actions.
  • The usual way to sell a store is an asset sale in which the LLC sells the Shopify store (the online shop platform), ad account access, supplier contacts and email lists. The LLC stays with the owner and can be dissolved (closed down) or reused later. This is older advice from 2022.

LLC Structure and Transfer

  • You typically do not have to sell your LLC structure. Buyers conduct more extensive due diligence when purchasing an LLC than when purchasing specific assets.
  • Large portfolio buyers (people who own many stores) usually set up a separate LLC for each store, which makes record keeping straightforward. This setup is common among portfolio buyers.

Trademark Risk in Listings

  • A business with a partially trademarked name (a brand name that is partly legally protected) can be listed for sale. Some buyers may avoid it because of perceived legal risk.
  • A partially trademarked name can be listed if the seller has not received a warning or cease and desist (a formal legal letter demanding you stop).
  • Avoid well-known brands, such as Marvel, in listings.
  • Some buyers may still be uncomfortable with the risk, even when the listing is allowed.

Brokerage or Private Sale

A broker is a middleman who finds buyers for you for a fee. A private sale means you find and handle the buyer yourself. Earn-out means part of the price is paid later, from future profit.

Factor Brokerage Private sale
Sale price Typically higher, through professional buyer matching. You keep the full sales price.
Negotiation Broker guides you through negotiations and earn-out scenarios. You handle negotiation yourself.
Buyer qualification Broker qualifies buyers to protect your time. Harder to qualify serious buyers.
Competitors Broker screens competitors using deposit systems (required up-front payments from buyers). Financial records are exposed to potential competitors.
Migration Broker handles business migration (moving the store, accounts and domain to the buyer). You are responsible for business migration.
Buyer list and protections Broker's buyer list and protections apply. No broker buyer list or protections.
Fee 15% of the sales price. No broker fee.

Broker Fees

  • Brokerage commission (the broker's fee) on dropshipping store sales under $1 million is 15% of the final sale price.
  • The broker charges a fee of 15% of the sales price, which the source describes as standard in the industry.
  • Private sales avoid this fee, but they lack the broker's buyer list and protections.

Escrow and Payment Protection

  • Escrow protects both sides by holding the buyer's money until the site has been fully transferred and checked. The seller is paid only after the new owner confirms that everything works.
  • Third-party escrow services (outside companies that hold the money) such as escrow.com move money safely but do not handle the migration. Moving the store, its accounts and its domain still needs someone to manage the details.
  • An escrow service can protect a private sale when everything goes smoothly. Escrow services are not specialised in online businesses, so arbitration (settling a dispute through an independent decision-maker instead of a court) can be difficult if a problem arises.
  • A broker that holds the buyer's funds until the transfer is complete may not be allowed to call itself an escrow company under US banking rules. Before paying, confirm how the money is held and whether the arrangement is legally valid in your jurisdiction (the country or region whose laws apply).

Due Diligence

  • Buyers must complete due diligence themselves. The sale platform vets (checks) listings before publication, but the responsibility to check suppliers, relationships and whether the business suits them stays with the buyer.
  • Prepare records that answer a buyer's checks on suppliers, relationships and the store's history. The buyer will run these checks regardless of the platform's vetting.

Addbacks in Valuation

  • Addbacks (costs you add back to profit) allow you to reintroduce expenses from your tax return into net profit (profit after costs) for valuation (working out the price) when they differ from what a buyer would actually incur.
  • Prepare to justify and negotiate these adjustments.

Seller Financing and Earn-Outs

  • Seller financing (the seller accepts payment over time instead of all at once), such as an earn-out or a share of profits paid over several months, becomes more common as deal sizes grow. It bridges the gap between the buyer's ability to pay upfront and the seller's asking price.
  • Seller financing can take the form of an earn-out or a share of profits over several months, for example $5,000 per month for six months.
  • In this structure, the broker holds the domain until the buyer makes the final payment. The broker can transfer it back or use it as leverage (a bargaining tool) if the buyer breaks the contract.

Non-Competes

  • When buying or selling a business, the buyer should require a non-compete agreement (a signed promise that the seller will not run a similar business). Without one, the seller can open the same niche (product market) again soon after the sale. This is older advice from 2020.
  • Observed non-compete terms ranged from 12 months to 5 years. This is older advice from 2020.

Cross-Border Transfers

  • Transferring accounts to a buyer in another country can take months.
  • One American buyer who had to set up an EU company (a company registered in the European Union) for an EU migration was three months into a migration that was not going smoothly. Check cross-border handovers early.

Deal Process on a Broker Platform

  1. The seller submits the store for vetting (checking), and a team reviews it.
  2. Once approved, the store is listed (put up for sale).
  3. The sales team screens buyer interest.
  4. A prep call with the seller takes place.
  5. A buyer-seller call led by the broker takes place.
  6. A post-call follow-up with the seller takes place.
  7. The broker helps negotiate.
  8. After the asset purchase agreement (the signed contract for buying the assets) is signed, the broker's team migrates the business and hands it over.
