Business structure: sole proprietor, LLC, s-Corp and entity strategy

Section 18.1 · 1 of 24 in this chapter · 21 min read

In short

  • Form an LLC (a separate company that keeps business debts away from your personal money) unless you have nothing to protect.
  • A sole proprietorship (trading as yourself, with no separate company) gives no shield. Your personal money and property can be claimed.
  • Keep business money and personal money apart. Mixing them breaks the protection.
  • Starting simple is allowed, but form the company as soon as sales begin.
  • Ask an accountant or tax professional before you choose an S corp or C corp.
More key points
  • Pick your business form first, before you sign up with any supplier (a company that sells you products) or sales platform (a website service where you sell).
  • A sole proprietorship (running the business as yourself, with no separate company) is the cheapest start, but you carry all the risk.
  • Sources advise against running a US store as a sole proprietor. An LLC (a company type that keeps your personal money separate from the business) is the usual safer choice.
  • Rules differ by state and country, so ask an accountant or lawyer before you rely on any choice.

Why a Legal Entity Matters

1 min
  • A legal entity is a company that exists separately from you, its owner.
  • An LLC (Limited Liability Company, a company separate from its owner) protects personal assets from business liabilities (debts and legal claims).
  • An LLC also looks more professional to suppliers than a sole proprietorship.
  • Form an LLC unless you genuinely have nothing to protect.
  • A sole proprietorship (trading under your own name, with no separate company) has no legal separation from its owner.
  • Personal assets, such as bank accounts and property, are exposed to business liabilities.
  • A separate legal entity limits liability to the business assets only.
  • ** A sole trader (another name for a sole proprietor) is personally liable for business debts.
  • ** In a dispute, personal assets such as a house could be claimed to settle those debts.
  • ** A proprietary limited company (a private company) or LLC is separate from its owner.
  • The owner is usually both director (the person who runs the company) and shareholder (a part owner).
  • If the company cannot pay its debts, it can be wound up (closed through a legal process) without the director generally being liable.
  • ** Limited liability (a cap on what you can lose) does not cover illegal conduct.
  • A director can still be held liable if the company or the director acts illegally.
  • Liability protection holds only if you keep business and personal funds separate.
  • Mixing business and personal funds is called commingling.
  • Commingling funds across unrelated LLCs makes separate businesses look like one entity.
  • This weakens the LLC's liability protection.
  • It also makes a later sale of one business much messier.
  • Lawsuits against the business target the business and not the owner personally.

Sole Proprietorship

1 min
  • Trading under your own name is the fastest and cheapest way to start.
  • It is a reasonable starting point.
  • It is usually replaced by a company structure once revenue (money coming in from sales) grows.
  • A sole proprietorship is not a separate legal entity.
  • All business profit is reported on the owner's personal tax return.
  • Choose the structure with this in mind before you start selling.
  • The owner's name is the business name, even if the website domain (the web address) differs.
  • A sole proprietorship has a higher tax burden (the share of profit paid in tax).
  • The owner cannot use as many write-offs (business costs that lower taxable profit).
  • The owner cannot split pay between salary and distributions (money paid out to owners from profit).
  • Some states require registration even for sole proprietors.
  • Upfront cost is lower, but there is no liability protection.
  • ** A sole proprietor operating under a business name may not need a DBA (a registered alternate business name) on day one.
  • Many stores launched without one and later formed business entities as they grew.
  • This is general experience, not legal advice.
  • ** A new store can start as a sole proprietorship with an EIN (Employer Identification Number, a US tax ID for a business) and no state sales tax certificate (a state permit to collect sales tax).
  • When sales grow, the owner can form an S corporation and obtain the state sales tax certificate at the same time.
  • ** Starting as a sole proprietor using personal accounts is a valid option while you test the business.
  • Once sales come in, a business structure can be set up with an accountant.

LLC

1 min
  • Forming an LLC typically costs about $100.
  • ** An LLC can be formed in a state other than where you live.
  • You can do this through an accountant or yourself, using an online filing service such as LegalZoom.
  • ** In the US, the LLC is the most commonly chosen structure.
  • It is quick to form and often possible entirely online.
  • It separates the company's assets from the owner's personal assets, unless the owner breaks the law.
  • ** Lean approach to entity setup in the US: form a cheap online LLC and optionally elect S-corp tax treatment.
  • A sole proprietorship can be used first and an LLC formed later.
  • Consult an accountant or tax professional for a personal situation.
  • An LLC is optional at launch, and a sole proprietorship can work initially.
  • Once the business is making money, consult an accountant about forming a separate entity.
  • Form an LLC early and elect S-corporation tax treatment.
  • This gives asset protection and tax benefits.
  • Start as a sole proprietor only if the business is brand new.
  • Form the LLC as soon as sales begin.

S Corp and C Corp Elections

1 min
  • An LLC can be elected to be taxed as an S corporation.
  • This decision affects tax obligations (what you must pay and file).
  • Confirm the S-corp election with a tax professional before filing.
  • An S corp is not a separate entity type but a tax election.
  • An existing LLC is taxed as an S corporation by filing an election form with the IRS.
  • The owner keeps the same liability protection while gaining the S-corp tax treatment.
  • An LLC taxed as an S corporation offers tax advantages.
  • It also separates personal liability from the business.
  • This structure is commonly used by US-based dropshippers (people who sell products shipped directly from suppliers).
  • Consider an S-corp election after formation to reduce self-employment taxes (the taxes owners pay on their own business earnings).
  • Under an S corp or LLC, different pay structures are possible that can reduce taxes.
  • In the US, the main difference between an LLC and an S corp is how business profits are taxed.
  • An accountant should compare both for the owner's personal situation.
  • ** An S corporation is the structure the source's author uses for protection and tax benefits.
  • The right legal entity depends on your situation, so confirm it with an accountant or attorney.
  • ** Form your business as an LLC elected to be taxed as an S corp.
  • Use a C corp only if you plan to seek outside investment.
  • ** An LLC can be elected to be taxed as an S corporation, which the source presented as a way to save on taxes.
  • This is an example structure only and not tax or legal advice.
  • ** A C corporation (a company type that can take on shareholders) is generally used when bringing in shareholders, seeking investment or going public.
  • Most small business owners use an LLC, which is the most popular structure.

  • Put each unrelated niche in its own LLC. **

  • An unrelated niche should get its own LLC so finances stay separate. **
  • Building a network of stores under one LLC works for stores selling related products.
  • Keep stores in separate LLCs when they operate in unrelated product types, so funds are not commingled. **
  • Put related stores that are cross-promoted or would be sold together under one LLC or S corp. **

Registered Agent, State and Address

Choosing a State

  • Form the LLC (a company type that keeps your personal money separate from the business) in the state where you live. You need only one LLC per state.
  • Alternative: Choose the US state for your LLC. Texas is business-friendly, meaning its rules are easy for companies. A virtual address (a rented mailing address that is not your home) through a UPS store or a PO box (a post office mailbox) can serve as the official business address, so you do not have to use your home address. **
  • Alternative: Choosing a state to form your company in is a personal decision. Texas is described as business friendly, and Delaware and Nevada are commonly used alternatives. **
  • Wyoming's tax benefits are mainly relevant to non-US residents forming a US company. A US resident should generally form the company in their own state, since Wyoming or Delaware give no clear advantage there. **
  • An LLC can be formed in any state, but forming it in another state usually does not remove the obligation to pay tax in the state where you live. Check tax rules with a professional before relying on a different state. **
  • An LLC formed in Wyoming while physically operating in Florida: assume a nexus (a tie to a state that makes you owe its tax) in the state of operation (the state where you actually run the business), and confirm with a tax professional. **

Registered Agents and Legal Address

  • A registered agent (the person or service that receives legal paperwork for a business) is needed when you set up a business in a state where you do not live. A legal service can provide one. (2023.)
  • A registered agent must be located in the state where the LLC is registered. An LLC outside your state needs a local registered agent who forwards mail. (2024.)

Structure by Country

2 min

United States

  • The usual US business forms are a sole proprietorship (trading as yourself), an LLC and an S corp. The last two are legal entities (companies the law treats as separate from their owner).
  • Conflict (sources disagree): A sole proprietorship can be used at the start, and an LLC can be formed later.
  • Conflict: A sole proprietorship gives no separation between the business and the owner. The owner is personally liable (legally responsible with their own money), and personal assets such as a home can be seized (taken by a court) to pay business debts. Do not run a US store as a sole proprietor.
  • Sole proprietorship, LLC and S corp differ mainly in liability (legal responsibility for debts and claims) and tax treatment (how tax is calculated and paid). Use the LLC and S-corp options above for the relevant decision.

Australia

  • ** In Australia, the two structures are the sole trader and the Proprietary Limited company (Pty Ltd, a private company type).
  • ** A sole trader can be set up in minutes. A Pty Ltd costs about $800 to $2,000 to establish and requires an accountant.
  • ** In Australia, a sole trader is personally liable for business claims. A company director's (the person who runs the company) personal assets are protected if the business operates legally. Liability protection and tax treatment are the two main differences.
  • Older advice (2021-22). Recommended path for most Australian beginners:
    1. Start as a sole trader to keep setup cheap and fast.
    2. Once the business earns meaningful revenue and liability exposure (how much legal risk you carry) grows, switch to a Pty Ltd company with an accountant's help.
    3. If you already own a Pty Ltd, use it from the start.

Sole Trader and Company Basics

  • ** A sole trader has no legal separation from the owner.
  • ** The choice between sole trader and company has no universal right answer. It depends on liability exposure, tax situation, cash available and cost. Advice from an accountant is recommended when tax is complex.
  • ** Starting as a sole trader is a valid low-cost choice, and it is easy to convert (change into) a company later. Many owners do this when cash is limited and convert once the company's cost is financially justified (worth what it costs).

Other Countries

  • Most other countries use different names but the same two-tier choice (two options) between trading as an individual and forming a company.
  • ** Outside the US and Australia, a local accountant or lawyer should confirm the structure choice.
