Researching supplier contact details

Section 3.6 · 6 of 22 in this chapter · 20 min read

In short

  • Only collect contact details now. Do not apply or submit forms yet.
  • Use the brand's own website, not a marketplace or reseller site.
  • Record a direct email. If none exists, use the link to the contact form.
  • Write N-A only after every search variant has failed.
  • Keep each niche's supplier list separate.
More key points
  • Check each brand's own website and the exact product before you write anything down.
  • Never guess general contact addresses like dealers@ or vendors@.
  • After one failed search, try another search wording before you mark a supplier as not available.
  • Give supplier research a long time block: about 20 to 25 minutes for 40 suppliers.

Column letters (A, B, C ...) refer to the research worksheet from section 2.3.

This section has 15 parts. Tap one to open it.

Where contact research fits in the workflow

1 min
  • Contact research comes after market research (studying what products sell and who sells them).
  • Once the supplier list is built, the next step is to find each supplier's contact details before reaching out for approval. A supplier is a company you buy products from.
  • The supplier list holds brands found while studying future competitors (online stores you plan to compete with). Collecting contact details in advance saves time at the approval stage.
  • Contact research only collects contact data. Do not submit applications, sell-with-us forms or sales inquiries during this phase.
  • Approval requests come later.
  • At this stage, fill only the URL (web address), phone and email columns.
  • Contact date, contact person and notes are completed in a later step.

Building the first list

1 min
  • Give each niche (a narrow product category, such as kitchen tools) its own master supplier list, named after that niche. This keeps suppliers from different niches apart.
  • Fill columns A to D first.
  • Columns E to G stay empty at this stage and are completed later.
  • Stage-one layout (the first set of columns to fill):
Column Content
A Supplier / brand name
B Supplier website URL
C Supplier phone number
D Supplier email address, or contact form link if no email exists
Notes Add-on or accessory products useful for cross-selling (usually blank for core-product suppliers)
  • The Notes column (a free-text box for extra remarks) is optional.
  • Use Notes for add-on products (extra items sold with the main product).
  • Use Notes for upsell (offers to buy a higher-priced version) and cross-sell (offers to buy a related item).
  • Use Notes for links to dealer or reseller application pages (forms for shops that resell products).
  • Record cross-sell items in Notes. For example, a supplier selling knife sets for add-on or upsell offers.
  • Most Notes stay blank for core-product suppliers. Core-product suppliers are the ones you would order the main products from.
  • Add-on sellers are noted, but they are not treated as core suppliers.
  • Brands that do not sell the core product can still be recorded in Notes. They are not treated as core suppliers.
  • A brand with no working contact information does not count toward the 20-brand minimum.

Per-supplier research routine

2 min

Source A procedure, per supplier, in this order

  1. Open the master supplier list for your chosen niche. Place it side by side with a browser window (split screen or a second tab).
  2. Search Google for the exact supplier or brand name, starting at the top of your list.
  3. Open the brand's own website (not a reseller or article). Copy its URL into column B (Supplier URL).
  4. Open the site's Contact page (usually via the header menu, the bar at the top of the page, or the footer, the bottom strip, if missing there). Copy the phone number and paste it into column C (Supplier Phone).
  5. Copy a direct email into column D (Supplier Email). If only a contact form exists, copy the form's link instead.
  6. If no site or contact channel appears (a contact channel is any way to reach them, such as a phone number, email or form), run the alternative searches: brand + niche + website; brand + niche; brand + customer service; brand + contact number; brand + Zoominfo. The "+" means add the words to the same search.
  7. If still nothing is found, enter N-A (not available) in the missing cells.

Source B procedure

  1. Search the brand name on Google. Open the official website from the organic results (normal results, not ads). Do not rely on a guessed domain (a website address, such as brandname.com). Check the exact spelling, since similar names can belong to different companies.
  2. Confirm that the site sells your product type and that the company is the manufacturer or brand owner (the company that makes the product or owns the brand), not a retailer (a shop that sells to the public).
  3. Open the Contact Us page from the top navigation first. It is the most likely place for contact details.
  4. Check the footer (the bottom strip of the page) for a phone number and any support or customer service page.
  5. Copy the website URL, the email address (if one is published) and the phone number into the spreadsheet.
  6. If there is no public email address, type 'Website contact form' in the Email column. Still record the phone number.
  7. Look in the header and footer for links such as 'Become a Dealer', 'Become a Retailer' or 'Become a [brand] dealer'. A dealer is a shop that resells the brand's products. If one exists, note its page URL in the Online Application column. Do not submit anything yet.
  8. If a dealer page gives specific contact instructions (phone, form, mailing address), record that channel as the way to reach them later.
  9. Move on to the next brand.
  • The routine above is copy-and-record. Do it in one pass per brand: search, verify, record, then move on.

Finding the official website

2 min
  • For an exact brand name, the first organic Google result (a normal result, not an ad) is often the official website.
  • Always verify that it is the brand's own domain (the main address of the website, such as brand.com) before copying it.
  • A Google search for a brand name returns its official site. It often shows the real domain, which is more reliable than guessing the URL.
  • Skip ads at the top of results.
  • Choose the brand's own website over marketplaces (sites where many sellers list products, such as Walmart, Amazon, Houzz, Target or Wayfair).
  • Marketplaces show who sells the products, not how to contact the supplier.
  • A marketplace or retailer listing is not the brand's website.
  • A brand that only appears on marketplaces may still be reachable through other search variants (different word combinations).
  • Do not guess supplier website addresses from the brand name.
  • Search the brand on Google first. Some brands use unexpected domains, or share a name with unrelated companies.
  • Do not record a retailer's page instead of the brand's own site.
  • Confirm the company is the manufacturer or brand owner, not a retailer.
  • If a brand search returns many unrelated websites, add the niche keyword (for example 'kitchen') to the brand name. Check the products section of the result. This narrows the results to the brand's own product website.
  • For a generic or shared brand name, add a niche word such as lighting, health or furniture.
  • Adding a product word such as chandelier can also help separate the right result.
  • Do not dig deep into Google results pages. After the first few results they are mostly unrelated.
  • Try a different search variant instead.
  • Copy the official website URL into the Supplier Website column.
  • Remove tracking parameters (extra code at the end of a web address that tracks where a click came from) and unnecessary URL parts.
  • If a supplier's website does not load, leave its row blank for now and move on.
  • Come back to it later rather than spending time on a broken site.

Finding the phone number

1 min
  • Phone numbers are usually under a Contact link in the header menu.
  • If the header has none, check the footer menu.
  • If a brand's header and footer have no Contact link, check the sidebar menu (a menu along the side of the page) for a Contact entry.
  • Only conclude that no contact page exists after checking the sidebar.
  • Phone numbers can appear in odd formats or as international numbers (numbers that start with another country's code).
  • If the number is international, look for the contact page number instead.
  • Check that the number has enough digits. Then reformat it to US style with hyphens (3-3-4 digits, e.g. 555-010-0199).
  • If several phone numbers are listed (for example toll-free (free to call) and local), they usually ring the same place.
  • Any one of them can be used.
  • Record the supplier's main contact phone number in the Supplier Phone Number column.
  • For a toll-free number, call it and ask for the person in charge of eCommerce accounts.
  • Request their direct email address for future communication if you prefer to follow up that way.
  • A supplier can have an email but no phone number. In that case, enter N-A in the phone column.
  • Keep the email.
  • Blank or N-A for a missing phone number: the Source A column rule says leave the cell blank if no number is listed.
  • The step-by-step routine says to enter N-A once all search variants have failed.
  • Use blank when the supplier lists no number at all and you are filling the column. Use N-A only after the alternative searches have also failed.

Finding the email address or contact form

3 min
  • Take the supplier's email address from its contact page.
  • When both a direct email and a contact form are offered, prefer the direct email.
  • When a contact page lists several emails for different distribution centres (warehouses that ship orders), such as USA and Canada, use the one address they all share.
  • The same applies when several offices share one address.
  • Otherwise, choose the entry that matches the market you sell in.
  • When a brand lists both a sales inquiries email and a parts inquiries email, use the sales inquiries address.
  • When a contact page lists several locations or departments, choose the e-commerce or online sales entry. It is the contact point for online sellers.
  • Enter the published email address in the Supplier Email column.
  • If none is published, enter the URL of the contact form instead.
  • If a supplier has only a contact form and no direct email, paste the URL of the contact form into the email column.
  • Some suppliers have only a contact form for drop-shipping requests (orders where the supplier ships straight to your customer).
  • Enter that form in the email column, so you can still reach them later.
  • Record every supplier with a form this way.
  • Brand inbox addresses often follow the pattern info@[brand].com.
  • Treat this only as a hint. Confirm the address on the brand's website before recording it.
  • Do not guess generic dealer or vendor addresses such as dealers@ or vendors@ and assume they exist. A vendor is a company that supplies products to a seller.
  • A test with this kind of address did not work for the brand checked.
  • Look for named dealer or vendor contacts on the contact page instead.
  • Many brands sell only through resellers and publish no public email address. They do not want consumers emailing them or receiving spam (unwanted mass emails).
  • A website contact form or a phone number is then a valid route (a way to reach them). Record it as the contact method.
  • Brands often do not sell directly to the public. They prefer to supply resellers.
  • Missing public email is normal. It is not a reason to drop a supplier.
  • A phone number or contact form is enough to start.
  • Brand contact forms often ask for: name, relation to the company, street address, city, state, ZIP code (US postal code), email, whether the company may contact you, preferred contact method, phone number, topic, subject and message.
  • Forms usually have a CAPTCHA check (a test to prove you are a person, such as picking pictures).
  • Fill these in only when you actually contact the supplier, not during research.
  • The way forms are labelled differs by source.
  • Source B's sheet types 'Website contact form' in the Email column when there is no public email.
  • Source A's sheet uses the form's URL.
  • Use the URL when you need to reach the supplier through that form later.
  • Use the label when the form is the only route and the URL is not needed.

Examples:

  • Airfree: the brand was found on Google. Its official site showed a phone number in the footer, and no public email existed.
  • The row got the phone number, the URL, and 'Website Contact Form' in the Email column. The brand's contact form was its only public email route.
  • Aerus: the official site had a contact form and a phone number at the bottom.
  • The row was completed with both, and the form was noted as the contact route.
  • Coway/Airmega: the contact page listed a support email, a toll-free number and opening hours. It also had a separate business and retail contact section.
  • The business and retail contact was recorded.
  • Some manufacturers publish a separate Business and Retail contact path next to their consumer support.
  • Use that business channel for supplier and wholesale inquiries (requests to buy in bulk).
  • Record the business contact, not the consumer support line.

Contact labels and routes

1 min
  • Contact pages may use labels such as Contact Us, Customer Support, Customer Service, Dealer Contact or Get in Touch.
  • Use the label that matches your goal. For example, use dealer or vendor pages when you want to become a dealer or vendor.
  • Brand websites often split contact routes by purpose, such as General Inquiry, Government and Volume orders, and Become a Dealer.
  • Choose the route that matches your request.
  • A dealer or wholesale request should go through the dealer page, not a general contact form.

Dealer portals and application pages

2 min
  • A footer link to a dealer portal (a members' website for dealers) with a 'request access' option means the brand has a dealer program.
  • Speak to the supplier first, by phone or email, before filling in the request form.
  • Record the phone number and email you find for it in the usual columns.
  • When a brand publishes a dealer or retailer application page, the brand is signalling the first step it wants resellers to take.
  • Follow the process it describes when contacting it later.
  • If a brand offers an online application, use it as the first contact step. Putting it on the site signals that this is the route they want.
  • If no application exists, that is normal and not a problem.
  • Dealer pages often name a specific route for applicants. Examples: a phone number for quick help, a form that a sales representative (a person who sells for the company) answers, or a postal address marked for dealer inquiries.
  • Follow the route the page specifies rather than a generic contact method.
  • Example (Austin Air, Source B 2021-22, older): the site had a 'Become an Austin Air Dealer' page.
  • The page had a phone line for immediate assistance and an online dealer application form.
  • The application page URL was recorded in the Online Application column.
  • The phone number was noted for contact.
  • A wholesale-only online store that hides prices behind a dealer login (a sign-in only approved dealers get) is still a valid supplier.
  • Use its vendor or dealer contact page. Prices only become visible after approval.

Conflict on first contact:

  • Source B says to use the online application first if one exists.
  • Source A says to speak to the supplier by phone or email before filling in a request form.
  • Both fit the research rule not to submit anything during research.
  • Use the application first where the page clearly names it as the route.
  • Use a phone or email call first where the supplier has a dealer-portal request form.

Verifying the address and the seller

1 min
  • Verify a listed address before you rule a seller out or in (decide whether to keep them):
    1. Copy the address from the footer or contact page.
    2. Search it on Google.
    3. Open Street View (street-level photos in Google Maps) to see whether the building is a store, a warehouse, an office or a training site.
    • An office or a learning centre does not count as a physical store.
    • It also does not prove the seller is online only.
  • When a brand name is searched with the word ZoomInfo (a business contact database), the results can show a company profile for a different business with a similar name.
  • Check that the profile's industry and location match the brand before copying any phone number or email from it.
  • Unrelated businesses can share a brand name, such as a bicycle shop, a home care company or a Facebook page.
  • Do not accept the first search result for an ambiguous name (a name that could mean more than one company) without checking it.

When the first search fails

2 min

Alternative search sequence, Source A (when the brand's own site does not appear)

(A "+" means type those words together in one search.)

  1. Brand name + niche + 'website' (for example Brand Name Furniture Website).
  2. Brand name + niche only (for example Brand Name Lighting).
  3. Brand name + customer service.
  4. Brand name + contact number.
  5. Brand name + Zoominfo (written as one word).

Alternative search sequence, Source A (order of combinations)

  1. [Brand Name] + Niche
  2. [Brand Name] + Website
  3. [Brand Name] + Customer Service
  4. [Brand Name] + Phone Number
  5. [Brand Name] + zoominfo
  • Both sequences cover the same search terms.
  • Run them in the order given in the list you are working from.
  • The sequence used in the per-supplier routine is.
  • Do not mark a supplier N-A after a single failed search.
  • Enter N-A only after the alternative search variants have also failed.

Reverse image search:

  1. Search the exact supplier company name on Google. Put the full name in quotation marks so only exact matches appear.
  2. If that fails, open a competitor's product page in Google Chrome.
  3. Right-click the product image and choose the option to search Google for the image.
  4. Review the pages that contain the same image. They may show the supplier's website or identifying information.
  5. Use that information to find the supplier.
  • A brand without its own website may be a sister brand (a brand owned by the same parent company, the company above it) of another supplier.
  • If the parent supplier is approved later, it may allow you to sell the sister brand too.
  • Keep the entry and recheck it later.

Reaching a person

1 min

Outreach sequence, Source A (2024)

(Outreach means reaching out to suppliers to make contact.)

  1. Call suppliers by phone whenever a number is available. It is the fastest way to reach the right person and get a yes or no.
  2. If no phone number is listed, find contacts through LinkedIn (a professional networking site) by searching the company name.
  3. If nothing works, message the company's Facebook page or Instagram account, if it is active.
  4. Start with the less well-known suppliers that rank lower on future competitor stores. They are easier to get approved with. They also help build phone confidence before calling the key brands.
  • Avoid sending physical mail to suppliers to get contacts. Mail is unlikely to reach the right person.
  • Email is acceptable as a first contact method only when a supplier has not listed a phone number on their website.
  • When a supplier asks you to email them, ask for the name of the right person, their direct email and a phone number.
  • Writing that person down lets you follow up with a specific contact. This is better than a general inbox (a shared company email address).
  • If the first contact has outdated information:
    1. Ask the supplier to confirm or provide a different contact person.
    2. If calls keep reaching the same person, look up the contact on LinkedIn and send a message.
    3. If the contact has a Facebook profile, send a Facebook message.
    4. Try email as well, and keep following up with the new contact.

Tracker and supplier list layouts

4 min

Working rules

  • Keep one tracking sheet for every supplier you contact.
  • Record each outreach date, response, approval status and notes in it.
  • This makes it possible to work down the supplier list in order without losing track of follow-ups (reminders to contact again).
  • Store every supplier contact in one sheet with the contact date, the person spoken to and notes.
  • This keeps all outreach traceable.
  • Track suppliers in a spreadsheet: Google Sheets (Google's free online spreadsheet) with a supplier tracking template. Keep an Excel version as backup.
  • Always make your own copy of the template before entering data, so the original stays unedited.
  • Use Google Sheets with a copied template. Never edit the original template. Or use the Excel equivalent.
  • The later columns for call tracking (Phone Call + Date?, Call Comments, Paused) are filled only when suppliers are contacted.
  • During research, only the first six columns are filled in: Supplier Name, Product Type, Email, Phone Number, Website and Online Application?.
  • The remaining columns are used later, when you start contacting suppliers.

Supplier type and product type

1 min
  • Record the primary product type (the main kind of product) of each supplier.
  • This lets you speak to the product the supplier actually makes.
  • A brand can make more than one product type, so one supplier row may list several.

Common mistakes

1 min
  • Guessing a domain (a web address), recording a retailer's page (a shop that sells to customers) instead of the brand's own site, or confusing similar brand names, for example Aeris and Aerus. Check each brand's official site and product match before writing anything into the spreadsheet (a table file).
  • Guessing generic dealer or vendor addresses such as dealers@ or vendors@. A dealer or vendor is a company that sells goods. A generic address is not tied to one person.
  • Accepting the first result for an ambiguous name (a name that could mean more than one company).
  • Marking N-A after one failed search. N-A is a short mark for not available.
  • Digging deep into Google results pages (the lists of links Google shows for a search) instead of trying a different search variant (another wording of the same search).
  • Copying phone numbers or emails from a ZoomInfo profile (an entry in a business contact database) without checking industry and location.

Planning the time

1 min
  • Finding phone, email and form details for over 40 suppliers (companies that provide products to you) took about 20 to 25 minutes in the worked example.
  • So plan a comparable time block for a niche's supplier research. A niche is a narrow product market.
  • The brand extraction step (pulling the brand names out of a list of competitors, meaning rival sellers of similar products) is faster, at about 1 to 2 minutes per competitor.
