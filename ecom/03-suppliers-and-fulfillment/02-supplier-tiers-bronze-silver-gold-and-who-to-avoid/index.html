Supplier tiers: bronze, silver, gold and who to avoid

Section 3.2 · 2 of 22 in this chapter · 21 min read

In short

  • Supplier tiers (levels) rank suppliers as bronze, silver or gold. There is no official standard for them.
  • Never pay a supplier to get access to its products.
  • Avoid bronze suppliers. They charge fees, screen no one, and cause price fights.
  • Start with silver suppliers. They are the default, and most of your suppliers should be silver.
  • Gold suppliers come later, after your store has visitors and sales.
More key points
  • Never sell with bronze suppliers, the lowest rank, and do not run ads for them.
  • Judge a supplier by its cost of goods and its price rules, not by its rank alone.
  • Start with silver suppliers, and aim for about 95% silver over the long term.
  • Keep gold suppliers near 5 to 10%. Apply to gold only after your store has sales and visitors.
  • Check older advice from 2018 to 2022 before you use it.

Supplier Tiers at a Glance

1 min

Supplier tiers are levels or ranks. They are an informal ranking, not an official industry standard. So searching for "gold, silver, bronze supplier" will not return a definition.

Each tier leads to different results. Check the tier before you decide which suppliers to pursue.

Supplier type decides four things. It decides your profit, how much price competition you face, how much control you have over prices, and whether your store can grow.

Key words used below:

  • MAP means Minimum Advertised Price. It is the lowest price a brand lets sellers show publicly.
  • Dropshipping means the supplier ships the order straight to your customer. You never hold the stock yourself.

Bronze Suppliers: Why to Avoid Them

2 min

What bronze means

  • Bronze suppliers accept anyone who applies. They usually charge a fee to see their catalog (the list of products they sell).
  • They accept any applicant without checking them. The only requirement is payment. Treat this as a major red flag.
  • Bronze suppliers often ask for approval fees of $50, $100, $1,000 or more before they let you sell.
  • An approval fee is money you pay to be allowed to sell.
  • A supplier that asks you to pay before you can sell is bronze. Skip it.
  • Bronze suppliers are the ones most likely to cause MAP violations. A MAP violation means a seller shows a price below the brand's minimum.
  • Bronze suppliers charge a fee to become an authorized retailer (an approved seller). Suppliers whose relationship benefits both sides through product sales are preferred.
  • Bronze suppliers often make money from fees or from poor sales terms.

Why they hurt profit

  • The same products are listed by countless other sellers. You cannot stay profitable under that price pressure.
  • Without MAP enforcement (the brand acting against sellers who go below MAP), retailers undercut each other. Margins, the money you keep after costs, disappear. This happens even when orders come in.
  • Example: a chandelier (a hanging light) has a MAP of $1,000. Competitors sell it for $600 to $800, and the supplier does nothing.
  • Competitors selling at thin margins (for example around 5%) undercut you. Listings (product pages in your store) and paid traffic (visitors you pay for through ads) sent to those products are largely wasted.
  • Products from bronze suppliers show inconsistent quality. They have high rates of shipping damage and defects (faults).
  • Service is poor. Replies are slow, shipping is delayed, and tracking numbers are missing. You may have to follow up a week after you submit an order to learn whether it shipped.
  • Bronze suppliers offer no real advantages. Their drawbacks are unenforced MAP, inconsistent quality and poor support.
  • A store built on bronze suppliers may make early sales. But the business tends to fall apart quickly and become a constant problem.
  • Their products often bring more headaches than profit. Do not run ads for them or sell them.

Rule for building your network

  • Bronze suppliers must be avoided entirely when you research suppliers and build your network.
  • Never sell for bronze suppliers, even when their catalog looks attractive.
  • Avoid bronze suppliers that require you to buy their product or pre-purchase inventory (buy stock upfront) before you can sell.

Exception for existing listings (conflicts with the rules above, see "Conflicting Guidance")

This applies to a bronze supplier with no MAP policy and an MSRP below 2X wholesale.

  • MSRP means manufacturer's suggested retail price, the full price the brand suggests.
  • Wholesale means the price you pay the supplier.
  • 2X means twice.
  1. Check whether its products still sell at a healthy margin without ad costs.
  2. If they do, keep them on the site and optionally in the product feed (the file that sends your products to shopping ads) for free listings.
  3. Set their Google Ads cost per click (what you pay each time someone clicks your ad) to one cent, so no ad money goes to them.
  4. Remove them if nothing sells.

Silver Suppliers: The Default Tier

3 min

Identifying a silver supplier

  • Silver suppliers review and approve each retailer one by one, based on credibility (how trustworthy you look).
  • Identify silver suppliers by three traits. They charge no fees to sell their products. They require approval before you can join (onboarding). They enforce MAP pricing.
  • A silver supplier does not charge for access, and it reviews its applicants. If a supplier meets both criteria, classify it as silver.
  • A supplier you found through niche product research (finding a small, specific product market) is generally silver. This applies if its website has no page offering paid access to sell its products. Confirm the other silver traits, such as MAP enforcement and retailer review, before you apply.
  • Silver suppliers do not show a "where to buy" section. This is a page listing the shops that sell the brand.

What silver suppliers offer

  • They enforce MAP quickly once a retailer reports a competitor selling below it. They contact the wrong-doing retailer to correct the price or remove it as an authorized seller.
  • Example: a MAP of $1,000, and a competitor lists it at $900. The supplier takes immediate action.
  • They provide good to great customer service. They ship fast, reply quickly to questions and care for their retailers.
  • They usually assign a dedicated sales representative (one named contact person). You can reach them by email or phone for orders, tracking, new variations (versions of a product, like colors or sizes) and questions.
  • They ship quickly and reply promptly. They earn from retailer sales, not from fees, so their interests match yours.
  • They protect your margins through MAP enforcement. They also provide quality control and fast shipping. They are plentiful, with thousands available.
  • Their drawback is competition. Any legitimate store can sell their products. So your traffic and paid ads must be well optimized (set up to work well).
  • Silver suppliers tend to offer good to great customer service and are preferred partners.

What silver suppliers require

  • Silver suppliers review every retailer that applies. They approve only selected sellers.
  • Expect a refusal if you apply without a finished demo store (a sample shop, ready to show) and a complete business setup. Prepare both before contacting them.
  • They review every applicant, including your live store. So the store must be built before you apply.
  • Trust is the foundation of the relationship. Silver suppliers use your business setup and presentation as signs of legitimacy (being real) and professionalism before they grant access.
  • Approval means the supplier decides it trusts you with its brand. Silver and gold suppliers expect retailers to earn that trust before approval.

Silver suppliers in practice

  • Silver suppliers approve most sellers. They make up nearly all of your suppliers until you have a sales track record (proof of past sales).
  • A store can be profitable with only silver-tier suppliers. Gold-tier suppliers add strength but are not required.
  • Silver suppliers have similar margins to gold but are less selective.
  • Silver suppliers are the core of the business. They make up most of the brands sold over time.
  • Silver suppliers are worth prioritizing because they approve new retailers without needing a reputation.
  • Start outreach (first contact) with silver suppliers to build momentum.
  • Select Silver as the supplier tier in your contact plan (your list of who to contact). Over 95 percent of the brands you will sell for are silver tier.

Gold Suppliers: The Top Tier and a Later Goal

3 min

Identifying a gold supplier

  • Gold suppliers are extremely selective. They enforce MAP. They do not sell directly to the public. They actively refer customers to authorized retailers through a "Where to Buy" page.
  • Identify a gold supplier by a "where to buy" section on its website. It links to other online stores in your niche, typically 5 to 10.
  • Seeing that the supplier already refers buyers to other stores shows it is gold, without prior approval.
  • A "where to buy" section with retailer logos (brand images) is a good sign of gold. Other suppliers usually need to be contacted to confirm their level.
  • Gold suppliers usually list their approved retailers on their site.

Scale and selectivity

  • Gold suppliers authorize only a small number of retailers, often around 5 to 10 companies in total.
  • Gold suppliers are the highest-quality tier. They make up a small minority, roughly 5 to 10 percent of a retailer's suppliers.
  • They build close relationships and work with only a very small number of selected retailers.

Requirements and approval timing

  • Gold suppliers require proof of an established business history, existing traffic (visitors to your store) and a sales track record before approval.
  • Gold is a long-term goal, after your business has proven sales success. It is not a starting point.
  • Gold suppliers often reject new stores until they show traffic, sales or time in business.
  • Gold suppliers do not approve new retailers on day one, because their screening is stricter. Approval typically comes after your store has traffic, sales and a track record with silver suppliers. This often takes three to twelve months.
  • Gold suppliers that send traffic by linking to your store can take 3 months, 6 months, or more than a year to authorize you.
  • Some brands approve a new retailer right away. Gold-level suppliers can take a year or two, because they want to see a long sales history.
  • Gold approvals are hard at first. They get easier as relationships develop and trust grows.
  • Gold suppliers are usually the hardest to get approved with. Still contact every supplier in your niche, because outreach is quick. Circle back on rejections (try again later).
  • Gold suppliers are normally slow to approve.

Service and referrals

  • Gold suppliers enforce MAP. They act right away against sellers who list below it.
  • They give excellent customer service. They have a point of contact who reaches out first, without being asked, to help you increase sales.
  • They do not sell to the public themselves. So they refer customers to their authorized retailers.
  • Approved gold-tier retailers receive referrals. When a customer wants to buy directly from the supplier, the supplier passes the lead (the customer's contact details) to an authorized retailer.
  • The referral flow (how customers get sent to you) is the main reason gold suppliers are so limited in number.
  • Gold suppliers are most likely to link back to your store. Not all suppliers will do this. Tier placement decides this benefit.
  • Expect referrals only after you have been approved and have proven traffic and sales.
  • Gold suppliers combine MAP protection, customer support and quality control with referrals. They limit competition because they work with only a handful of online retailers. Their main drawback is that approval is hard without a sales history.

Using gold for credibility

  • Once suppliers begin approving you, use the approved brands to build credibility (trust) before you approach your top targets.
  • Over time, get approved with as many brands in your niche as possible, as long as they are silver or gold. A low-selling brand with strong margins can fund monthly promotions.
  • Once you have revenue (money coming in) and traffic, circle back to gold suppliers. Their approval odds are much higher then.
  • Reapply to gold once the store is proven, after building with silver suppliers first.

Identifying Tiers Before You Apply

1 min

Check in this order:

  1. Check whether the supplier charges for access to its products. If yes, classify it as bronze and drop it.
  2. Check whether it enforces MAP and reviews applicants. If yes, it is a silver supplier.
  3. Look for a "where to buy" section linking to 5 to 10 online stores. If yes, it is a gold supplier. Plan to approach it after you build traffic and sales.

Supporting checks:

  • A supplier without a MAP policy ranks lower than suppliers that meet all criteria, including MAP. It can still be workable if margins hold after shipping.
  • A supplier that enforces MAP belongs to the higher-quality group. Prioritize suppliers that enforce MAP when you choose whom to approach first.
  • Rank slow-responding, frequently out-of-stock or slow-shipping suppliers below reliable ones. These can signal money trouble (financial instability). They may not survive a cash-flow shock (a sudden money problem).
  • A supplier with an outdated website, low-resolution images or thin product information can still be worth carrying. Your job is to give customers a reason to buy. Clearer product content and professional presentation on your own store can make that difference.

Sequencing:

  • Start by contacting silver-tier suppliers. Work up to gold-tier brands once you have experience.
  • Submit only your top two or three niche ideas for verification (checking).
  • Do not wait for the best supplier before you begin. Open an account with any supplier who will work with you. Build demand. Premium suppliers may approach you later.
  • Premium or top-tier suppliers are not needed at the start.

Red Flags: Paid Access and Fees

1 min
  • A legitimate supplier never charges a retailer for access to its products, including approval fees. If a supplier asks for money to get approved or to sell, decline and move to the next supplier on your list.
  • Do not pay an opening fee, or buy a minimum starter quantity, just to open a supplier account. Answer a minimum order request by explaining when you will reach that volume.
  • Never pay for access to any supplier. Use the course login on the dropship platform (a software tool that lists dropship suppliers) for supplier access. It is included in the membership.
  • Distributors (companies that buy from brands and resell) that require a paid membership fee to access products should be avoided. Distributors without a fee can be worth working with if they enforce pricing controls.
  • Do not accept offers that charge per order. Example: $25 per order, plus a $100 fee per invoice on small invoices, is a warning sign. Ask whether there are sales tiers without fees. If there are none, do not sell the products.
  • Do not pay a one-time pre-purchase stock fee, such as $10,000. Propose a one-month test listing with ads instead. If the supplier refuses, launch with other brands and return later with a stronger store.
  • Some suppliers outside China charge yearly license fees, reported at up to $300 to $500.
  • Big Buy requires a paid membership, reported at about €300 per year.

Red Flags: Paid Directories and Middlemen

2 min

Paid directories

  • Paid supplier directories (lists of suppliers you pay to see) charge a monthly fee, for example $150 per month, for access to products. They are middlemen (go-betweens) and should not be used. Their products are not worth the cost.
  • Do not pay monthly or yearly fees to paid supplier directories, even when they claim access to over a million products. They typically offer no direct relationship with the brands.
  • Paid directories sit several layers away from the brand. Products pass through distributors, overstock sellers (who sell extra stock) and resellers. So pricing control, MAP compliance and current product data are lost. Products are often outdated and already marked up at least twice. Every paying retailer competes with the same products.
  • Avoid supplier directories. They mostly connect you to middlemen, and the margins through them are usually too thin.
  • Avoid supplier directories that sell access to brand catalogs for a percentage. They skip the direct relationship with the brand, and that relationship is what makes the business work long term.
  • Supplier directories and paid supplier lists were judged not worth the money. Finding suppliers through your own direct research gives much better brand quality.
  • Supplier directories and paid apps that promise instant access to thousands of products are bronze-level sources.

Middlemen

  • Do not pay middlemen for supplier lists or catalog access. A paid monthly CSV (a spreadsheet file) of supplier products is a red flag. It can harm your account standing (your good record with platforms).
  • Bronze-tier suppliers that route you through a marketplace or middleman, such as Alibaba (a large online wholesale marketplace), are generally not worth working with. Look for suppliers elsewhere.
  • Overseas contacts may be middlemen rather than factories, so verify before you invest. When an overseas contact offers an affiliate-style commission (a percentage for sending buyers to them), ask for their U.S. contact or U.S. warehouse. Also ask for the reseller program.
  • Source A suppliers charge no fee for approval or access. They are the manufacturers or brand owners themselves, not middlemen or distributors.
  • Using a distributor is only acceptable without a fee and with pricing controls.

Red Flags: Preloaded Stores and Store-Building Offers

1 min
  • Some bronze suppliers offer to build a preloaded store (a ready-made shop filled with their products) for $2,000, $5,000 or $10,000. Such an offer is another red flag for a bronze supplier.

Red Flags: Affiliate-Only Deals and Low Margins

2 min

Affiliate-only offers

  • Affiliate-only offers come from a dealer that refuses drop shipping. An example is an 8% commission (a cut of each sale). These are not worth it. Promoting as an affiliate only makes sense for a related niche you would never sell yourself.
  • Do not accept offers that pay a commission of about 8% to 12% on sales. That is an affiliate arrangement, not an e-commerce (online store) or dropshipping deal. It does not leave enough margin.
  • Suppliers that sell to physical stores often suggest becoming their affiliate instead. Affiliate promotion only fits when customers may want the product but it does not make sense for your store to sell it directly. Example: promoting a drum school to buyers of beginner drum sets.
  • Affiliate offers are not suitable for your own core products. Your store cannot show add-to-cart buttons (the button that puts an item in the shopping basket) or run the usual ads for products you do not sell. Affiliate links fit indirectly related products, such as courses or services for the same audience. The margins are very small.
  • Affiliate-level margins of 10 to 15% leave too little room for ad spend (money spent on ads). Such products should be listed only if the sale costs nothing. They should not receive paid traffic.
  • Suppliers that offer only an affiliate program should be deprioritized (ranked lower). Follow up with other suppliers. Return to affiliate-only suppliers once your store has traffic and sales.

Low margins and no MAP

  • A supplier that requires a minimum annual sales volume (for example $10,000 per year) or has tiny margins (for example 12%) is a red flag. Starting with a supplier that has a credible brand can help you get approved by more suppliers later.
  • When a supplier has no MAP policy and offers only a thin percentage margin (for example 20 to 35 percent with volume tiers, which are discounts for buying more), treat it as bronze and avoid it. Advertising costs, merchant fees (card and payment processing fees) and shipping leave no margin.
  • Margins of 15 to 20 percent place a supplier in the bronze category. Bronze suppliers should not be used.

Red Flags: Trial Terms and Unusual Setups

1 min
  • Do not accept terms that require you to sell products at no profit as a trial period before you negotiate a profitable margin. This signals a low-tier supplier and is not worthwhile. Avoid suppliers that require this as a mandatory trial phase before they allow margin negotiation.
  • Negotiate a test where products go live on your store first. The best sellers show up before any inventory is bought.
  • Be cautious with a wholesale company that asks retailers to send customers to its own website without a cart (shopping basket), and to take orders by email or phone. This setup is unusual and a poor fit for a dropshipping store. Question its terms before you accept.
  • Do not sell Black Friday orders (the big shopping day after Thanksgiving) through supplier stores based in China. The risk of bad customer experiences is not worth the sales.
  • Prefer silver or gold suppliers for reliability, and work with as many suppliers as possible. Holiday peaks such as Black Friday, Cyber Monday (the online shopping day after Black Friday) and Christmas can cause stock shortages with disorganized suppliers.
  • Avoid sourcing from marketplaces like AliExpress (a large online store for cheap goods, shipped from China). Sourcing means finding products to buy. The reasons: long shipping times, unbranded products and little to no quality control.

Conflicting Guidance

3 min

Bronze: never sell versus sell on existing listings

  • A supplier is the company whose products you sell. Its tier is its rank. Bronze is the lowest rank.
  • Never sell with bronze suppliers.
  • Do not run ads for them. Ads are paid promotions online.
  • Exception: products already listed for sale, meaning they are already on your store for buyers to see.
  • The exception applies where the margin works without ad costs. Margin is the money you keep after costs.
  • The exception also requires that MSRP is below 2X wholesale. MSRP (manufacturer's suggested retail price) is the price the brand recommends. Wholesale is the price you pay the supplier.
  • Keep these products with a one-cent CPC. CPC (cost per click) is what you pay each time someone clicks your ad.
  • Remove them if nothing sells.
  • Applies when: you are building a store. The rule above applies.
  • Applies when: you already have listings that earn a healthy margin without ad spend. The exception applies.

Tier versus judgment

  • A supplier's tier does not decide whether you work with it.
  • Judge the supplier by its cost of goods, which should be about 50% of the selling price. Cost of goods is what the product costs you before you sell it.
  • Judge the supplier by whether it enforces MAP. MAP (minimum advertised price) is the lowest price a seller may show publicly. Enforcing it means making sure every seller follows it.
  • Judge the supplier by the discount it offers.
  • Silver and gold are higher ranks than bronze. Tier rules still apply to their screening, approval, and MAP expectations. Screening means checking your store before a supplier decides. Approval means the supplier says yes.
  • Use the cost-of-goods test to judge any supplier you can actually access.
  • Applies when: you are comparing offers from suppliers you can access without paying.

Bronze without MAP

  • A supplier without a MAP policy is a lower tier.
  • It can still be workable if margins hold after shipping.
  • Thin percentage margins with no MAP are treated as bronze. Thin margins mean a small share of profit per sale. Treat these as bronze and avoid them.
  • Applies when: margins are high enough after shipping and ad costs. The supplier is workable.
  • Applies when: margins are thin and volume tiers apply. Volume tiers are discounts for bigger orders. Avoid the supplier.

Gold approval timing

  • Gold is the highest tier. Approval usually takes 3 to 12 months after your store has traffic, sales, and a silver track record. Traffic means visitors to your store. A track record is proof of past results.
  • For suppliers that link to your store, approval takes 3 months, 6 months, or more than a year. Link means they point customers to your store.
  • For brands that want a long sales history, approval takes a year or two.
  • Applies when: the supplier's own site has a referral section. A referral section is a page that sends buyers to stores.
  • Applies when: the supplier wants to see sales history.

Silver share of suppliers

  • Silver is a tier above bronze.
  • 100% of your first five suppliers should be silver.
  • About 95% of all suppliers should be silver over the long term.
  • Silver share: 90 to 95 percent of a retailer's suppliers. A retailer is a store owner.
  • Majority of both total suppliers and sales revenue. Sales revenue is the money you take in from sales.
  • Nearly all suppliers until you have a sales track record.
  • Applies when: you are planning your first five suppliers and your long-term base.
  • Applies when: your store is still building its track record. Use nearly all silver.

Gold share of suppliers

  • A gold supplier authorizes around 5 to 10 retailers in total. Authorizes means gives permission to sell its brand.
  • Gold share: roughly 5 to 10 percent of your supplier base. This counts how much of your suppliers are gold.
  • Applies when: you are judging how many retailers a gold brand works with, versus how many gold brands you should keep.

Target Mix and Sequencing

1 min

Target mix

  • Silver: 100% of your first five suppliers, and about 95% of all suppliers over the long term.
  • Gold: about 5 to 10% of your suppliers.
  • Bronze: avoid entirely.
  • A store can be profitable (make money) with silver suppliers only.
  • Gold adds strength but is not required.

Sequence

  1. Build a store with silver suppliers, which should make up most of your supplier base.
  2. Build sales, traffic, and brand reputation with silver suppliers. Brand reputation is how much customers trust the brand.
  3. Once you have sales and traffic, apply to gold suppliers.
  4. Keep contacting other suppliers while you wait for gold approval.
