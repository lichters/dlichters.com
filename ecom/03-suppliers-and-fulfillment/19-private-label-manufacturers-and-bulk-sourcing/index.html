Playbook › 3. Suppliers and fulfillment

Private label, manufacturers and bulk sourcing

Section 3.19 · 19 of 22 in this chapter · 41 min read

In short

  • Choose how you will buy before you choose a supplier.
  • Start with dropshipping: the supplier ships each order to your customer, so you do not hold stock.
  • Do not buy stock in bulk until you know the product sells.
  • Order one sample and check quality before you order more.
  • Negotiate the minimum order quantity down before you agree to a price.
More key points
  • Email a supplier first. If there is no reply in 2 weeks, call them.
  • Order a sample and check it yourself before you commit to a big order.
  • Pay by bank transfer: 30% upfront, the rest after you see photos of your finished product.
  • Production and shipping take weeks. Order early so your stock does not run out.
  • Do not pay for custom packaging at launch. Wait until the product sells well for 1 to 2 weeks.
  • Test with small orders first. Buy bulk stock only after sales are steady.
  • A 3PL is an outside company that stores and ships your stock. Move to one after two to three weeks of steady orders.
  • Order one sample before you buy a full range.
  • Keep the same supplier for repeat designs, so the product stays the same.
  • Only sell products you can deliver on the promised date.

Choosing a sourcing model

2 min
  • Pick the model before you pick a supplier. A supplier is the company that makes or ships your products.
  • Dropshipping means the supplier ships each order directly to the customer.
  • A manufacturer model means you buy upfront in minimum order quantities, or MOQs (the smallest amount a supplier will sell you).
  • Start with an existing store in your chosen niche (a narrow market segment) before you move into private label (your own brand on a product made for you) or bulk importing (buying a large shipment from overseas).
  • Bulk importing is more capital-intensive and time-consuming than dropshipping. Capital-intensive means it needs a lot of money. One example is a 40-foot HQ container (a large shipping container) at 30,000 to 50,000 USD.
  • Do bulk importing only with more money and time available.
  • Use a two-step path: launch the dropshipping store first, then research the niche.
  • Enter private label only when you can use the store's sales data (your record of what sold) to make the decision.
  • Use off-the-shelf white-label products as an entry tier. White label means a ready-made product sold under your own brand. An entry tier is a simple first step.
  • Sell an existing product under your own brand to prove demand (proof that customers want it). Later, move to custom fragrance, molds (custom shapes) and packaging in a later version.
  • Margins (the profit you keep per sale) are lower at this stage, but you learn how manufacturing works.
  • Do not invest in inventory, custom molds or fragrance development before demand is proven.
  • Investing early locks up time and money before anyone has shown they will buy.
  • Avoid private labeling technically complex products at the start. These need specialized manufacturing know-how (skills only a few factories have).
  • Customers often prefer to buy from a specific established manufacturer. Enter that category later, once expertise and demand are clear.
  • Use the just-in-time model first for private label. Just-in-time means the supplier makes your branded product only when an order comes in.
  • With just-in-time there is no upfront inventory cost (money paid for stock before you sell it). Choose this before any loan-funded stock purchase (buying stock with borrowed money). Consider in-stock inventory only later. **
  • Test a private-label product in this order:
    1. Find one or two products in the niche that could be private-labeled.
    2. Test the model with a few units.
    3. If it works, create a version 2.0 (an improved second version) with the supplier and seek exclusivity (only you can sell it).

Finding and vetting suppliers

5 min

Where to search

Vetting means checking carefully before you trust. This section uses the word in that sense.

  • Alibaba and AliExpress are the usual online sourcing platforms (marketplaces where suppliers sell) for white-label commodity accessories. Commodity accessories are basic products many factories already make.
  • Electronics such as remote thermometers are harder to source. Check the supplier more carefully. **
  • Alibaba.com is the platform for finding manufacturers. It is mainly useful for sourcing from China. **
  • Thomasnet.com is used to find American manufacturers. Prefer an American manufacturer when the store sells primarily in the United States. **
  • Thomas Net and Google are used to find American suppliers. Search for the product plus terms such as manufacturer, private label or white label. Examples: "grilling utensils manufacturer" or "grilling utensil white label". **
  • Alibaba and AliExpress are used to source products internationally. Prepare your questions before you contact suppliers. **
  • Search Alibaba when you want to sell under your own brand or source directly, not only for dropshipping.
  • Alibaba's catalog can hold items useful for building a private-label store.
  • If you cannot find an exact match of a product on AliExpress, it may be a private-label item made by a factory with custom features.
  • Such products need a different sourcing route than standard AliExpress items.
  • Search Google for suppliers in your product category.
  • If the dropshipping supplier cannot produce custom packaging, search Google for Chinese box companies.

Finding the manufacturer behind a brand

**

  1. Identify the companies in your market that already sell the product. They may white-label for you (put your brand on their product) if you build a relationship with them.
  2. Check whether one of the brands you carry is actually a house brand (a brand owned by a manufacturer). Compare the company address on the product listing (the product's sales page) with the address of a known manufacturer.
  3. If the addresses match, the brand is produced by that manufacturer. That manufacturer can usually white-label the product for you.
  4. Contact the manufacturer directly to request white-label production.
  • If a brand's address matches a manufacturer's address, that manufacturer makes the product. Expect it to offer white-label production. **
  • Once a product becomes popular in China, many factories will manufacture it.
  • Manufacturers will often contact a store that sells a hot product to white-label it. Expect this outreach. Do not treat it as a differentiator (a unique edge over competitors). **
  • Not every product category suits American manufacturing. A store targeting the US market should check for a US manufacturer first.
  • You may need to accept a Chinese manufacturer for some products. **

Agents and intermediaries

An agent or intermediary is a middleman who connects you with factories.

  • Chinese agents cannot easily provide customization (changes made to fit your needs) or branding services.
  • These services require connections to separate factories and English-speaking representatives. This adds complexity and time.
  • Unified Dropshipping, CJ Dropshipping, Honest Fulfillment and Ecom Ops all offer branding services and fulfillment center integration. A fulfillment center is a warehouse that packs and ships orders.
  • Use these services for private labeling and quality control (checking that products are good).
  • Most dropshipping suppliers, including CJ Dropshipping, Hyper Rescue USA Drop and Zendrop, offer customization. Examples are printed thank-you cards and custom boxes.
  • The store owner only needs to supply the design files.
  • For logo product customization (putting your logo on a product), contact the manufacturer directly, for example via Alibaba. Avoid a dropshipping middleman.
  • The manufacturer can offer the best price for 500 to 1,000 units of custom printing.
  • Avoid unknown private suppliers reached through random messages or unlisted websites.
  • One operator paid such a person. They received silver jewelry instead of the ordered gold jewelry, and had to refund most customers.

Supplier checks before ordering

  • Confirm the supplier allows private label before you negotiate price.
  • There is no point negotiating costs if you cannot put your brand on the product.
  • Ask for a copy of the supplier's business license (the official registration of the company) as proof of a legitimate company. Refusal is a red flag.
  • Before you order from a new supplier, ask for a photo or video of the actual product as it will be made in the factory.
  • If a supplier refuses or delays this, treat it as a serious warning sign.
  • The request is simple. It gives you a basis for quality control.
  • Visit the factory, or have a representative visit, for due diligence (checking a supplier before you pay). This is strongly recommended.
  • Finding a supplier for a unique product made to spec (to your exact design) means messaging and emailing many suppliers.
  • Only one of them may deliver the right quality.
  • Chinese suppliers are often easy to work with, but local suppliers can also be a fit.

Private label, white label and dropshipping

4 min

Definitions

  • White label means buying a supplier's already-developed product and adding your own label.
  • It needs far less product development than building a new item from scratch.
  • Commodity accessories with many existing suppliers are the easiest starting point. **
  • Private label products are made by a contract or third-party manufacturer. They are sold under the buyer's own brand name.
  • They can be sold through wholesalers (companies that buy in bulk and resell), your own website, or marketplaces such as Amazon, eBay, Walmart or Etsy. **
  • Creating a private-label product requires the manufacturer to produce a special batch for the buyer.
  • This custom run is why private label costs more and is harder to start than white label. **
  • White label manufacturers can produce thousands of units of a single product per day.
  • That is why they can supply many brands at once. **
  • A supplier that relabels its stock product under your brand name is still called private label.
  • The product is unchanged and still dropshipped from the supplier.
  • Private label usually requires a higher minimum order quantity (MOQ) than white label.
  • The manufacturer must run a special batch built to the buyer's own ingredient, packaging or label specifications.
  • White label products made in large standard runs (large batches of the same product) typically allow a much lower entry order. **
  • Importing a generic product to sell under your own brand requires a warehouse or fulfillment center relationship.
  • The brand name you create must differ from your company name.
  • Competitors can sell the same product through their stores. **

Which products fit

  • Commodity accessories with no differentiating factor are strong candidates for white labeling.
  • Examples include utensils, cedar planks, thermometers, smoking racks, grill covers and slicing knives. **
  • Pellet or consumable refills (products that get used up, such as wood pellets) can be sold as a white-label product.
  • This creates recurring revenue (repeat income from repeat buyers), because manufacturers often approach brands to put their label on existing products. **
  • For electronic accessories such as remote thermometers, a simpler manual or non-electronic product that serves the same purpose can be easier to source.
  • It also carries less quality risk. Check whether a manual option exists before you commit to an electronic supplier. **
  • For average-priced products around 50 to 100 dollars, order samples first and check quality.
  • If quality is poor, create a private-label version and fulfill it through a fulfillment center.
  • This keeps the flow similar to dropshipping.
  • Give private-label products their own brand name, separate from the main store.
  • Sell them alongside established brands on the main site and through other online retailers.
  • This gives customers a familiar buying environment while building a separate asset (something of value you own).
  • Owning the supply chain (control over where products come from and how they reach customers) removes stock-out surprises. A stock-out means you run out of product.
  • Stock-outs make forecasting (predicting future sales) hard. They also lead to refunds when a supplier suddenly reports no stock.
  • Private labeling shifts this control to the brand, with its own set of problems to solve. **
  • For gift products, ask the supplier to include personalized note cards in the shipping packaging.
  • If the supplier does not do this by default, contact them and request it.

Testing a private-label idea

  • Test a private-label product with a supplier in this order:
    1. Find one or two products in the niche that could be private-labeled.
    2. Test the model with a few units.
    3. If it works, create a version 2.0 with the supplier and seek exclusivity on it.
  • Transparency costs money. Third-party testing (tests by an outside lab) and full sourcing disclosure (telling customers where products are made) make manufacturing more expensive.
  • The brand estimated that some of its products could be made for roughly half the cost without these practices. It keeps them for authenticity.

Transition from a multi-brand store

Private label transition from a multi-brand store (a store that sells many brands' products):

  1. Build the store with third-party brands and keep it running for six months to a year. Push as much traffic (visitors) and as many sales as possible.
  2. Survey customers after purchase about the buying experience, what they like about products and what help they want.
  3. After that period, analyze your sales data to find the top three best-selling products and the reasons they sell. Reasons include price, durability or color options.
  4. Build a separate brand website, for example on WordPress, that presents your brand instead of selling products as a store.
  5. Find three product variations from manufacturers that match the winning criteria, such as a good price, durability and multiple colors.
  6. Add your new brand to your existing store alongside the other brands. Promote it in the product page sidebar, without claiming ownership on the store itself.
  7. Once you have shipped the products, gathered reviews and proven demand, add them to your brand website. Then sell your products to competing retailers as a supplier.

Research before private labeling

Research before private labeling:

  1. Launch a dropshipping store in the chosen niche first.
  2. List products from around 20 brands in that niche.
  3. Track which brands and products sell most, their top price points, and their features and benefits.
  4. Introduce your own brand at a price range and with features that proven products already sell at.
  5. Sell that brand on your own store and on competitor stores.
  • Once a product sells consistently, a refined or better-quality version from a manufacturer can improve the product. It can also support a higher price.
  • Only do this if the base product is already selling well.
  • Manufacturers offer products as dropshipping or bulk stock.
  • Choose the model by the stage of the business, not by the supplier's pitch.

Negotiation

  • Always negotiate minimum quantities. Suppliers initially quote high minimums.
  • Emphasize that this is your first private-label attempt, to push them lower. For example, go from 500 to 200 to 100.
  • Propose a minimum order quantity, negotiating down from the supplier's initial quote.
  • Request an MOQ waiver (the supplier agrees to a smaller minimum). Explain that you are new and will order larger quantities once satisfied.
  • Raise MOQ questions early, during sampling.
  • MOQs are usually negotiable. Suppliers often open with very high numbers, so do not be discouraged by the first quote. **
  • Do not ask "What is your MOQ (minimum order quantity)?" as the first question.
  • This signals you are small and unprofessional. Request a tiered pricing sheet instead. A tiered pricing sheet is a price list that changes by order size. The MOQ will be provided in the response.
  • Ask every question you have with a supplier. Leave nothing unknown, and treat all terms as negotiable.
  • When a supplier's minimum order quantity is too high, ask for a sample or tester order of around 10 pieces before committing to the full MOQ.
  • Alibaba-type platforms are commonly used for this request.
  • Ordering fewer than 100 units will not get a cheaper price from manufacturers such as those on Alibaba.
  • Ask for a single sample unit at wholesale price (the bulk price) to inspect quality before committing. Mention it during the phone call.
  • Sample units are not required for every business, but they can be requested. **

Costs and pricing effects

2 min
  • Product cost with private labeling increases by double or by a quarter, depending on product type.
  • Upfront stock investment is typically $500-2,000. It depends on product price and minimum quantity.
  • Example: a product costing $4.99 including shipping may double in cost, or rise by a quarter.
  • Pricing varies by supplier and customization level, such as logo placement and packaging.
  • Putting a logo on a product costs about 30 cents extra per unit.
  • It requires buying a minimum order quantity in bulk and holding inventory.
  • There is no way around the bulk purchase when branding products.
  • Example: a $49.95 product. Supplier cost was around $6 to $7 for a single unit.
  • When ordered in bulk, the cost was about $2 to $3 or less per unit.
  • Bulk orders lower unit cost significantly. They only pay off once sales volume is consistent.
  • A product costing $10 at 500 to 1,000 units can cost $12 to $13 at lower quantities.
  • Bulk orders in the source reduced unit costs by about 50-60%. This raises profit margin.
  • Inventory is a risk. Buy in bulk only with consistent sales projections (estimates of future sales).
  • Example: buying 200 units at about 2 USD per unit instead of 7 USD per unit lowers unit cost substantially.
  • Stock is kept at the supplier's location. Orders are fulfilled from there, with only shipping costs paid per order.
  • Do not buy stock in bulk at the start, because it is not known yet whether the product will sell.
  • Consider bulk buying only when scaling (growing sales).
  • Buy stock in bulk only once a product generates consistent orders. An example is around 50 to 100 orders per day for 10 consecutive days.
  • Start with a small stock and expand as the product proves itself.
  • Stock investment can be as high as 50,000 dollars for private label.
  • Start bulk buying only after sales have been consistent for at least 3-4 weeks, ideally one to two months.
  • Minimum order quantities tie up capital (money stuck in goods you have not sold). Sea freight (shipping by boat) adds delay and risk.
  • Do not buy in bulk before product quality has been verified.
  • Test with a small first order. A bulk order from a supplier with quality problems multiplies the losses.

First contact, communication and channels

1 min

Outreach procedure

1 min

Procedure for contacting suppliers (the companies that make or sell your product):

  1. Search Google for suppliers in your product category.
  2. Send an initial email asking for information.
  3. If there is no response within 2 weeks, follow up with a phone call. Suppliers often ignore first emails.
  4. Ask for a tiered pricing sheet (a price list where the cost per unit drops as the order gets bigger). It should show unit costs for volumes such as 1-500, 500-1,000, 1,000-2,500, 2,500-5,000 and 5,000-10,000 units.
  5. Confirm the supplier allows private label (you put your own brand name on a product they make).
  6. Ask for the lead time (the time from order to shipment ready).
  7. Build the relationship by keeping in touch and calling when needed.
  • Use phone calls when emails go unanswered. Suppliers often prefer phone contact to check whether the buyer is serious.
  • A phone call can turn an email that got no reply into an engaged supplier.
  • Overseas suppliers bring time zone differences (the clock hours between countries) and a language barrier.
  • Reputable suppliers usually have someone who speaks decent English, but the English will not be perfect. **
  • Private suppliers give dedicated support through chat apps such as Skype or WhatsApp. This includes daily updates and phone calls.
  • This helps build a long-term partnership.
  • Private-label suppliers such as Unify may prefer different contact channels, for example Skype or WhatsApp. Ask which channel they use before sending product links and requests.

Samples, tests and quality control

1 min

Ordering a sample

1 min

A sample is one test copy of the product.

  • Use a fast but cheap shipping method for a sample, not an expensive express courier.
  • A sample only needs to arrive quickly enough to test the product.
  • Ask the agent or supplier to order one sample.
  • Have them film the item and take close-up shots of the stitching (the thread seams) and details.
  • Judge the sample by your own standards.
  • If quality is good enough, start selling and see whether demand exists.
  • Ask the warehouse to take photos when the product arrives.
  • Expect to pay for samples, although a small discount may be offered.
  • Some suppliers may never send samples, so do not rely on a promised free sample.

Sample check procedure

1 min

Procedure for checking a supplier sample before committing:

  1. Ask for a photo or video of the sample. Include close-up shots of the material and a stretch test (pulling the fabric to see how far it stretches) for stretchable fabric.
  2. If the product needs a logo, have the logo applied to the sample. Then check its placement and quality.
  3. Have the sample shipped to yourself so you can inspect the product in person.
  4. Place a test order through your own store. This shows the real packaging, the shipping and how the parcel arrives at the customer.
  5. Expect to pay for samples, although a small discount may be offered. Some suppliers may never send them, so do not rely on a promised free sample.

Inspecting your own first batch

1 min

Procedure for inspecting a first batch (the first small run of products made):

  1. Ship your first smaller batch directly to yourself, via Amazon Partnered Carrier (Amazon's paid shipping service for sellers), typically about $50.
  2. Inspect the product quality, packaging and branding.
  3. If satisfactory, reship to Amazon, another about $50.

Alternatively, use a third-party inspection service (an outside company that checks goods for you) in China or the United States. Check before forwarding to Amazon. This shifts quality control risk to the service provider.

Substitutions and mismatches

1 min
  • A substitute product causes quality complaints and refunds.
  • Example: a printed item shipped instead of the woven product (cloth made by weaving threads) that was ordered.
  • Confirm the exact material and construction before ordering.
  • Check what arrives instead of trusting a video call alone.
  • A mismatch in product, color, material or country can lead to refunds and closing the store.
  • Make sure the supplier actually ships the product you sell.
  • Check quality before going live (before the store opens to customers).

Ongoing quality control

1 min
  • Start with tech packs (technical design documents for the factory) and spec sheets (product specification sheets).
  • Then run quality checks continuously.
  • Get photos or videos of production regularly.

Procedure for production runs (one batch made in the factory):

  1. Write a contract with clauses (single rules in the contract) that hold the supplier accountable for the approved sample.
  2. Have the first lot of every production run sent for inspection.
  3. Request weekly photos or videos of the product during production.
  4. Check that each batch matches the approved sample.
  • Suppliers can change quality over repeat runs. For example, they may use less plastic in molds (the shapes the product is poured or pressed into).
  • They may also substitute ingredients or prioritize other customers.
  • Without checks on every run, a brand may only notice after the damage is done.
  • Holding stock at a supplier warehouse in China means you can see the product before shipping it out.
  • Pure dropshipping only pushes the product to another supplier.
  • This visibility matters for quality control.

Tech packs

1 min

A tech pack is the key document for suppliers and manufacturers (factories). It can be:

  • Downloaded as a template.
  • Hired out to be made from scratch.
  • Made yourself in Illustrator or Photoshop (design programs).

It must include:

  1. Digital sketch of the garment
  2. Construction details and trims (small parts such as buttons and zips)
  3. Measurements
  4. Fabrics
  5. Size
  6. Look inspiration
  7. Colours
  8. Artwork scales (how big the logo or print is)

Essentials needed to run a clothing brand from the start:

  1. A label maker for product labels.
  2. Poly sender bags (plastic mailing bags) for shipping.
  3. Home storage or a warehouse space to keep stock.

Payment terms and order risk

1 min
  • Negotiate payment terms (the rules for when and how much you pay) with factories as early as possible.
  • Without them, the business has to fund every order upfront (paid before the goods exist).
  • That quickly strains cash flow (money coming in and going out) when money is scarce.

Payment terms procedure:

  1. Use bank wire transfer (money sent directly from bank to bank), not Western Union (a money transfer service), for secure payment.
  2. Offer a 30% deposit upfront and 70% on completion.
  3. Request photographic proof of your manufactured product with branding before releasing the final 70% payment.
  4. Verify the supplier's legitimacy through its verified badges (official marks on the supplier's profile that show it was checked).

Check that the verified badges are Trade Assurance (Alibaba's buyer protection program), Gold Supplier (a paid membership level) or Assessed Supplier. These indicate third-party inspections and legitimacy.

  • Negotiate a smaller initial order, for example 200-250 units, for a new supplier.
  • Use this as a test batch to verify quality and reliability.
  • Once Amazon pays you after two weeks of sales, use that money to fund the 70% balance on a reorder (a new order of the same product).
  • The supplier is still building the first batch at that point.
  • Keep lead times short, 7-14 days, so reorders arrive before inventory runs out.

  • Lead time is the time from payment to finished product.
  • Longer lead times delay your first stock arrival.
  • They make quick reorders hard when stock runs low, especially in peak seasons (the busiest sales periods).
  • Long production lead times must be included in inventory (your stock) and promotion planning.

  • They also differ by freight method. Measure your own lead times.
  • Longer supplier lead times of 6-8+ weeks reduce competition, because most sellers avoid the wait.
  • Accepting longer lead times is a competitive advantage.
  • It makes ranking (showing high in search results) and dominating the listing (taking most of the top spots for a product) easier.
  • Bulk stock (a large order of stock) should be ordered when there is still enough time to cover production and transit (shipping).
  • Plan the next order so the stock does not run out.

Inventory in transit

1 min
  • Your stock can sit at several stages at once.
  • The stages are: at the manufacturer, at the freight origin ready for shipping, on the vessel (ship), at customs (the border checkpoint), and in your warehouse.
  • Even at a steady sales rate, you must have several shipments ordered ahead to avoid stockouts (running out of stock). **

Sea and air freight

1 min

Choosing the mode

1 min

Freight means shipping goods in bulk.

  • Air freight is faster but more expensive.
  • Sea freight is cheaper but takes 30-45 days.
  • Use air freight for initial orders and smaller shipments with short lead times.
  • Switch to sea freight only when ordering large quantities of 5,000-10,000+ units lasting 1-6 weeks.
  • At that volume the total shipping cost becomes economical, despite the longer transit time.
  • Shipping time from China by sea freight is about 14 days to California.
  • Add about one more week to reach Amazon's warehouses.
  • Air freight from China can arrive within three to five days. **
  • Compare the total landed cost (every cost added up to get goods to your door), not only the unit price.
  • Include shipping and time in that comparison.
  • A US supplier can reduce shipping cost and lead time.
  • Products can be labeled Made in the USA, which helps on Amazon. **
  • Amazon's paid carrier service can collect goods from a US supplier or printer. It then delivers them to Amazon warehouses.
  • The seller reported this is cheap enough to beat sea freight costs for US-made products. **

Landed cost by mode (case)

1 min
  • The landed cost of one blanket was about 20 to 25 USD by air freight.
  • It fell to about 7 to 10 USD with sea freight at large order volumes.
  • Order the next season's stock early and ship it by sea to lower the per-unit cost.

Consolidated shipping to a warehouse

1 min

Consolidated means combined into one batch.

  • Shipping from a supplier to a warehouse in China costs about $0.50 per piece for a single order.
  • It drops to about $0.10 per product when local domestic shipping is consolidated across around 10 orders.
  • Stock shipped in one consolidated batch therefore has a much lower per-unit logistics cost (the cost of moving goods).

Sea freight and suppliers in China

1 min
  • Sea freight from a supplier with a direct address (the supplier ships straight to your own address) takes about 20-30 days.
  • For a fast-moving product, order in advance and keep stock on hand.

Freight on board (FOB, a shipping term: the seller pays costs until goods are loaded on the ship) terms for goods from China. Map the full route and cost every stage before setting prices.

  1. Freight on board at the origin port, where the goods are loaded onto the vessel.
  2. Ocean transport to the destination.
  3. Customs clearance (getting goods past border checks).
  4. Transport from the port to your warehouse.
  5. Preparation for shipping to customers.

Add the costs of all these stages, plus the outbound (outgoing) shipping cost to the customer, into your product price. **

Custom packaging means boxes made for your brand. Inserts are small papers placed inside the parcel. Branding means making products look like your own brand.

When to invest

1 min

Inventory pipeline and stock levels

Staging stock

1 min

Staging stock means placing stock in places closer to customers, step by step.

  • Stock up a little before the holiday.
  • The warehouse is closed only for about three days, but it has limited capacity (space).
  • Start with bulk stock from the supplier while testing.
  • Switch to a 3PL (a third-party warehouse company that stores and ships for you) once consistent orders have run for two to three weeks or more.
  • A 3PL is an investment and carries risk.
  • It fits best with predictable sales.
  • Consider holding stock in a local market only once a product brings consistent orders.
  • For example, at least 50 to 100 orders per day for a consecutive period of 10 days.
  • Before that, buying a small stock locally or in China, around 10 pieces, keeps risk low.
  • It also shortens delivery to about three days.
  • Stock levels should be planned around the time it takes for inventory to move through each stage.
  • Stock can be ordered ahead to cover the next period.

Phased approach to moving stock

1 min

Phased approach to moving stock:

  1. When entering a new region, fulfill directly from China (pack and ship orders from there). Buy customized goods, store them in China and ship from there.
  2. When a product sells strongly in one country, move at least the best sellers to a warehouse close to the customers. For example, the UK for UK customers, Germany for German customers.
  3. For widespread demand across Europe, keep some stock in China. Shipping from China to a country such as Sweden can be cheaper than shipping from a neighboring country.
  4. Scale later once the first country is serviced well (customers get fast, good service there).
  • Fulfil from China when margins are thin (little profit per sale).
  • Shipping a product to a local warehouse can eat up profit.
  • Move fulfillment closer to customers when the product is high-ticket (expensive) and margins are large enough to afford local fulfillment.
  • Shorter delivery times improve the customer experience.
  • Placing stock in a warehouse close to the buyers is worth doing once a product sells consistently.
  • Wait until demand is proven before moving stock overseas.
  • Moving stock to a US warehouse could cut shipping times by three to four days or more.
  • It means shipping large containers that can be lost with the money inside.
  • Only move stock once the store is stable and the extra speed justifies the container risk.
  • For faster shipping in the US, products should be stored in US warehouses. **
  • The products themselves do not need to be manufactured in the US. **
  • Bulk stock held in China can help avoid delays around Chinese holidays.
  • Keep the stock ready there so orders can still ship.

Before the holiday

1 min
  • Communicate with your supplier about Chinese New Year closure dates.

  • Without stock, orders take about a month, and the advice is to wait until the holiday ends.
  • Plan bulk orders ahead of predictable slow-supply periods such as Chinese New Year.
  • Stock up a little before the holiday, because the warehouse is closed only for about three days but has limited capacity.

During the holiday

1 min
  • Stop running ads for products you cannot ship, because during the factory closure you cannot fulfil orders.
  • If you have no stock, a pause of at least one week is advised around the holiday.
  • Slow down or turn off ads during the roughly two-week period around New Year, when suppliers are barely available.
  • Reduce product variants (versions of one product, such as different colors).
  • Keep only the two best-sellers, marking others as sold out without removing them from the site.
  • During the factory closure, suppliers reply slowly, so ask questions early.
  • Use the time to prepare creatives (ad images and videos), optimise the website, improve existing products and research more products.
  • Use the quiet period to catch up on orders and negotiate a better price, because quantities per item are higher.
  • Watch for suppliers who offer bulk orders as a fix for Chinese New Year without checking whether stock will be fulfilled during the holiday.
  • The bulk order can become a money trap (money you pay and cannot use) if workers are away.

Stages of the transition

1 min
  • Start with bulk stock (many units bought at once) from the supplier while you test.
  • Switch to a 3PL warehouse (an outside company that stores and ships your stock) after consistent orders have run for two to three weeks or more.
  • At $100k-500k per month, if a fulfillment agent (a helper who packs and ships orders) is still used, the store should build its brand and move products into a US 3PL.

Tariffs and country of manufacture

1 min
  • Manufacturing in the US can avoid import taxes (tariffs) that change often.
  • This matters for brands whose 3PL and customers are in the US.
  • Compare the total landed cost (the full cost of goods at your door, including tariffs) before choosing a country.

Unsold MOQ stock

1 min

  • For products bought under a supplier minimum order quantity (MOQ, the fewest units the supplier sells per order), ship orders quickly.

  • Slow shipping drives chargebacks (a customer reverses a card payment through their bank).

  • Slow shipping also drives RDR refunds (quick refunds that settle a dispute before it becomes a chargeback).

  • This applies especially to products sold on a custom brand.

  • Options for an MOQ product that is not selling well:

    1. White-hand method: refund all customers who ordered the product.
    2. Black-hand method: ask the supplier to send a similar product with a different book cover, and keep selling the same product under the new cover.

  • The black-hand method has downsides.

  • Customers may complain.

  • The total gross revenue (all sales money before costs) and net margin (profit after costs) are still kept.

  • Chargebacks can still occur because customers say the product was not as described.

  • It is worth doing when the order value is low, since profit is still profit.

  • Use the black-hand method only when the product suddenly stops selling and the proof of concept (a test showing the product works) no longer holds.

  • Do not use it frequently.

  • It is mostly a testing tool.

1 min

Clothing development workflow:

Private Label, Custom Labelling and Trademarks

1 min
  • Custom labelling and private label products (your own brand on a product made by someone else) are not realistic for a beginner with a contract.
  • The reason: a single Chinese factory cannot be bound by contract to stay loyal unless it is a very large brand.
  • A cheaper, more practical route is custom labelling, such as a logo on the product, custom packaging and boxing, combined with a better customer experience.

  • Factor the cost and time of these registrations into any private-label plan, along with sampling and contracts.
