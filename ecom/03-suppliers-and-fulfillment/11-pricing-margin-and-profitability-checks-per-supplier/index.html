Playbook › 3. Suppliers and fulfillment

Pricing, margin and profitability checks per supplier

Section 3.11 · 11 of 22 in this chapter · 58 min read

In short

  • Run paid ads only on products with a healthy margin (the profit left after all costs). Thin-margin products stay in free listings.
  • Check the MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price a shop may show in ads) before you list a product.
  • Do not offer a price-match guarantee on products whose supplier does not enforce MAP.
  • When a competitor breaks MAP, take screenshots, send them to the supplier, and follow up.
  • Each supplier has its own prices and rules. Check them one supplier at a time.
More key points
  • Your wholesale cost should be 50% or less of MAP, the lowest advertised price.
  • Count every cost first, including shipping, fees and ads. Then set the price.
  • Shipping can erase your profit. Check it before you list a product.
  • Build steady sales first. Then ask the supplier for better prices.
  • Run paid ads only on products whose profit covers the ad cost.
  • If a competitor sells below the MAP (the lowest price the brand allows you to advertise), send the supplier screenshots and ask them to fix it.
  • Set aside a share of every sale in its own account so you can pay supplier bills.
  • If the supplier does not fix a MAP violation, stop paid ads for that brand.

Cost inputs for the margin calculation

3 min
  • Cost of goods sold (COGS) is only accurate when it is entered per product in Shopify. COGS equals the cost to deliver the product to your 3PL plus the average shipping cost per unit to the customer.
  • Packaging and supplies cover only the materials used to fulfil an order: the box, tape, polymailer and the cost of the shipping label itself. Postage paid to the carrier is entered separately as shipping cost.
  • Shipping cost is the average postage paid per order for a typical order size, for example $9.17. Take it from actual carrier costs, not from packaging.
  • If a 3PL charges per pick, an order with several items incurs one pick fee per item, so the 3PL cost per order must be scaled to the average number of picks. Typical 3PL pick and pack fees run about $1.50 to $2.50 per pick. If no 3PL is used, this input is $0.
  • Keep COGS as low as possible, but never at the expense of the quality customers notice. Cutting costs that buyers feel will hurt repeat sales.
  • Customisation and rush production options from the supplier carry their own costs. Add a markup to them, and ask suppliers which custom or rush options they offer before building them into your pricing.
  • A supplier's percentage margin must be checked for its basis. Ask whether it is a net margin left after the product cost or a markup on that cost. The two give very different selling prices, so confirm which one applies before committing.

Worked cost examples

  • Ceramic flower pot: sold at $30 (about $29.90) with an $11 COGS including fulfilment costs.
  • Zendrop: one unit sourced at $20 with $10 shipping to the customer gives a total of $30 entered as COGS + SHIPPING per unit.
  • CJ Dropshipping: a cost of $17 per item on CJ was entered into profit calculators to check margin.

CJ Dropshipping price basis

  • On CJ Dropshipping, after you choose the variant and the target country, the product page shows a field called total dropshipping price. It already includes the product cost and shipping to the customer, so use it as the base cost for pricing instead of the item price alone.

Confirming cost before pricing

  1. Estimate the product cost from AliExpress listicles by checking average prices.
  2. Open a sourcing request at CJ Dropshipping to get accurate supplier prices.
  3. Use the sourcing result as the real cost basis for pricing.
  • AliExpress prices are only estimates, because sellers without fulfilment can set any price. Use a sourcing request to confirm the cost.

Shipping outside the MAP

  • Shipping costs are typically not included in a supplier's MAP price, because shipping varies by destination. If a supplier does not explicitly state that shipping is included in its pricing, assume it is not.

  • Estimating shipping and margin:

    1. Find the supplier's location and zip code on its website.
    2. Note the product's size and weight.
    3. Get a freight estimate from freightbroker.com between the supplier's zip code and two customer zip codes in the states you plan to sell in, for example one in Florida and one in California.
    4. Average the two estimates to get a shipping cost.
    5. Subtract wholesale cost and shipping from the selling price to see the margin left for ads and fees.

  • Some suppliers based in Japan charge an average of about $15 shipping per product, which can remove most of the margin on lower-priced items. Add this shipping cost into the spread calculation before listing their products.

  • If a supplier adds tax to its wholesale prices, compare competitor prices with that tax included and recalculate the MAP-to-wholesale spread. Keep the products only if the spread still works.

Retail price basis

1 min
  • Google Shopping is used for competitor price research: search your product names as a customer, then compare sellers' prices to your wholesale cost. Use store averages, not the lowest prices.
  • Extremely low competitor prices are often a warning sign rather than a bargain. Stores selling with almost no margin often have poor customer service, late or missing shipments and short business lifespans. Check company names, websites, reviews and ratings before treating their price as market price.

Benchmarks: MAP, wholesale and the 50% rule

2 min

The sources give different thresholds. Each one applies in a different situation, so read them together.

Target spread Wholesale of $1,000 should correspond to a MAP of about $2,000. Planning target when reviewing a price list.

Where sources give different starting points for a supplier with no MAP, use the 3x starting point only as a first guess. Use the 2x fallback when no clear competitor price exists. Revise both against competitor prices and your own margin.

  • Under the 50% rule, if a supplier's price is far off and it will not negotiate, the supplier is usually not worth working with.
  • The minimum acceptable wholesale margin is about 50%. A wholesale cost above half of MAP should be negotiated or reconsidered.
  • Judge a discount by the resulting cost of goods, not by the percentage alone. A 50% discount can be acceptable, while 20-40% off usually is not.
  • Off-MSRP example: a supplier offering 50% off MSRP while MAP is 25% off MSRP leaves too little margin. The deal only works if competitors sell at MSRP.

Net profit and ad-spend thresholds

2 min

Worked profit example at 2x

  • Wholesale $800, sale price $1,000: gross profit $200. Minus 2.9% fees ($29) gives $171. Minus 10% customer acquisition cost ($100) gives $71 profit. Subtracting shipping and operational overhead leaves minimal or negative margin.

  • Solution: price at $1,600 instead, and drive only organic and remarketing traffic, not paid ads.

  • Transaction fees (2.9%) and customer acquisition (up to 10% of sale) are the costs that a 2x MAP has to cover.

Dollar profit matters as much as percentage

  • A wholesale price of $161 against a MAP of $230 leaves too little dollar profit per sale to justify advertising. Accepting a lower spread is only reasonable for very high-ticket products worth thousands of dollars, where gross profit per unit is still large.
  • A lower spread is acceptable only when the dollar profit per sale still makes the sale worthwhile.
  • Established industry suppliers offered about $125 profit on a $1,000 product and about $200 on a $4,000 product, which can be too thin for paid ads.
  • Do not sell products at very low margins, such as 5% or a flat $50 per sale. This is especially true for suppliers without a price policy, where other retailers race to the bottom. These deals create more problems than sales.
  • Products sold below 25% margin including shipping should not be added to the store. Keep the supplier on file for later.

Product-level margin checks

  • Product-only margin in the example was roughly 20% once outbound shipping was removed, showing that shipping was the main reason the margin was low.
  • Outbound shipping in the example cost about $98,000 to $100,000 per year, roughly 6.3% of total income. It is one of the largest levers for improving margin.
  • Check each product's margin including shipping before listing it. Add only products with at least 25% margin including shipping.
  • Small, lightweight products have low fulfilment costs because they ship in small packages. This leaves room for a good selling price and margin, so use it when choosing test products.
  • Outbound shipping cost in one case was about $98,000 per year; the owner planned to bring it to about $75,000, a saving of about $23,000 per year, while keeping the rest of the cost structure. This was the third of three supplier priorities.

Margin review steps

  1. Contact the supplier and build the relationship before asking for terms.
  2. Request the supplier's current pricing sheet.
  3. Calculate the margin for each product, including shipping.
  4. Add only products with at least 25 percent margin including shipping.
  5. Keep low-margin suppliers on file and note any individual high-margin products they offer.
  6. Keep searching for higher-margin suppliers as the first priority.
  • Products from suppliers with weak sales can stay listed in free Google Shopping listings if they still show margin, so they earn traffic without ad spend.

Evaluating a supplier before listing

5 min

Supplier margin is set per supplier

  • Margins are supplier-specific, not niche-specific. No actual margin is known before approval, because pricing tiers and credit terms differ by supplier. One supplier may offer 50% margin while another in the same niche offers only 25-30%.
  • The profit margin is set by each supplier, not by the niche. Two suppliers in the same niche can offer very different margins, so judge every supplier on its own price list before deciding what to sell.
  • Supplier margins are negotiated per supplier, not per niche. A low 20-30% margin from one supplier does not mean the niche is wrong. Keep adding approved suppliers in the niche.
  • Margin between MAP and wholesale varies by supplier, not by niche. Calculate each supplier's margin after approval rather than assuming an industry margin.
  • Supplier-specific prices, MAP policies and margins are unknown until approval. Use category averages for planning and confirm the real margins after approval.
  • Wholesale cost is not known until you are approved, but you can still plan around it.
  • A supplier's price list is the fastest way to confirm true profit per supplier. Margins can often only be confirmed after approval, but they can be calculated as soon as the price list is received.
  • Approved suppliers typically send an Excel sheet with three columns, or three separate price lists: MSRP, MAP and wholesale. Use these to estimate margins before listing products.

Checking a supplier with no MAP policy

  1. Get the supplier's price list.

  2. Search Google Shopping for several of its products.

  3. Check whether anyone already advertises those products and at what markup.

  4. If competitors mark them up only 10% or 15%, do not list them; this is a no-go.

  5. If there is little competition, become the main seller for those products; if competitors sell them at healthy margins, list them too.

  6. Search the supplier's products in Google Shopping.

  7. Check the prices other sellers use for those products.

  8. If sellers are selling at no margin, skip the supplier.

  9. If sellers still make a healthy margin, sell the products and mark them as ineligible on your price match policy page.

  10. Search the product names on Google Shopping.

  11. Compare the prices other sellers charge.

  12. Drop the product if many sellers price it at almost no margin, because that is a race to the bottom.

  13. If margins hold up, exclude the product from your price match policy before the first sale.

  14. Search for that brand on Google Shopping.

  15. Check what prices competitors list the products at.

  16. If competitors are listing at very low markup (e.g., 5% margin), list the product on your site but only target free/organic traffic (no paid ads).

  17. If competitors have healthy markups, you can list at similar prices and use Google Shopping ads.

  18. Decide ad spend based on competitor pricing and your margin. (2025.)

  • When no MAP policy exists, check the supplier's price list, shipping estimate and competitor prices before listing its products. Compare the expected margin for each brand before committing, since margins can still be strong when the brand approves only selected retailers.

  • Brands that approve retailers selectively, especially those without a MAP policy, can still offer good margins because other retailers also protect their prices. Do not undercut the best competitor price in these cases, because price wars leave no one profitable.

  • Pricing for a brand with no MAP starts from competitor prices. If competitors sell above your planned price, match their price instead of undercutting them, since buyers pay more.

    1. Search Google for a few products of that brand as a normal customer would.
    2. Open Google Shopping and check what competitors list the same products for.
    3. If competitors sell above your planned price, match their price instead of undercutting them, since buyers pay more.
    4. If you cannot find a clear competitor price, set the price at least 2x wholesale.

  • If competitors sell a popular product at only about 1.5x wholesale, you can still list it at 2x wholesale but run no paid ads for it. Let it earn sales from free Google product listings and SEO instead of spending money where you cannot beat the price.

Judging a supplier's position and terms

  • Suppliers that sell direct at MSRP and let retailers sell at MAP are good partners. Avoid brands that sell at MAP themselves or below MAP, because this removes your price advantage.
  • If a brand sells direct at MAP, look for a unique value proposition (UVP), such as better presentation, trust or customer experience, that sets your store apart. Skip the brand if its own site already sells well and it expects you to match or beat its price.
  • Retail margins are usually the same for every reseller, so negotiate only if the numbers do not work.
  • Supplier prices are usually fixed once you are approved, so negotiation is rarely possible. Focus on the research and the approval process instead.
  • Some manufacturers sell direct at MSRP but allow retailers to sell at MAP, so a retailer can price below MSRP. Get approved, request the price list and confirm the margin before deciding.
  • When a brand is only available through a wholesaler, check the margin after the wholesaler's price and whether you have pricing control. Working through the wholesaler is acceptable when margin and pricing are right.
  • A supplier that sells the same product direct at the same price with free shipping leaves no room for a value proposition. List such products only if the store offers a bonus or better service; otherwise skip the supplier. (Older advice? Undated, 2023.)
  • If a supplier sells direct to consumers and runs its own paid ads, listing its products on your store can still help build credibility when approaching other brands. Do not include them in paid ads, since they are unlikely to become a major earner. (2025.)
  • If the supplier also sells directly to consumers at MAP, check its own retail website before deciding: whether it runs ads and whether the site is built to convert visitors. A supplier that does not invest in its retail channel can still be a profitable partner.
  • Judge a supplier's offer on its own numbers before listing. Approval does not mean every product is worth selling.

Supplier with thin margin after shipping

  • If a supplier's margin is too thin after shipping, drop that supplier's products even after approval. Approval does not mean every product is worth selling.
  • Supplier margins of 10-20% before payment fees and ad costs signal an affiliate-style relationship. Negotiate with these suppliers or skip them in favour of suppliers that meet the 50% wholesale rule.

Negotiating better terms

7 min

Timing: when negotiation works

  • In the early stage, with minimal sales history, you have little negotiating power with suppliers for better rates or terms. Focus optimisation on conversions and product selection. Negotiate more heavily once you have proven sales volume.
  • Wholesale cost is usually not negotiable for a brand-new retailer, even one with a track record in other businesses. Suppliers typically want proof of sales first. Wholesale cost becomes negotiable as your sales volume with that supplier grows.
  • Once a relationship is established, a supplier may give about 10% off wholesale cost. Some suppliers also offer special holiday pricing so the retailer can sell more of their products.
  • Demand comes before cost. Build consistent demand first, for example 20 sales per day consistently and later 50 per day. Inconsistent demand is a bigger problem than supplier cost at an early stage.
  • Validate first, then negotiate once you know the product will scale. Keep a second backup supplier so you can justify the price.

Basic sourcing sequence

  1. Start with CJ Dropshipping for convenience, since it is easy to set up and offers acceptable prices and shipping.
  2. Build consistent demand first, for example 20 sales per day consistently and later 50 per day.
  3. Once demand is steady, contact other suppliers, state your sales volume and ask whether they can beat your current price.
  4. Move the business only if the new supplier matches or beats the price and service.

Negotiation procedures

  1. Ask your current supplier, such as CJ, whether they can lower the price.
  2. Request quotes from several other suppliers for the same product.
  3. Compare the quotes and choose the supplier that gives the best margin and delivery terms.
  • In the shoe example, fulfilment cost per pair was about $13 to $15 after negotiation. That cost still left a healthy margin on a selling price of $40.
  • A jewelry product with a fulfilment cost of about $7 per item allowed a selling price of $30 with a comfortable margin. The margin came from low cost of goods rather than from a high selling price.
  • Check your supplier's best price rather than accepting the first offer, and ask whether the price can go lower once you have scale.

Negotiation procedure with a volume threshold

  1. Obtain the supplier's price list showing wholesale and MAP.
  2. If MAP is not about double the wholesale price, state that the price must be at that level to sell and that otherwise the brand will not be sold.
  3. Ask immediately whether the supplier offers volume-based pricing incentives, for example a discount once a monthly dollar or unit threshold is reached.
  4. Track monthly volume and follow up for the discount once the threshold is reached.

Incentive pricing after approval

  1. Once you are approved, get their price list and upload their products right away.
  2. Ask whether they offer incentive pricing for selling a set number of units per month or for reaching revenue targets.
  3. Ask whether they give discounts off wholesale prices for your account.
  4. Before the busy season (Black Friday, Cyber Monday through Christmas), contact them again to negotiate special seasonal pricing.

Template: incentive pricing request

Hi [Supplier contact name], we are now approved and have uploaded your products to our store at [store domain]. Do you offer any incentive pricing if we sell [number] units per month or reach [revenue target] in revenue? Are there any discounts off wholesale prices that would be available to us, and do you offer special pricing during busy periods such as Black Friday, Cyber Monday and the holiday season? Thank you, [Your name].

Requesting better margins

  • Ask suppliers for a tiered retailer discount structure early in the process. Standard options include a percentage off wholesale or discounts at set sales volumes.
  • Ask whether the supplier offers tiered retailer pricing, such as 20% off wholesale. Ask for discounts at set sales volumes, such as a number of units or sales. Decide to list or advertise the products only after you see the final margins.
  • A supplier can be asked for better margins with a specific framing: say you want to promote their products and spend more on ads, and ask about incentive pricing. Start with friendly questions rather than an immediate request for discounts.
  • Before running ads on a supplier offering a 15-20% discount off retail, ask for better margins first. Some stores have negotiated better margins with suppliers, so a low spread can sometimes be improved.
  • When a supplier adds a drop-ship fee, shipping and taxes that erode your margin, negotiate first. If prices cannot change, exclude that supplier from paid ads, because organic sales still earn margin. Remove the supplier if the margin is still too small.
  • Ask whether a supplier offers a value-for-volume deal. Ask for better wholesale prices, incentive or volume programmes, and whether a retailer hitting a set number of units or sales unlocks lower wholesale prices.
  • Add the question to your early calls: ask suppliers for their tiered retailer discount structure, including volume discounts. Ask for 5 or 10 percent off at set sales levels.

Template: asking for better margins

Hi, this is [Your name] from [Store name]. Now that we have reviewed your price sheets, we want to move a good number of your products. After running the numbers, our margins are lower than we expected, around [current margin]%, and we need closer to [target margin]% to run ads profitably. Do you have other pricing tiers, or is there room to negotiate better margins for us? We would also like product photos and videos we are allowed to use, and we would like to order one sample unit at wholesale price to inspect the quality before we commit.

Template: volume-based discount request

Hi [Supplier name], we have been ordering from you since [month and year] and placed about [total annual order value] in orders over the last twelve months, so we are one of your larger accounts. We would like to discuss better pricing for next year. Could you offer us [5] percent or [10] percent lower prices on [product list or category], and could you send your volume pricing tiers? If we can agree on this, we can commit to about [expected monthly order volume] orders. Thank you, [Your name], [Company name].

Template: incentive programme follow-up (2023)

Hi, this is [your name] from [your brand]. We were looking over the price lists you sent us. We really want to put a real ad budget behind these products and become one of your top retailers, but the margins aren't where we need them to be. Do you have any incentive programs where, if retailers reach a certain number of units or a certain sales volume, wholesale prices get discounted? If not, please let me know so we can decide our next steps.

  • Ask a supplier whether a discount is allowed:

    1. Collect screenshots of competitor coupons or discount pop-ups.
    2. Email the sales rep with the screenshots and ask whether you may do the same.
    3. If there is no reply, follow up briefly to make sure the email was seen.

Excess stock and special offers

  • Suppliers often hold excess or discontinued stock they want to clear quickly, and offer discounts of up to 40% for sellers who promote it. A store that already has traffic can start moving that stock almost immediately by featuring the products or running new ads on them. A brick-and-mortar retailer must place a bulk order, wait for shelf space and then handle the stock before it can sell.
  • When a service provider such as a finisher or waterproofing shop has to market its services itself, you can ask for a discount if you can line up a steady group of customers who need the same service. The provider accepts a lower price because you reduce its acquisition effort and time.

Using the list of suppliers as leverage

  • When competitors sell the same product for less than your supplier price list allows, they most likely have better supplier pricing or better shipping rates. They are not usually selling at a loss.
  • Ask suppliers about the product's price when a competitor is undercutting you. Possible answers include a cheaper carrier or a lower wholesale tier.
  • Email the supplier with your wholesale price, the competitor's price with free shipping and your shipping cost. Ask whether they can offer better pricing or cheaper shipping.

Low-margin handling

3 min

Options when the spread is too thin

The spread is the gap between what you pay and what you sell for.

  1. Contact the supplier to negotiate better wholesale pricing (the price the supplier charges you, before your markup).
  2. If the margin is still acceptable but ad spend is unaffordable, keep the product and rely on organic traffic only (visitors who find you without paid ads).
  3. Otherwise, drop the product.

  • Low-margin products can still be listed without paid campaigns (ads you pay for).

  • Keep them in free Google product listings (free product entries in Google Shopping, Google's price comparison page) for organic traffic.

  • Keep paid campaigns focused on products with the largest spread.

  • If a supplier's margin is too thin, either do not list its products or list them without any paid ads, so only free traffic reaches them.

  • Products with too small a margin to advertise can still be listed on the store and in the free listings section of Google Shopping, with no ad budget.

  • Spend paid traffic only on products from suppliers with healthy margins.

  • Send the full catalogue to free Google product listings.

  • Add suppliers to paid campaigns only after their margins are proven.

  • Non-MAP suppliers (suppliers that do not set a MAP): do not run Google Shopping ads for products where competitors undercut your price on every listing (sell cheaper than you).

  • Invest in free traffic sources instead.

  • Do not expose the products to price-match guarantees.

  • When margins are too thin, ask suppliers whether they have incentive or volume programmes (discounts for reaching a set number of units or sales) that lower wholesale prices.

  • If the answer is no, decide whether to list the brand with no ad spend or skip it entirely.

  • Do not match prices of non-MAP competitors when no margin is available.

  • If the supplier enforces MAP, ask it to contact the competitor.

  • Otherwise keep your price where you can make money, and do not run Google Shopping ads on those products.

  • Do not sell a product at a price below MAP through advertising.

  • If a supplier that does not enforce MAP sells the same product elsewhere, check it has no price-match policy before approaching.

  • If margins are too thin, exclude those products from paid ads and rely on free Google Shopping listings.

Thin margin: a shipping example

  1. Calculate the margin: retail price minus wholesale cost minus shipping. Example: $699 - $530 = $169, but $200 shipping would leave a loss.
  2. Email the supplier with your wholesale price, the competitor's price with free shipping and your shipping cost. Ask whether they can offer better pricing or cheaper shipping.
  3. If the supplier refuses, still list the product if people search for it, but exclude it from paid traffic.
  4. Price the product with your own margin, and exclude it from your price-match guarantee.
  5. Add perceived value (how valuable the customer feels the offer is) with bonuses and shipping upgrades, such as white-glove delivery (premium delivery with setup).
  • A supplier's cost should be checked against its MAP before it is listed.
  • A thin spread should be reconsidered or negotiated (see the 2x and 50% benchmarks above).
  • If a supplier adds a drop-ship fee (an extra charge for shipping from the supplier) or tax that erodes (reduces) margin, first negotiate.
  • If prices cannot change, keep the product on free traffic only.
  • Do not sell at very low margins.
  • Do not advertise products whose spread has become too thin.

Ad versus free-traffic decisions

3 min

Paid versus free listing by margin

  • Spend paid traffic only on products from suppliers with healthy margins.
  • Send the full catalogue to free Google product listings.
  • Keep the paid campaigns focused on products with the largest spread.
  • Products with thin spreads stay in free listings.
  • Products from suppliers with weak sales can remain in free Google Shopping listings if they still show margin, so they earn traffic without ad spend.
  • Keep products with thin margins in free listings only, so that organic traffic can still reach them.

Checking whether a supplier's product is working

  • When a supplier's products get more than 100 unique visitors (different people, each counted once) from paid ads without any sale, shoppers are most likely buying from the supplier directly.
  • Check unique visitors per supplier product before deciding whether to keep advertising it. (2023.)
  • If big retailers ignore a supplier's MAP enforcement, check that supplier's SKUs (stock codes, one per product version) one by one.
  • Advertise only products where big retailers are not selling at a lower price.
  • Filtering this way keeps ad spend on products you can actually sell at MAP. (2023.)

Products that do not sell

  1. First optimise (improve) the product pages and the ads for those products.
  2. If they still do not sell, lower the target cost per click (the most you pay for one click) for them in Google Shopping, so they still show but receive less budget.
  3. If that fails, remove them from your paid alpha and beta campaigns (paid ad campaigns with those names), and let them sell through free Google product listings and organic search.

Competitor price-cutting in paid campaigns

  1. Take screenshots of the violating listings and send them to your supplier.
  2. Watch how the supplier responds. A supplier that acts quickly is the first step.
  3. If the supplier is not helpful, check how your ads convert (turn visitors into sales) for those specific products.
  4. If they do not convert and the spend looks like unfair competition, drastically lower the bids (the amount you offer per click) for those products, rather than removing them from the feed (the product list you send to Google). (2023.)
  • Stop paid ads for a supplier's products when the supplier's margin does not hold after shipping and ad costs.
  • Keep the products on the store for organic traffic.

Ad spend after a supplier discount

  • A supplier discount of 30-35% is not enough if you still pay shipping and must fund customer acquisition (the cost of winning each new buyer).
  • Ask suppliers for better margins, check whether they offer pricing tiers (price levels by order size), and review their terms, exclusivity (whether only you may sell the product) and pricing before deciding which products to fund with paid traffic. (2025.)
  • Products where a 2x MAP (the price is twice the wholesale cost) leaves room are run in ads only if the remaining profit covers acquisition.
  • A 2x MAP at 10% customer acquisition still needs the rest of the costs covered.

Ad decision by supplier type

  • Suppliers that sell direct to consumers and undercut your price while running ads are hard to beat.
  • Keep them on the store without ads and rely on organic traffic.
  • Advertise products where your store adds value. (2023.)
  • For a product where competitors undercut your price, bid lower and run no ads on those products instead of matching their price.

Direct-to-consumer undercutting

1 min

Direct-to-consumer means the brand sells straight to shoppers, not only through shops.

Price-match guarantees, coupons and advertised price

2 min

A price-match guarantee is a promise to match a lower price. A coupon code is a discount code entered at checkout (the last payment step).

  • Coupon codes that reduce price below MAP are generally acceptable, because MAP is the minimum advertised price, not the minimum selling price.
  • Check individual supplier agreements for language forbidding promotional codes.
  • The advertised price (the price shown in ads and emails) must not go below MAP.
  • A coupon code applied at checkout can lower the price the customer pays, because the advertised price stays at MAP.
  • Product-specific discounts that only apply at checkout are generally allowed unless a supplier forbids them. (2023.)
  • Clear coupon mechanisms that only apply at checkout are the only standard exception to not selling below MAP through advertising.
  • MAP is a minimum advertised price, so you may advertise a product above it but not below it.
  • A retailer below MAP is in violation of the supplier's policy. (2025.)
  • Ask whether the supplier has a MAP policy after approval.
  • If it does not, the site should not carry a price guarantee, because it can only be honoured when MAP applies.
  • Do not offer a price-match guarantee on a supplier's product pages if that supplier does not enforce MAP.
  • Otherwise a competitor willing to sell at a loss can force you to match and lose money on every sale.
  • Do not work through a supplier's MAP with a price-match guarantee if the supplier does not use MAP.

Negotiation and supplier handling checklist

1 min

Use this order when a new supplier is added. A supplier is the company that ships the products for you.

  1. Get the price list from the approved supplier and upload its products.
  2. Confirm whether a MAP policy exists. MAP (Minimum Advertised Price) is the lowest price the brand lets you advertise. Also confirm whether the supplier enforces it, meaning it checks that sellers follow it.
  3. Calculate the margin for each product. Margin is the money you keep from each sale. Include shipping, packaging, 3PL pick fees and payment fees. A 3PL is an outside warehouse that packs and ships orders for you. A pick fee is the charge per order for that work. Payment fees are the charges for taking card payments.
  4. Compare the spread against the 50% rule and the 2x floor. The spread is the gap between MAP and the wholesale cost, which is the price you pay the supplier. The 2x floor means the price should be at least twice the wholesale cost.
  5. Decide for each product: paid, free-only or dropped. "Paid" means you run paid ads for it. "Free-only" means you list it with no ads and rely on organic traffic, which is free visitors from search and social media. "Dropped" means you remove it.
  6. Ask for better margins, incentive pricing and volume tiers. Incentive pricing is a special lower price from the supplier. Volume tiers are prices that drop as you buy more.
  7. Renegotiate once your sales volume is proven.

Priorities and sequencing

1 min
  • In the early stage, focus on conversions and product selection. A conversion is a visitor who buys. Negotiating with suppliers gets easier once you have proven sales volume.
  • Build steady demand before you chase cost savings. Once steady sales are proven, negotiate supplier prices.
  • Keep a backup supplier so you can justify the price. Switch only when the new supplier matches or beats price and service.

Checks before listing a product

1 min

Set up each supplier separately

1 min
  • Pricing rules, margins and MAP requirements belong to each supplier. They do not belong to the niche. A niche is a specific market you sell into.
  • One low-margin supplier does not rule out a niche. This is true if other suppliers offer the 50%-of-MAP structure.
  • Margins are set per supplier. They can differ within one store. Some suppliers offer 15%, others 20% or 30%. So track margin per supplier, not one store-wide figure.
  • Individual products from one supplier can also have different margins.
  • MAP requirements differ from brand to brand. Run the 2x wholesale check for each supplier, not once across the catalog. The catalog is your full product list.
  • If a brand's MAP is too close to its wholesale cost, negotiate better pricing before listing its products.

Check the pricing rules before discounting

1 min
  • Ask for the MAP policy before you discount. Many suppliers allow discounts, but some forbid coupon codes. An automatic coupon can break the policy if it pushes the advertised price below MAP.
  • Extra discounts or coupon codes on top of MAP are allowed. The exception is when the supplier's retailer approval agreement forbids them. That is the contract a brand uses to approve sellers.
  • This restriction is rare. If a supplier has it, exclude that brand from promotions.
  • To find a brand's MAP without asking, search the brand name on Google Shopping. Google Shopping is Google's price comparison page. Compare the prices sellers list for its products.
  • Ask the supplier directly only if this does not reveal the price.
  • If a supplier's listed online price is below its MAP, ask the supplier about it immediately. Do not assume MAP is being enforced.

The 50% rule (wholesale against MAP)

1 min
  • Wholesale cost should be no more than 50% of MAP. Example: a MAP of 5,000 USD requires a wholesale cost of 2,500 USD or less.
  • The spread must cover shipping, payment fees, ad spend and profit. Ad spend is what you pay for ads.
  • A wider spread leaves more room for these costs.
  • Exceptions are rare. They are only considered for very expensive items that sell consistently and easily.
  • MAP is often around 2x the wholesale cost.

Profit after product cost

1 min

Formula:

Profit after product cost = MAP − wholesale cost − average shipping cost − payment fees (2.9% for Shopify Payments) − other fees

Shopify Payments is Shopify's built-in payment service. "Other fees" means any extra costs not listed.

  • Example: MAP $1,000, wholesale $650 and shipping $100 leave $250 before fees.
  • In that example, roughly $210 is left after the fees. That number may be lower.
  • Arithmetic check: 2.9% of $1,000 is $29. That alone would leave about $221 from $250.
  • The source's roughly $210 implies further fees that it does not name.
  • Use the full cost list in and recalculate per product.

Estimate COGS for bookkeeping

1 min
  • Estimate COGS as 100% minus your gross margin. For example, a 20% margin gives 80% COGS.
  • Base the estimate on the last six months of your P&L, which is your profit and loss statement.
  • Add a small buffer, meaning extra money set aside, so the COGS account never runs dry.
  • The result is an estimate. It is close enough to pay suppliers without checking each invoice to the cent.

Net profit targets

1 min

  • Target a net profit of 30 percent per sale. Never go below 20 percent.

  • Accept 20 percent only for competitive products that need heavy ad spend, or for large bulk orders. Bulk means buying or selling in large amounts.

  • Reseller deals (deals where you buy to resell) should leave at least 20% net profit after all costs. That is the minimum.

  • The actual deal needs a larger margin than that. Ads and credit card processing fees come out of it.

  • Formula for net profit per sale:

    Net profit per sale = MAP − wholesale − shipping − ad spend per sale

  • Example with round numbers: 1,000 USD minus 500 USD wholesale, 100 USD free shipping and 100 USD ad spend leaves 300 USD net.

  • That equals a 30 percent net margin.

  • This is not the same as the markup over wholesale. Markup is the extra on top of your cost.

  • Use the gross margin target for early screening, before shipping, fees and ads.

  • Use the net profit target for the decision on each sale.

  • The sources give these as different measures. They do not explain how the two fit together.

Shipping in the margin

1 min

Judge shipping against price

1 min
  • Judge shipping cost against the product's sale price and average order value. Average order value is the average amount a customer spends per order.
  • Do not judge shipping by weight alone. An $800 shipping cost on a $5,000 product can still leave about $1,500 net profit.
  • The same $800 on a $1,200 product destroys the margin.
  • Example: two dining tables of the same size and weight cost $300 on average to ship. At $500 retail, the $300 shipping makes the product unworkable.
  • At $1,500 retail, the same shipping cost is absorbable.
  • Shipping is judged relative to the product price, even within one industry.
  • Example: a $1,000 product with about $500 gross margin and shipping of up to $300 leaves only about $200 for ads.
  • Bringing shipping down to about $200 frees up enough money for ad costs and profit.
  • Targeting customers in states close to the supplier is one way to lower shipping.
  • Shipping costs vary widely by destination. Remote states such as Montana or Idaho can be far more expensive to ship to.
  • For a large item, a shipping cost of $450 instead of $250 can push a product into the danger zone. The danger zone is where a product loses money or barely earns any.

Estimate shipping without exact quotes

1 min
  • Shipping costs depend on origin (where it ships from), destination, package size, weight and the carrier (the shipping company) used.
  • You do not need exact numbers for every product at the start.
  • If shipping makes low-price items unprofitable, sell only the higher-priced items in that range.
  • Shipping can be quoted or estimated from the average shipping cost for each niche, when those averages are known.
  • Quotes are only needed when shipping costs are unknown.
  • Estimate shipping as follows.
    1. Ask your supplier what it generally costs to ship an item from its location to your target regions.
    2. Do not aim for exact figures. The cost varies per order unless the supplier offers flat-rate shipping, which is one fixed price per order.
    3. Estimate a typical shipping cost for each product, and compare item A with item B.
    4. Enter the estimate in the supplier shipping cost field and adjust it once real orders come in.

1 min

Hi [Supplier contact name], we have recently been running our numbers. To run ads to your products profitably, we cannot sell at MAP and still keep a profit. Is there any incentive or deal you could offer on the wholesale price for [product or SKU names]? Thank you, [Your name], [Store name].

Low-margin handling

1 min

Decide what to do with a thin product

1 min

A thin product is one with little margin left.

Procedure when a supplier's margin is too low to fund ads (2023):

  1. Ask the supplier if it has pricing incentives or different pricing tiers.
  2. If nothing changes, list the products on the store without running ads for them and rely on organic traffic.
  3. Only keep them if the margin is enough for the products to be worth the time.

Procedure when a product has negative net after shipping (2024):

  1. Ask the supplier for a better wholesale price.
  2. If the supplier says this is their best price, exclude that product from paid ads.
  3. Add other suppliers who can offer better margins, so the budget goes to winning products.

Procedure when a supplier's product is priced below your margin needs (2021):

  1. Ask for better wholesale pricing first.
  2. If they refuse and the margin is too thin for ads, keep the product at MAP and rely on organic traffic without including it in Google Shopping, or drop it.
  • If a supplier gives a pricing incentive, the question is whether margins support ad spend. (2023)
  • If a supplier refuses any change in price structure, and competitors list at a 10 percent markup over the supplier price, it is often not worth listing. (2023)
  • For a very poor supplier where little margin remains, either skip the products entirely or list them above MAP with no paid ads.
  • Paid ads should be reserved for products with enough margin to cover the ad cost.
  • Case: a seller had large, heavy products whose shipping cost wiped out the margin at MAP. The case and fix are in the shipping section above.

Ads or free traffic

2 min
  • If the remaining margin (the money you keep from a sale after costs) covers a sale at MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price the brand lets you advertise) but not paid ads (ads you pay for), exclude those products from Google Ads and keep them listed.
  • Those products can still earn free Google product listing (a free product listing in Google Shopping) and organic traffic (free visitors who find you without ads).
  • Reserve paid ads for products with enough margin to cover the ad cost.
  • Before you run ads on a product, confirm the target price covers the average cost per purchase (the average ad cost to get one sale).
  • For TikTok ads, the source sets 3x landed cost as the minimum sale price. Landed cost is the total cost to get the product to the customer, including product, shipping and fees.
  • Target product margin before running ads, so that sales are profitable and scalable (able to grow without losing money).
  • When the supplier's margin is too low, follow this order:
    1. Ask the supplier for incentives (deals, discounts or better prices).
    2. If that fails, list the product without ads and rely on organic traffic.
    3. Keep the product only if the margin is worth the time.
  • If MAP enforcement (the brand's effort to make sellers follow MAP) fails after escalating up the supplier's chain of command (the order of managers you contact, from the rep upward), stop paid ads for that brand.
  • Keep the products on the store for organic traffic only if the brand's MAP policy (its written pricing rules) allows it.
  • If a product has negative net after shipping (you lose money once shipping is paid) and the supplier cannot improve the price, exclude it from paid ads.
  • Move that budget to suppliers with better margins.
  • Before you pause ads on a product whose competitor is violating MAP, search the competitor's name online and read the reviews. (2023)
  • If reviews show poor customer service, late shipping or payment problems, the violator is likely to lose customers. In that case, it can be worth keeping ads running.

Direct-to-consumer undercutting and competitor prices

1 min
  • Direct-to-consumer means the brand sells straight to customers. Undercutting means selling for less than others.
  • Supplier sales and temporary discounts can be listed on your store. You do not need to worry about undercutting other suppliers. (2023)
  • Contact brands around the holidays to ask if they will offer deals.
  • Your store can promote those discounts with graphics and emails.
  • Clearance items (old stock sold cheaply to clear it out) are typically exempt from MAP compliance.
  • So a competitor selling clearance stock below MAP is not automatically a violation.
  • If a supplier starts selling its best-selling products below your dealer cost (the price you pay the supplier) or discounts them heavily on its own site, pause those products.
  • Do not spend ad money on them until the supplier explains and corrects the pricing.
  • Some suppliers sell at a price so close to cost that a MAP violator cannot sustain it.
  • Such retailers tend to get bad reputations, so check their reviews and customer complaints before reacting to their price. (2023)
  • A retailer that sells below wholesale price (the price brands charge stores) is very likely a scam or burner store (a fake store that takes orders and money but never ships).
  • Do not match such prices. Build trust with your own store through reviews instead. (2024)

MAP violation monitoring and reporting

1 min

Find the violation

1 min
  • Testing whether a competitor violates MAP on Google Shopping (Google's price comparison page) (2023):
    1. Search the product on Google Shopping.
    2. Read the prices shown by the listings, including the free listings from three stores.
    3. If most listings show one price and one or two show a much lower price, the most common price is probably MAP and the lower one is a violation.
  • Search the brand name and SKU (the product's stock code) in Google to find competitors selling below the minimum advertised price. (undated)
  • Copy the links to the competitor listings.
  • Expect manufacturers to use software to monitor MAP compliance.
  • Large retailers are less often penalized than smaller ones.
  • A distribution center (the supplier's warehouse) that warns you about not updating prices on time is a good sign. It means they care about you maximizing profit. (undated)
  • Keep your product prices current in your store to avoid these warnings.

Report the violation

1 min
  • When a supplier's MAP is known and a competitor sells below it, report the violation to the supplier right away with a screenshot. (2024)
  • Ask whether the competitor has a special deal. Ask whether the supplier will contact them.
  • Good suppliers enforce MAP and may remove an unauthorized retailer as an authorized dealer (a store officially approved by the brand).
  • Handling a MAP violation by a competitor (2021):
    1. Screenshot the competitor's product page showing the low price.
    2. Search the product in Google Shopping and screenshot the listing showing the same lower price.
    3. Email the supplier contact with the competitor URL, both screenshots and a request to correct the pricing.
    4. If the price is not updated within a few days, reply to the same thread (the same email conversation).
    5. Most suppliers fix pricing quickly.
    6. Amazon and Walmart listings below MAP can be reported to the supplier the same way.
  • Reporting procedure:
    1. Report every MAP violator to the supplier.
    2. Follow up with the supplier regularly.
    3. If nothing changes after about one to two months, stop selling that supplier's products and stop spending ad budget on them.
    4. Take screenshots of competitors selling below your cost, send them to the supplier, and ask whether a better pricing tier or incentive is available.
  • Send the supplier screenshots of the Google Shopping listing and the competitor's website.
  • A good supplier enforces MAP and responds within the number of days stated in the MAP policy.
  • If nothing changes, stop paid ads for that brand. Then either keep listings at MAP for organic traffic, or remove the supplier.
  • Send the supplier links to the competitor listings. (undated)
  • The supplier can then contact the violating retailer through its representatives.

Escalation and when to stop

1 min
  • If MAP enforcement fails after escalating up the supplier's chain of command, stop paid ads for that brand.
  • Keep the products on the store for organic traffic only if the MAP policy allows it.
  • Sources differ on timing.
  • The 2019 procedure stops selling after about one to two months without change.
  • The 2021 procedure responds to the number of days in the MAP policy, and then stops paid ads.
  • Use the timing in the supplier's MAP policy where it exists.
  • Where it does not exist, use the one to two month limit.

Supplier invoices and cash reserve

1 min
  • Not tracking exactly what you owe suppliers for each order leads to unexpected supplier bills (invoices).
  • Allocating a COGS percentage (the share of each sale that covers cost of goods sold, meaning what the products cost you) of every sale into a dedicated account gives a reserve that covers these invoices.
  • Case: a high-ticket dropshipper (someone who sells expensive products that the supplier ships directly to the customer) had just started out. Sales and profit were rising, and the owner believed the business was close to breaking even.
  • After several months, a supplier sent an unexpected bill of 10,000 to 15,000 dollars.
  • The dropshipper had believed all supplier orders were paid, but they were not. The owner had to borrow money from friends and family to cover the bill.
  • Allocate the COGS percentage from into a dedicated account from each sale, so that supplier invoices are covered.
