Playbook › 3. Suppliers and fulfillment

Scaling supplier base and business continuity

Section 3.21 · 21 of 22 in this chapter · 14 min read

In short

  • Going live is only the start. Keep finding new suppliers all the time.
  • Do not depend on one supplier. If it stops, your store stops too.
  • Before you stop selling a product, check by email whether the supplier still answers and ships.
  • Check where each product is made. Tariffs (import taxes) depend on it.
  • Before you sell a store, reduce its dependence on one supplier.

Continuous Supplier Outreach After Launch

2 min
  • Going live is not the finish line. Keep reaching out to new suppliers. Do not stop at the first handful. A store with only five authorized suppliers (brands that officially approve you to sell their products) is a starting point, not the target.
  • Outreach (contacting suppliers to ask them to work with you) is ongoing growth work. Never stop it once the store is live. A single new supplier can change the business a lot.
  • Settling for a few authorized suppliers because the store already works is a mistake. Keep expanding. Expansion is what lifts revenue (total sales money).
  • Stopping outreach after the first handful of authorized brands and some traffic (visitors to your store) is a common mistake. Ongoing outreach turns a small catalog (all the products you sell) into a scalable one. Keep the weekly routine running.
  • Letting product collections (groups of products on your store) stop growing after launch keeps traffic low and limits sales. Treat supplier outreach and product additions as an ongoing task. **
  • Do not wait until every approval in a niche (one specific market) is secured before going live. Contact new suppliers continuously after launch. Outreach, follow-ups and product uploads never end.
  • The supplier search is never finished. Keep looking for new suppliers. Keep applying to the remaining ones, even after the first five approvals.
  • Ongoing supplier work never ends. Add every new product a supplier launches to your store too. Supplier catalog maintenance (keeping your product list up to date with each supplier) is a continuous process. **
  • Keep adding new suppliers in the background during the ad phase. Do this even after you launch paid ads (ads you pay for) with your initial suppliers.
  • The long-term goal is authorized retailer status (an approved seller of the brand) with every supplier. In the early stage, the goal is to be approved by as many suppliers as possible.
  • Supplier outreach never ends. Aim to be authorized with every supplier in your niche.

Recurring Outreach Routine

1 min

Put outreach in your calendar as a repeating block (a set time slot that comes back every week).

  • Once per week, for 30 minutes to 2 hours, Monday through Friday.

Run the routine in this order:

  1. Contact suppliers on the master list (your main list of suppliers) who have not been reached yet.
  2. Follow up with suppliers who did not respond.
  3. Follow up with suppliers who said "not right now."
  4. Record in the supplier notes: follow-up timelines, traffic or revenue requirements, and any special conditions.
  5. When a supplier approves, complete their authorization paperwork.
  6. Upload that supplier's products to the store.
  7. Return to the master supplier list and continue outreach.

Expanding the Supplier List

1 min
  • Adding new suppliers is one of the fastest ways to grow a store. A single strong supplier with many in-demand products can change sales a lot.
  • Before starting from scratch in a new niche, first add more products from suppliers you already have.
  • Check whether competitors have uploaded new collections or products from your suppliers. Uploading new products before a competitor does lets you capture those sales first.
  • Upload first the suppliers whose products are most similar to those already on your store. Then you have replacement options when one supplier fails.

Expanding a US Manufacturer into a Smaller European Market

1 min

To expand a US manufacturer's product (a company that makes products in the US) into Europe from a small country:

  1. Calculate whether shipping directly from the US to the target market is cost-effective (worth the money). Include product cost, shipping and duties (import taxes).
  2. If it is not, research local fulfillment centers (warehouses that store and ship orders) in the target country. Get their rates for storing and shipping products.
  3. Contact the manufacturer with a volume estimate (how many units you expect to sell) and the fulfillment rates. Offer to ship from the local center.
  4. If talks go well, ask for exclusive distribution (the sole right to sell in the region).

Supplier Risk During Disruptions

1 min

Diversification

1 min
  • Work with several suppliers rather than only one. A single supplier can become unavailable through illness, closure or other disruption.
  • Diversifying (spreading out your suppliers) protects the business from losing its whole catalog. **
  • Depending on only a few suppliers is risky in a shortage. If half of 20 suppliers have stock problems, the remaining ones absorb all demand (take in all the customer orders).
  • Build a broader supplier base before a crisis. Include suppliers you have not yet pushed sales for (not yet actively sold products from).
  • Suppliers that were weak before a supply crisis can become top suppliers afterward. Form supplier relationships early.
  • Do not write off suppliers you have not yet pushed sales for.
  • Keep multiple backup suppliers or fulfillment providers (companies that pack and ship orders for you).
  • Backups protect against supplier failure. They let you compare prices and quality. They also ensure after-sales support such as return and refund policies.
  • Keep a backup manufacturer so a single supplier failure does not stop production.
  • This matters most for regulated products (products with legal rules), such as skincare that needs certifications (official proof that a product meets the rules).
  • Do not rely on a single traffic source such as Google Shopping (Google's product ads).
  • If Google policies (Google's rules) change, Merchant Center access (your Google account for product listings) is lost, or costs rise, a single-source business can lose revenue overnight.
  • Build a network of profitable traffic sources so the loss of one is absorbable. **
  • Relying on one supplier for most of a store's sales is a concentration risk (too much depends on one thing). That supplier's silence stops the whole order flow.
  • Get authorized with several suppliers in the niche early, even while one supplier is carrying the sales.

Supply Shortages and Market Conditions

1 min
  • Chinese COVID-related lockdowns (government shutdowns caused by COVID-19) in early 2023 slowed shipping. A seller cannot control this.
  • Reducing this risk means working with suppliers in Europe, the US or other regions outside China.
  • In early 2023, US-China trade tensions had not yet affected drop shipping (selling products that a supplier ships directly to your customer).
  • Experienced suppliers keep sellers informed about freight (cargo shipping) and shipping-policy changes in China. Ask your supplier for these updates rather than relying on general news.
  • During the April 2020 COVID-19 period, supplier warehouses in closed states operated with severe restrictions (e.g., 5 people maximum per building, working every other day).
  • This caused email response delays, but communication did not stop. **
  • In 2022, fitness niches were the most affected by supply chain shortages (not enough products moving from makers to sellers).
  • The expectation given was that supply would level out within several quarters (several three-month periods). **

Supplier Goes Silent or Stops Shipping

1 min

Handle a supplier who has gone silent or is not shipping as follows:

  1. Email the supplier. Check whether they still answer and process orders. A dead phone line (a phone number that no longer works) alone does not prove they have stopped.
  2. If they still respond and ship, keep selling their products.
  3. If they do not respond or ship orders weeks late, stop selling their products. Do this even when those products are profitable.
  4. Log every order with the date it was sent to the supplier and whether it has shipped. Follow up with the supplier on unshipped orders.
  5. For open orders the supplier cannot fill, message the customer and offer 2 or 3 in-stock alternatives (products they can buy now). See the customer message template.
  6. Stop all ad spend (money paid for ads) on the affected products immediately.
  7. Turn on inventory tracking (the store counts stock and stops sales at zero) on the Shopify product pages (pages in Shopify, the online store software) of those products.
  8. Keep the products live instead of deleting them. Add the out-of-stock note above the description (a message saying the item is unavailable now), with links to 1 to 3 similar in-stock products.
  9. Email your customers about the change.
  10. Keep onboarding new suppliers (bringing them into your store), starting with those whose products are most similar to the ones already on your store.
  • A supplier whose phone number is out of service can still be reliable if they answer email and keep processing orders. Check this by email before deciding to stop selling their products.
  • Stop selling a supplier's products once they stop responding or ship orders weeks late.
  • Repeated delays damage the business even if each sale is profitable.

Case: Single-Supplier Store Hit by Silence

1 min
  • A store open for about one month had close to 6,000 dollars in sales.
  • All of that money came from a single supplier.
  • The supplier then stopped responding for about two weeks.
  • This left four customer orders unprocessed (not yet sent out).
  • The supplier's phone number no longer worked. Only email could reach them.
  • Attempts to authorize other suppliers to fill the gap did not produce a replacement quickly.
  • The owner had to choose between absorbing the lost orders and offering customers alternatives.

Closing a Supplier That Goes Out of Business or Stops Responding

1 min

When a supplier closes or stops responding:

  1. Keep the products that still sell and are still available for a short time.
  2. Stop using the products whose supplier is no longer responding. Phase them out (remove them slowly) as new supplier approvals come in.
  3. Keep images used on the blog, collections or homepage sliders (the rotating pictures at the top of the home page) if the supplier is closing. Do not sell products that are no longer available.

Tariffs and Cost Changes

1 min

Tariff Rates and Timing

1 min
  • Proposed US tariffs (import taxes) at the time this advice was written were up to 60% on goods with China as country of origin (the country where the product was made).
  • Other countries faced a proposed 10 to 20%.
  • These were planned changes, not yet in effect.
Country of origin Proposed rate (at time this advice was written) Status
China Up to 60% Planned, not yet in effect
Other countries 10 to 20% Planned, not yet in effect
Made in USA Not tariffed under the planned rules

Managing Exposure

1 min
  • Record the country of origin of every product you sell. Do this even when the supplier is US-based.
  • Many US-supplied products are made overseas. Country of origin determines the tariff exposure (how much tariff risk) of each product.
  • When tariffs are expected, move ad budget (the money you plan to spend on ads) toward products whose country of origin is not China.
  • Look for more suppliers with Made in USA products. The planned rules did not tariff those.
  • Suppliers with China-origin products are not cut off. But their ad spend is reduced relative to other products.
  • To reduce exposure to tariffs, search for "[niche] made in USA" to find domestic brands (brands made in the US).
  • These are usually more expensive. But their prices are less affected by import tariffs.

Price Pass-Through

1 min
  • Tariffs mainly affect suppliers who import from China. They often pass added costs on by raising wholesale prices (the price suppliers charge stores).
  • When wholesale rises, MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price you may advertise) rises too.
  • So the new MAP is set to the new cost structure.
  • Tariff-driven supplier surcharges (extra fees caused by tariffs) usually pass through to the advertised price.
  • The reason: the minimum advertised price is typically a multiple of wholesale cost (a set number of times the supplier's price).
  • Price increases affect all sellers of the same brand equally.
  • Keep operating as usual until a supplier actually announces a price increase.

Supplier Price Increases and Margin Pressure

1 min
  • Supplier price increases can come with a MAP increase and a cut in the retailer's margin (the profit left as a share of the price).
  • For example, margin can drop from about 20 percent to about 10 percent.
  • Watch for suppliers who both raise cost and sell directly to consumers. This signals reduced dependence on resellers (stores that buy and resell). **
  • Case: a store about one and a half years old had built almost all of its traffic through Google Ads (Google's paid ads).
  • It had only started organic traffic (free visitors from search) that year.
  • Its largest supplier accounted for roughly 80 percent of sales.
  • The supplier raised sale prices because of higher shipping costs.
  • It also raised the MAP and increased the store's cost at the same time.
  • So the store's margin dropped from about 20 percent to about 10 percent.
  • The supplier also started selling directly on its own website and on Amazon. This reduced its need for resellers.
  • The response discussed was threefold (three parts):
    • Keep adding suppliers over time.
    • Lower Google Ads bids (the amount you pay per click) as an interim measure (a temporary step). Still aim for at least break-even sales (no profit, no loss).
    • Build bundles (several products sold together as one package) on the low-margin products where a supporting product is naturally bought alongside the main one. A bundle can sell to only part of the buyers.

Selling or Transferring a Store with Supplier Dependency

1 min

Reducing Dependency Before a Sale

1 min
  • Avoid a single-supplier dependency before selling.
  • A store that relies on one exclusive supplier (a supplier that works only with that store) is seen as high-risk by buyers.
  • Set up a second supplier. Place a few real orders with them at similar prices.
  • Transfer that backup relationship to the buyer as part of the sale.
  • Keep at least two alternate suppliers available before a sale, even if you do not use them.
  • Buyers dislike single-supplier risk. That risk could come from a supplier raising prices or cutting you off.
  • Supplier relationships that take time to build and negotiate act as a moat (an advantage that is hard to copy).
  • They protect a store from being copied quickly.
  • A store that can be set up in minutes by picking a product from AliExpress (an online marketplace for cheap goods) has little moat.
  • Spread revenue across several suppliers to reduce risk.
  • A store relying on one supplier is less attractive. A supplier problem can halt its earnings.

Conflicts of Interest

1 min
  • If a store's supplier is also a seller of the same products, or is connected to the seller, buyers see conflict-of-interest risk (a clash between the owner's interests and the buyer's).
  • Buyers may then pay less or walk away.
  • Wholesale (selling in bulk to other sellers) and retail (selling to shoppers) run by the same owner should be separated.
  • A wholesale operation and a retail operation supplied by the same owner should be separated into two businesses.
  • Owning both the supplier and the store, and giving yourself a sweetheart price (an unusually cheap deal for yourself), is a risky pattern.
  • The owner can raise prices after the sale. Buyers watch for this.

Contracts, Transferability and Due Diligence

1 min
  • Buyers should only buy a store that depends on a single supplier if the supplier agrees in a contract to keep selling to the new owner.
  • Supplier relationships are a key part of due diligence (the buyer's careful checking before buying).
  • The buyer carries the responsibility for checking that the supplier relationship will continue after the sale.
  • Special supplier or affiliate deals (partner deals, such as discounts) must be transferable (able to pass to the new owner).
  • If a new owner starts with a new account and gets worse rates, the site's value falls.
  • Exclusive supplier relationships are a major benefit for valuation (how much the store is worth) when the store has them.
  • A store with 18 suppliers, some with exclusive relationships, was a strong example of diversification (spreading risk across many suppliers).
  • A supplier may stop working with a store on the day the store changes owners.
  • Before paying for an existing store, confirm in writing that supplier accounts and agreements transfer to the new owner. **

Briefing Suppliers Before a Sale

1 min
  • Brief suppliers about the sale early, since they know the relationship best.
  • A supplier who refuses to deal with a new owner can block the migration (moving the store's supplier accounts to the new owner).
  • Ask suppliers whether they will keep the same terms for the buyer.
  • Or will they require cash payment for the first six months?

Delegating Non-Relationship Tasks

1 min

What to Hand Off and When

1 min
  • An unimportant task does not have to be avoided. But it does not need to be done by the owner.
  • When it is costly or time-consuming, either shorten the time allotted in the daily to-do list or outsource it (hire someone outside to do it).
  • Outsourcing only makes sense when it is financially viable (affordable and worth it) at that stage. **
  • Hand off outsourced fulfillment (ordering products from suppliers and sending orders) only once the store has a track record (a history of results).
  • In the beginning, one person can often handle both support and order processing. **

Team Structure by Revenue Stage

1 min
  • A small high-ticket dropshipping store (selling expensive items that suppliers ship) typically needs at least two regular VAs (virtual assistants, remote helpers you pay).
  • One VA handles customer service and phone calls. Another handles order processing and supplier management.
  • In a very small business, these can be combined into one person.
  • A store in the seven-figure annual revenue range (about $1 million or more per year) usually has two to three full-time VAs.
  • It also uses freelancers (independent workers) for specific tasks. **
Stage Typical team Source
Very small business One person for support and order processing
Small store At least two regular VAs: customer service and calls; order processing and supplier management
About $1M+ per year Two to three full-time VAs plus freelancers for specific tasks

Cost and Tools

1 min
  • Outsourcing a virtual assistant who manages customer service and order fulfillment costs around 500 extra per month.
  • OnlineJobs.ph is a marketplace (a website where you find and hire people) for remote virtual assistants, often from the Philippines.
  • It helps you handle recurring store tasks at lower cost.
  • At $100k-500k per month, a supply chain manager calculates how many units of each SKU (stock keeping unit, one product code for one exact item) to order.
  • This is based on current and forecast sales volume.
  • Expect to pay about $6-15 per hour on Upwork (a freelance marketplace).

Delegating Research Without Delegating Brand Choice

1 min
  • For high-ticket dropshipping, the owner picks the brands to sell.
  • The owner delegates contact lookup, application forms and similar manual research to someone else.
  • Define the research need in advance.

Further details

1 min
  • Adding one new supplier can raise store revenue by roughly 30-50%.
  • It also opens demand that the existing catalog cannot reach.
  • More suppliers means more products. More products means more chances to sell.
