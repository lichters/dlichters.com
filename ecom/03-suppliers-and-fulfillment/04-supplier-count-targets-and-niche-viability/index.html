Supplier count targets and niche viability

Section 3.4 · 4 of 22 in this chapter · 25 min read

In short

  • Aim for at least 20 suppliers per niche (a small product market) before you commit to it.
  • Launch with two approved suppliers and their products uploaded.
  • Keep contacting new suppliers every day, even after the store is live.
  • Reach five approved suppliers before you work on improving the store.
  • Keep adding brands and suppliers over time, aiming for about 20 brands, even after the store is live.
More key points
  • Get five suppliers live on the store before you focus on making visitors buy.
  • If you find too few suppliers, widen the niche (the product group you sell) instead of launching with a small catalog.
  • Never depend on one supplier. Keep contacting new ones all the time.
  • Check shipping cost and whether the market is full of famous brands before you choose a niche.

Why the Supplier Count Matters

1 min
  • Several suppliers (companies that sell you their products) lower your business risk. You do not depend on one vendor (seller). Brand variety also makes buyers trust you more, and it builds store credibility (the store looks trustworthy). Establish this foundation first, because a stronger product base makes conversion optimization (getting more visitors to buy) work much better.
  • Supplier depth (how many suppliers you have) is one of the two main levers (ways to push) for growing revenue (sales money). A dropshipping business (you sell, and the supplier ships the item to the buyer) should secure as many suppliers as possible within its chosen niche.
  • Supplier count matters because not every brand you contact will approve you (say yes to you selling its products). You need enough brands that some saying no still leaves enough products on the site for the market to be viable (able to make money).
  • Multiple supply sources reduce risk, because a supplier can close, have an owner stroke (the owner becomes ill with a stroke), or move on to another project.
  • Expect the 80/20 pattern (about 80 percent of sales come from about 20 percent of suppliers), so keep approaching many suppliers and following up over time.

  • Before committing to a niche, confirm that at least 20 suppliers are available for it.
  • Before choosing a niche, identify at least 20 potential suppliers in your research.
  • Aim for at least 20 different brands or suppliers per niche. If a niche cannot reach 20, niche up a level (broaden it) and add related products to the range.
  • Build a master supplier list (your full list of target brands) of at least 20 brands before outreach begins (before you start contacting suppliers). Then aim to become authorized (get official permission to sell) with every viable brand on that list over the long term.
  • Research at least 20 suppliers before starting outreach, because approval rates (the share of suppliers that say yes) from day one are low for new stores.
  • Build a list of at least 20 different suppliers before you start calling. If one call goes badly, you still have 19 others to approach, so a weak first attempt does not end sourcing (finding products to sell).
  • During niche research, find at least 20 different brands you could work with. Not all will approve on day one, which is acceptable.
  • For the first store, find at least 20 brands or suppliers. Many will not approve, some will have low margins (little profit per sale) or no MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price a brand lets you show), and some may charge for access, so the extra count is needed for a working store.
  • Research should yield at least 20 suppliers, and ideally 50 to 100 or more, to work through.
  • Expect about 20 suppliers per niche as a minimum target, and as many suppliers as meet the criteria if there are more.
  • Plan to find at least 20 suppliers before launching a first store, so enough remain when many of them decline.
  • Find at least 20 potential brands per niche before launching. Confirm they sell through online-only stores without warehouses (buildings where stock is kept), so the niche is drop-ship friendly (the brand ships straight to buyers). Brands that sell through distributors (middlemen who stock and resell goods) with warehouses usually do not drop ship. (2023)
  • Require at least 20 approved brands and at least 100 products per store as a minimum. Expect some suppliers to reject the application, so aim for a cushion (extra room).
  • A store needs at least 100 unique products from at least 20 different suppliers. Each supplier should contribute several products rather than just one.
  • Aim for at least 20 supplier candidates (possible suppliers) early, because some rejections are expected. That gives enough suppliers to launch, build traffic (visitors to the site) and make sales before the rest are approved.
  • Aim for a supplier count of at least 20 brands in a niche during research.
  • Contact at least 20 suppliers, because not every supplier approves a new seller on the first application. Approval standards have not become easier or harder over time.
  • Before testing a niche's competition, source at least 20 suppliers. More suppliers are always better, and 20 is the minimum for the supplier competition test. (2024)
  • Contact at least 20 suppliers to see the range of margin differences, because some will refuse and others will offer better margins. (2024)
  • Aim to get approved with at least 20 suppliers in a niche. Then put paid ad budget (money spent on paid ads) only on products from suppliers that have real margins.
  • A competitor site (another store selling in the same niche) listing 20 or more brands and at least 100 products shows enough variety, even if you do not recognize the brands. Use competitor catalogues (their product lists) to gauge this. (2023)
  • Working toward about 20 suppliers on your list runs alongside the first ads, so keep the list growing while traffic runs. (2023)

  • Smaller markets have fewer suppliers and fewer customers. A lower brand threshold of 10 is accepted because competition is also lower.

Market Size and Warehouse-Heavy Markets

1 min
  • Supplier counts scale with population (the number of people). The US, with roughly 330 to 350 million people, has far more suppliers per niche than smaller markets. Expect to find fewer suppliers for the same product in a small country, and plan your research accordingly.
  • Aim for at least 20 suppliers that sell into the country where you do business. Finding 60 suppliers in the US is more than enough for a niche.
  • In markets where most suppliers keep a warehouse, expect lower approval rates. Contact a larger number of potential suppliers (for example 50 to 60 instead of 20) in the niche. If too few suppliers exist, consider a broader niche to get enough products.
  • For a store in a country outside the US, the main constraint (limit) is usually supplier availability. Check whether enough suppliers exist locally, or whether suppliers in another country will ship into it.
  • US suppliers shipping into Canada is a workable option. Canadian suppliers are the ideal case because they avoid cross-border shipping (sending goods across a country border).

Building the Candidate List and Expected Approval Rates

3 min
  • Build the list of potential suppliers in this order:
    1. Research and compile a master supplier list for all potential suppliers in the niche.
    2. Complete the approval process with the first five suppliers.
  • Limit time spent on research. Keep the list growing, but the goal at this stage is enough suppliers to launch the store. More suppliers can be found later, sometimes when brands contact the seller after seeing it sell in their market.
  • Do not spend weeks perfecting the supplier list. At the start, you only need enough suppliers to stock the site, so you can launch and begin growing sales.
  • Aim for at least 20 suppliers in the list before moving on to the next stage. Once the store is running, more suppliers usually surface on their own, and some contact you after seeing you sell their products.
  • Approval rates (the share of suppliers that say yes) to plan around:
Reference point Value Source
Typical first-stage approval About 50 percent at first, improving over time, with at least 20 suppliers contacted
Decent approval including follow-ups 50 percent is considered decent
New store calling suppliers Just over 60 percent of suppliers called
Warning level Approval rates well below about 5 percent signal a problem with the outreach method, not just bad luck
  • Approval is a numbers game. Expect many rejections. Write down every "reach out again" answer, and follow up in three to six months, sometimes a year.
  • Example from a bike, e-scooter and e-skateboard niche: a list of 30 brands was built from Google Shopping listings (product results on Google search), and each brand was tracked with its phone, email and website. The owner needed only about 10 approved suppliers for a viable start. With about 60 percent approval expected from calls, that meant roughly 19 approvals from 31 suppliers.
  • A single approval is enough to start, so calling many suppliers is worth more than calling a few.
  • A new store may land about 10 suppliers in its first week when outreach is active. Over about six months, one partner added around 30 more suppliers for the first store.
  • Suppliers unfamiliar with dropshipping can take a year or more to approve a new seller. The first months of a store need enough runway (the time and money you have before sales arrive) to wait for approvals. So plan the launch and budget around slow approval, rather than assuming the first application will succeed.
  • Approval is an ongoing process. Keep prospecting (searching for new suppliers) and following up while the first ads run.
  • Keep contacting suppliers while the store is built and running traffic. Store building and supplier outreach can run in parallel (at the same time), so do not wait for a full supplier list before launching.
  • Do not stop supplier approvals when ads start. Get the first two suppliers live, switch on Google ads (paid ads on Google) to create data and sales, and keep adding suppliers and products.
  • Apply for approval with every brand in the niche, including the lower-tier brands (tier means rank level), and do not stop after the first one is approved. More approved brands give more products and more options when some are out of stock.
  • Get approved with every possible supplier in the niche. Then identify standout brands by margin strength and willingness to enforce MAP.
  • Approvals from several brands, even ones that are not the top sellers, add to store credibility. A store with more brands also makes it easier to approach larger brands in the niche later. (2023)
  • Aim to get approved with every supplier and carry every available product in the niche over time.

Growing from the Launch Catalog

2 min
  • Keep applying to more brands (companies that make the products you sell) and uploading their products to your online store as each one approves you. Aim for around 20 brands over time.
  • There is no limit on how many brands you can sell, as long as each brand approves you. Keep applying to new brands while the store is live (open to customers).
  • If you have few approved suppliers (companies that agree to ship orders for you), keep adding brands and products rather than stopping at launch. Adjacent products (products close to your main ones, in the same niche) can add more brands and products. For example, ottomans or end tables next to leather chairs.
  • Set a goal of adding one or two new suppliers per week, even by going back to earlier applicants (companies that already applied).
  • Products from five different suppliers give a usable sample of items and brands to test and build on. This is more useful than relying on a single supplier's catalog (its full product list) when choosing what to sell.
  • Build the first product base in this order:
    1. Get approved by five dropshipping suppliers. (Dropshipping means you sell products, and the supplier ships them straight to the customer.)
    2. Upload the products from those five suppliers to the online store.
    3. Once the approvals are in and the products are live on the store, mark this step as complete before starting conversion work (getting more visitors to buy) on the store.
  • Verify that all products from all five suppliers are live and visible on the store before moving on.
  • Upload products from each of the five approved suppliers to the store.
  • Continue supplier outreach (contacting companies) and approval requests until five suppliers confirm they will work with the store.
  • Your store should have products from at least five different suppliers live before focusing on conversion optimization (making more visitors buy).
  • Once the store is running, more suppliers usually show up on their own.
  • Keep the product catalog on the store as approvals come in. Approval of a supplier alone is not enough to make progress. Only approved suppliers with uploaded products add to the count needed before moving on.
  • Add the rest of the approved suppliers' products once the store is live. Upload all their products before ads go live. Ads are paid placements that show your products to shoppers.
  • Heavy or freight-shipped products (large items sent by truck) priced around $1,500 to $3,000 can be a sound niche if they pass all niche criteria. Such orders can bring roughly $500 to $1,000 profit each. (2020)

When Too Few Suppliers Exist

2 min

  • If you find fewer than 20 brands in your niche, validate viability (test whether it can work) before building.

    1. Call companies directly.
    2. Do not mention wanting to sell their products.
    3. Ask neutrally whether they work with online distributors (businesses that sell products online).
    4. Use their answers to decide whether online distribution is feasible in this niche.

  • To check a market with no visible dropshipping, call the suppliers directly and have a proper conversation about dropshipping. Do not rely on competitor stores (other shops selling the same thing) as evidence. Supplier approval is the test that counts.

  • Some markets have little visible dropshipping because suppliers were not open to it in the past, while many suppliers are now more open. Such markets can be underserved (few shops serve them) and are worth testing.

  • If a niche has only one drop-ship-friendly retailer (a shop that accepts drop shipping), check that it sells at least 20 brands before continuing. Then email from a personal address asking whether it has a warehouse (a building that stores stock) or a showroom (a space where people view products). Proceed if it is online-only. (2023)

  • Count how many stores advertise the most popular products of a niche on Google Shopping (Google's product search and ad page). The competition threshold (the limit that decides pass or fail) in that test is 10 stores. (2024)

  • Research the niche competition: at least 20 suppliers, then count stores advertising the top products on Google Shopping against the threshold of 10.

  • Expect approval rates (the share of companies that say yes) to be low for a new store. In markets where most suppliers keep a warehouse, contact 50 to 60 suppliers instead of 20.

  • If you cannot reach about 10 suppliers after following up for roughly two weeks, move up one niche level (go to a wider product group) to increase the number of products you can offer at launch. (2025)

  • Gold-tier suppliers (the top-level, best-terms suppliers) that run out of stock: one option is to contact more suppliers in the niche before changing niches.

  • Fewer suppliers are acceptable only as a temporary stage in a smaller country, not as the target. (2023)

  • Some stores abroad are built with well under 20 suppliers, but this is an exception.

  • Estimated suppliers in a niche worksheet (a spreadsheet form where you count possible suppliers) is the count of brands across the future competitors you found, not the number of competitor sites. Estimates are rough. If a brand list is long, a rounded figure such as 100 is an acceptable estimate.

  • The count of brands in a Google Shopping check across the product types in the niche must reach at least 10 in total. Procedure:

    1. Set the search to the target country, for example the US market.
    2. Read the seller and brand names in the Google Shopping listings and scroll through the next pages.
    3. Count the potential brands or suppliers. The rule requires at least 10 in total across the product types in the niche.
    4. Open a retailer that sells the product category and note the brands it carries.
    5. Mark the local-brand criterion (one of the pass or fail rules) as yes if the count is met.

Broadening the Niche

1 min
  • If too few suppliers approve you, broaden the niche (widen it to more products) to find more brands and products with better margins (the profit left per sale after costs). This is a fallback, not a day-one move. (2023)
  • If a niche cannot reach 20 suppliers, niche up one level (move to a wider product group) and add related products to the range.
  • When niching up because you found fewer than 20 suppliers, identify an additional 20 to 40 suppliers in related categories to expand the product range.
  • When a store has only a few approved suppliers with low margins or no MAP (a minimum advertised price, the lowest price a seller may show to shoppers), grow the catalog with the steps in the section "Growing from the Launch Catalog."
  • In markets where most suppliers keep a warehouse and too few suppliers exist, consider a broader niche to get enough products.
  • Before choosing a niche, make sure the number of brands is enough. If it is short, broaden rather than launch thin (with too little).
  • Before moving to the niche, confirm the supplier count first. Supplier availability is checked before product count, so the niche can still be widened if the count is short.
  • Expect that some niches will have too few suppliers for the target count. When that happens, the broader niche is the way to reach enough products.
  • Supplier-count thresholds below 20 are a temporary stage, so widen the niche to restore the standard.
  • If a niche cannot reach about 10 suppliers after two weeks of follow-up, move up one niche level. (2025)
  • Brands in a niche should be checked for drop-ship viability before broadening. A niche dominated by household names (brands everyone knows) is not viable, so broadening should not lead there.

Niche Viability Checks

1 min
  • The niche must be drop-ship friendly, meaning suppliers can ship orders directly to the end customer without the store holding stock.
  • Brands that sell through online-only stores are the ones that drop ship. Brands that sell through distributors with warehouses usually do not. (2023)
  • Shipping is often the largest variable cost (a cost that changes with each order) for heavy items. Check shipping rates before choosing a niche. If shipping is charged out of a thin margin, such as a 35 percent margin on a large item, the product is generally not viable, because no budget remains for ads.
  • The best suppliers and brands are known to enthusiasts within the market (people who follow the field closely) but unknown to the general public. Markets dominated by household name brands are not viable for drop shipping.
  • Televisions are not a viable drop shipping product, because the market is dominated by massive international brands such as Sony, Panasonic and LG. Those large companies do not need drop shippers to grow and rarely work with them.
  • Seasonal niches (products that sell only at certain times of the year): for a seasonal niche, for example in Texas with a short winter, start with suppliers for year-round products that sell in all four seasons. Add seasonal products later, as the relevant season approaches. (2025)
  • Most industries see sales spikes around Black Friday, Cyber Monday (big online shopping days) and Christmas. A holiday spike alone is not a reason to reject a niche. Plan around it by confirming with suppliers that stock will last through the peak.
  • Heavy or freight-shipped products priced around $1,500 to $3,000 can be a sound niche if they pass all niche criteria. (2020)
  • Before testing a niche's competition, source at least 20 suppliers.
  • The worksheet's Estimated Suppliers number counts brands, not competitor sites.

Single-Supplier Dependency Risk

2 min
  • Do not depend on one supplier. A single supplier can stop selling to you and remove your whole catalog. Upload the first supplier's products and start ads, but keep contacting new suppliers continuously.
  • Do not start traffic (visitors to your store) with only one brand. Get at least two brands live before turning traffic on. Use the free time to get the rest of the suppliers approved.
  • Working with few suppliers raises business risk sharply. If two of three suppliers close or one raises prices, the store stays open but becomes far less profitable. A large supplier base protects margins (profit per sale) and continuity against losing individual partners. (2023)
  • Multiple supply sources reduce risk. A supplier can close, have an owner suffer a stroke (a serious medical event), or move on to another project. So you need several brands and several suppliers in each niche.
  • Supplier depth (how many suppliers you have) is one of the two primary levers for growing revenue (money coming in). Secure as many suppliers as possible within the niche.
  • Put paid ad budget only on products from suppliers that have real margins. Send MAP suppliers' products into paid ads, and sell the rest through free traffic only.
  • Where a supplier enforces MAP, route its products to paid ads. Use free Google product listings (free product pages on Google) for the rest.
  • Set up Google Merchant Center (Google's tool that holds your product information) and free Google product listings for the products already approved.
  • Build a store with a large supplier base from the start. The larger the base, the less any single partner's exit can damage revenue.
  • Keep the master supplier list (your full list of target suppliers) continuously worked. The long-term goal is authorization (being approved to sell their products) with as close to 100 percent of it as possible.
  • Supplier approval is a numbers game. Expect many rejections, and do not treat any single supplier as the business.
  • Expect the 80/20 pattern: about 80 percent of sales come from about 20 percent of suppliers. Keep approaching many suppliers and following up with them over time.
  • Some operators have built stores with few suppliers, but this carries more risk and should be an exception.
