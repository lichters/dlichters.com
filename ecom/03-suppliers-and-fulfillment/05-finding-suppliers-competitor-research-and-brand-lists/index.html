Finding suppliers: competitor research and brand lists

Section 3.5 · 5 of 22 in this chapter · 37 min read

In short

  • Build your supplier list from brands sold by online-only competitors (stores with no physical shop).
  • Delete any brand that is the competitor's own private label (a brand the store owns and sells under its own name). You cannot buy from it.
  • Enter each brand once, with its website, phone and email. Aim for 20 or more brands.
  • Do not contact suppliers yet, and do not delete duplicates until every competitor is checked.
  • Big brands usually do not drop ship, but their big box store range can still be worth contacting.
More key points
  • Big online stores like Wayfair show you which brands to contact. Search those brands on Google to find more sellers.
  • Call or email each supplier. Write down every date and answer.
  • Find at least 20 suppliers in your niche before you build your store.
  • Do not look for suppliers on Amazon or eBay. Look for brands that sell directly to you.
  • Keep updating your list. New brands appear every year.
  • Build your Master Supplier List (one main list of possible suppliers) before the store is ready.
  • For now, only find and write down suppliers. Do not send applications yet.
  • Ask suppliers for approval only after the website is built.
  • Keep notes on every contact, and keep adding new suppliers.

Column letters (A, B, C ...) refer to the research worksheet from section 2.3.

Set up the working sheets

3 min

Work inside the Niche Research Worksheet (your spreadsheet file for this niche), in the tab "Niche Master Supplier List." (A tab is one sheet inside the spreadsheet file.)

To create the niche's own list, follow these steps:

  1. Right-click the "Niche Master Supplier List" tab.
  2. Select Duplicate (make a copy).
  3. Rename the tab with your niche name, for example "Outdoor Kitchens, Master Supplier List."
  4. Move the new tab next to your Future Competitors sheet.

A second build procedure gives the same sequence in more detail:

  1. Open the niche's master supplier list template, a sheet named "niche master supplier list."
  2. Right-click it, choose Duplicate, then right-click the copy and choose Rename.
  3. Name the copy after the niche, for example "Chandelier Master Supplier List."
  4. Drag each copy next to the matching future competitor list so the sheets stay in order.
  5. Work only on column A (supplier names) in this step. (A column is one vertical line of cells, marked by a letter at the top.)
  6. Open the first future competitor website in split-screen view next to the spreadsheet. (Split screen means two windows side by side on one screen.)
  7. Find the brand list: search the niche category, then open the filters (tools that narrow a product list) and select Brand. On some sites the brand list is in the footer (the bottom of the page) under a "shop all brands" link.
  8. Before copying a brand, open its brand page and confirm it sells products in your niche.
  9. Type each qualifying brand name into column A, skipping brands already on the list.
  10. Repeat for every future competitor website, then repeat the full process for every other niche that is still a winner.

Both build procedures are kept in full. The fuller one is in the section on collecting brands below.

Which columns to fill depends on the stage:

  • Name collection stage: only column A (supplier names) is filled. URL (web address), phone, email, date of contact, contact person and notes stay empty until the supplier-contact stage.
  • Full build: Columns A to D are completed in one pass: Supplier Name, Supplier Website, Supplier Phone Number, and Supplier Email or Contact Form. Columns E, F and G are covered in later steps.
  • Other template: a Master Supplier List spreadsheet can use four required columns: Supplier Name, Supplier URL, Supplier #, and Supplier Email.

A Future Competitors sheet is created in the Niche Research Worksheet. A future competitor is a store in your niche that you will compete with later. The sheet has these columns:

  • Supplier Name
  • Supplier URL
  • Supplier Phone Number
  • Supplier Email
  • Date of Contact
  • Contact Person
  • Notes

To fill it:

  1. Open your Niche Research Worksheet.
  2. Navigate to the [Niche] Future Competitors sheet.
  3. Examine all products your future competitors sell.
  4. Identify which suppliers provide each product.
  5. Enter each supplier with all available information in the tracking columns.
  6. Record every supplier you discover from your competitors.

A Source B tracker uses different columns. Its name-stage fields are Supplier Name, Product Type, Email, Phone Number, Website and Online Application. Phone Call + Date?, Call Comments and Paused come later, in the outreach phase (the stage of contacting suppliers).

Before outreach begins, keep the list ready. For each supplier, gather the website URL, phone number and email address, and put all of it in one document or spreadsheet. Aim for 20 or more potential suppliers in the niche.

Collect brands from future competitor websites

8 min

Before you start, keep in mind that this section takes brands from competitors, not from supplier directories (online lists of suppliers). Build from online-only competitors and from competitors known to dropship. Dropshipping means the store sells the product, but the supplier ships it straight to the customer, so the store keeps no stock.

Use this procedure for a single competitor:

  1. Open the category page (the list of products in one product group) for the niche, for example CNC machines or outdoor kitchens.
  2. Find the brand list in the sidebar filter (a column on the side of the page with filters).
  3. Click show more to expand the full list.
  4. Count every brand. This total is the estimated supplier number that competitor contributes.

Use the building procedure to add brands to the list:

  1. Duplicate the master supplier list template, rename the copy " master supplier list," and place it next to the niche's future competitor sheet.
  2. Open the research sheet on one side of the screen and the competitor's website on the other (split screen).
  3. On the competitor's site, open the exact niche category, not a parent or neighbouring category.
  4. Scroll to the brand section, click "show more," and copy every brand name into column A.
  5. Repeat for each future competitor, adding new brands to the bottom of column A.
  6. Sort column A from A to Z and delete repeated brand rows.
  7. If fewer than 20 brands remain, go back, find more future competitors, and extract their brands.
  8. Google each brand name. Add a niche keyword (for example "kitchen") if the results are unclear.
  9. If the top result is the competitor itself, the brand is a private label (a brand the store owns and sells under its own name). Delete its row.
  10. Copy the supplier website URL, remove any trailing code after it, and paste it into column B.
  11. Find the phone number, reformat it to hyphenated US style (dashes between the groups of digits), and paste it into column C.
  12. Find the email address on the supplier's contact page and paste it into column D. If there is none, paste the contact form link (an online form on the website) instead.
  13. If a supplier's site does not load, leave its fields blank and continue with the next brand.
  14. Write any add-on or accessory products in the notes column.
  15. Validate the niche: at least one competitor needs a traffic rank (an estimate of how many visitors a site gets; a lower number means more visitors) under 100,000, and at least 20 brands need contact information.

Brand lookup on competitor sites:

  • Where to look: category page, then Filters (or More filters), then Brand.
  • Where there is no brand filter: check the footer for a "Shop all brands" or brand page link, and open that list instead.
  • Brand section: the brands sit in the 'brand' section of the category page, which appears after clicking show more. The competitor's category item count (for example 407 items) shows how big the section is. Zooming out in the browser makes the list easier to read while copying.
  • Where to look for headings: brand lists may sit under headings such as "Brands," "Brands we stock" or "Our Supplier Brands," or in the main navigation (the top menu), the footer, or a small finder widget (a small box that helps you search products). If none exists, brand names can still appear in the sidebar or in product category links, so keep scanning before deciding a site has no brand list.
  • Menu first: if the site has a Brands item in the main menu, use it. If not, open the main product category page and scroll down for a "best brands" section, a brand list in the sidebar, or a brand filter.
  • Other categories: browse the other product categories the site sells (for example dehumidifiers next to air purifiers), add brands not yet listed, and record each supplier's primary product type (the main thing it makes). A brand covering two product types can stay in one row with both types listed.
  • Site search: use a competitor's own site search (the search box on their site) with the niche keyword, such as CNC or outdoor kitchens, to see the full range. Brands that the category menus do not show can be found this way and added to the supplier count.
  • Best sellers first: use the brands that top the Best Sellers or Recommended sorting on future competitor category pages first, because stores that know what they are doing place their best sellers at the top.
  • Default sort: to find the most popular brands on a large marketplace (a website where many different sellers offer products), open the category and read the brand names in the default sort, which is bestsellers first.

Brand selection rules:

  • Add a supplier only if it actually makes products in your target niche. Skip a brand that sells mainly furniture or decor, even when it appears on a competitor's brand filter. A competitor selling a mixed catalogue does not mean the brand makes the niche product.
  • Use the exact niche category on a competitor's site. Competitors often sell in several niches, and brands from other niches would be wrong suppliers. Clicking back to the parent category by mistake leads to products outside the target niche. Always confirm the category name before copying brands.
  • Before copying a brand, open its brand page and confirm it sells products in your niche.
  • Each brand a reseller (a store that sells other companies' products) lists is usually a manufacturer or company that makes its own product. So every brand you count is a possible direct supplier (the company that makes or owns the product) to contact later. The brand still counts even if the reseller does not carry its full range.
  • Competitor brand filters are a practical way to discover suppliers, because competitors have already vetted which brands they sell. Verify the category on the brand's own page before adding it, which keeps the list relevant.
  • Retailers and e-commerce sites (online stores) that sell several brands are the source of supplier names, not suppliers themselves. A retailer is a competitor and is not added as a supplier. An own-brand site that sells only its own products can be added to the list but is not the primary target.
  • Do not enter a retailer's own website as a supplier. Do not confuse the retailer's product list with a brand list. A combined listing, such as two brand names in one entry, should be split by checking each brand separately.
  • Add each brand name as its own row, noting its primary product type. Record names only at this stage.
  • Older example: on an allergy-focused retailer's air purifier category, the category page had no brand menu. Brands were found by scrolling below the collection links to a "best brands" section and a brand list in the sidebar filter. That single site yielded about 16 to 17 manufacturer brands, which were added one per row: Airfree, Aerus, Airmega, Airpura, Alen, Austin Air, Blueair, Coway, Germ Guardian, LiteAir, PowerPure, QuietPure, Winix, and others. Checking the same retailer's dehumidifier category added further brands that were not on the air purifier list, so both categories were recorded.

Use the following procedure for a retailer-based build:

  1. Build a tracking spreadsheet (Google Sheets, Google's free online spreadsheet, or Excel, Microsoft's spreadsheet program): make your own copy of the template. Columns: Supplier Name, Product Type, Email, Phone Number, Website, Online Application? and, later, Phone Call + Date?, Call Comments, Paused. For this phase only fill the first six: Supplier Name, Product Type, Email, Phone Number, Website, Online Application.
  2. Search Google for your main product type (for example "air purifiers") and open retailer or e-commerce sites that sell many brands in your market, not only their own label.
  3. On each retailer site, look for a brands filter or brand list in the main menu or sidebar. If none exists, open the category pages and scroll for a "best brands" section or a brand list in the sidebar navigation. Record each brand name as its own row in the Supplier Name column, noting its primary product type.
  4. Check several retailer sites (at least 4 to 6) and other product categories in the niche (for example humidifiers, dehumidifiers). The same brand often appears on many sites, and new brands appear in other categories.
  5. Aim for at least 20 suppliers. Do not spend weeks on this step.
  6. For each brand, search its name on Google (do not guess the URL) and open its official website.
  7. Record the brand website URL, a public email address if available, and the phone number (often in the footer).
  8. Look for a "contact us" page and footer links. If no email is public, record "Website Contact Form" in the Email field to show no email is known yet.
  9. Look for dealer links (a dealer is a company officially allowed to sell the brand) such as "Become a dealer," "Become an [brand] dealer," or "Become a retailer." These are often in the header (the top of the page) or footer. If found, record the online application page URL in the Online Application column and follow that page's instructed contact method.
  10. Do not submit applications yet. Only record the page. Contacting suppliers and seeking approval come in a later step, after the website is built.

Older example (2021-22): a second site can add brands missing from the first. On an Australian 4x4 accessories reseller's brands page, the visible names included Eezi-Awn, Direction Plus, Thunder Auto, Harrop, GME, CTEK, Hema, Roadsafe, Tough Dog, Warn and Ultra-Vision. That single site already showed at least 20 brands. Brand names such as Clearview, Boss and Bushranger also appeared in another reseller's product category links.

Stop at one site only when you have a reason to. Do not stop at one competitor site when checking supplier counts, because a single site may show a list that is incomplete or only partly relevant.

Start the brand count with the competitor e-commerce sites you already checked for average price and shipping, instead of starting a new search from scratch. Reuse those sites first, then add further stores found through Google results that sell products matching your market idea.

Keep the online-only, multi-brand competitor websites (sites that sell many brands) open. They will be reused later to find more brands and to track future competitors. Do not close a competitor site until the niche has been fully evaluated.

Clean and de-duplicate the list

1 min

"De-duplicate" means removing repeated entries. Sort the list and remove repeats as follows:

  1. Right-click Column A.
  2. Sort A to Z.
  3. Identify duplicate brand entries.
  4. Delete duplicate rows.
  5. Keep one entry per unique brand.

Other rules for duplicates:

  • Duplicate brand names across competitor websites are expected and normal. Do not delete duplicates until you have finished collecting data from all competitor websites.
  • Brands sold by several competitors appear more than once, so duplicates are expected and must be removed before counting. Sorting column A alphabetically makes repeated names easy to spot. Counting only unique brands keeps the number of real suppliers accurate.
  • Do not add a supplier twice. Brands that appear on several competitor websites should be entered once, so before adding a name, check whether it is already in the list. Overlap between competitors is normal. In one example, three or four brands from the first competitor also appeared on the second.
  • Cross-check across four to six competitor sites. Many brands appear on several sites, so add each brand only once.

Verify each brand before it stays on the list

2 min
  • Private label check: if a brand's top Google result is the competitor's own site, it is that competitor's private label and cannot be sourced directly, so delete its row. Marketplaces such as Wayfair (an online store) private-label their own brands while still dropshipping them. Private label brands are excluded from the supplier list.
  • Rare case: if a brand's own website cannot be found, the product may be a private label owned by a retailer such as Wayfair. This is rare and should not be the default assumption.
  • Verify the company: when you search for a supplier's website, make sure you reach the brand's own company site, not a retailer page that only contains the brand name in its URL. A master brand (the main brand that owns others) may sell under several sub-brands (smaller brands under it) in the same niche. So verify the company before outreach, and avoid contacting a competitor that already sells for that brand.
  • Parent companies: a brand that appears only on one large retailer may be a white-label product (made for that retailer and sold under its name). If the same brand shows up on other sites, it likely belongs to a parent company (the company that owns it) with sister or daughter brands (other brands owned by the same parent). Approval with the parent can open those brands too.
  • Search each brand: search each brand name on Google and check its website to qualify it before you reach out.
  • Judging brand quality: when no quality signal is given, use this procedure:
    1. List the brands on the first page of competitor listings.
    2. If price is used as a quality proxy (a stand-in measure of quality), sort by price.
    3. Check that at least about 20 brands exist at the chosen price level.
    4. Read the reviews for each brand.
    5. Search each brand name together with problem-related words to see whether a recurring complaint shows up.
  • Brand with an unlabelled product: to identify the real brand behind a product with no brand name, use this procedure:
    1. Search the product name and any SKU (a store's own product code, short for stock keeping unit) or identifier on Google.
    2. In Google Chrome (a web browser), right-click the product image and choose Search image with Google Lens (Google's picture search tool).
    3. Check the other sites that list the product for a brand name or identifier.
  • Nothing published stays current: no published list of drop-ship suppliers stays accurate for long, because brands and companies appear and close constantly. The most current supplier list for a niche comes from researching that niche yourself with the same method each time.
  • Directories do not replace research: supplier directories and lists are not a substitute for researching brands yourself. Direct research uncovers more current and higher-quality suppliers than any directory.

Identify the brand behind a competitor's product

1 min

Reverse image search (searching the web with a picture to see where else it appears) is a common way to find the supplier behind a competitor's product. Use the following procedures.

Procedure 1:

  1. Use Google Chrome.
  2. Right-click the product image on the competitor site.
  3. Select Search Google for image.
  4. Review the sites that show the same image. If only the competitor appears, the product may be exclusive to them (sold only by them).
  5. Open other retailers that list the same product and check whether they show the brand name.

Procedure 2, for finding the product name and brand:

  1. Find product name and description from the competitor site.
  2. Copy the product name into Google search to find which other retailers stock it.
  3. Check which retailers display the brand name on the product page.
  4. Right-click on the competitor product image, select "Search Google for image," and check visually similar results for brand names and other retailers selling the same product.

Setting: in Google Chrome, right-click the product image on a competitor's product page and select Search Google for image. This returns every web page where the image appears, which can reveal the supplier's website.

Image search on AliExpress: use the AliExpress image search (AliExpress is an online marketplace where many sellers offer products) to find the exact product, or the closest match seen on a competitor's store. Check whether it is listed there. This is a verification step, not a sourcing recommendation. For the status of AliExpress as a supplier source, see the channel section below.

Products from Wayfair: many Wayfair products are dropshipped, so suppliers can be sourced from Wayfair product listings by identifying the brand behind them.

Channel fit: online-only versus brick-and-mortar

3 min

A channel is the way you sell to customers. Here, the question is whether a supplier works with stores that have no physical shop (online-only) or with stores that have a physical building (brick-and-mortar).

Core principle: suppliers that already sell to online-only retailers are more likely to approve a store without a physical location. Source from online-only competitors first.

  • Online-only competitors: a competitor that sells online only, with no warehouse (a place where stock is kept), store or showroom (a room where customers look at products), is a source of suppliers that already work with online-only retailers.
  • Future competitors: an online-only store that sells in your niche is a future competitor, and the brands it sells are suppliers you may want. A manufacturer that sells many other brands online is probably focused on e-commerce, so its products are usually not a fit to list.
  • Brick-and-mortar niches: when a niche has brick-and-mortar competitors (stores you can walk into), build the supplier list from brands sold by online-only competitors. Those are your true future competitors.
  • Preferred route: finding suppliers through the websites of online-only future competitors is preferred, because those competitors already work with online-only retailers. Brands that only work with brick-and-mortar stores are not worth pursuing when the goal is an online-only store.
  • Grandfathering: suppliers that have online-only competitor stores, or brick-and-mortar stores with long-held brands, may still be worth contacting. They can be grandfathered into brands (allowed in because of an older, existing arrangement). Do not build the initial list of 20 brands from them.
  • Online-only sellers: suppliers that do not require a physical storefront are the best supplier source. Find online-only competitors and reach out to the brands they carry, rather than sources that own physical stores.
  • Find through online-only stores: a supplier that also sells in physical stores is acceptable if you first found it through an online-only store.
  • Online supplier that also sells in stores: still reach out, get the price list and consider approval. Do not remove them from the master list automatically.
  • Showroom competitors: competitors that take a showroom approach can still be checked for new brands. Get approved and look at their price lists before deciding if they belong in the store.
  • Physical retailers add markup: buying from physical retailers adds their markup (the extra amount they add to the price), which leaves small margins (profit per sale). Find online-only retailers in your country that sell the brands, then contact those brands directly, since their distribution (the network that gets products to sellers) already exists.
  • Brands with a dominant chain channel: to source from a brand dominated by large chains:
    1. Find online-only stores that sell new products in the niche.
    2. Note which brands they carry.
    3. Contact those brands directly to become an authorized retailer (a store the brand officially allows to sell its products). Do not go through a distributor (a middleman company that buys and resells) or other middleman.
  • Partner pages: a brand's partner or "our partners" page can name the online retailers that already stock it. This helps you identify the brand's distribution channels.
  • Big box stores: brands sold at big box stores (large chain stores such as Home Depot or Lowe's) should still be added and contacted. A brand may list only a small share of its range there (for example 10 of 300 products), leaving the rest open for your channel.
  • Big brands: big brand suppliers generally do not drop ship (ship orders straight to the customer), so do not contact them.
  • Older conflict on big brands (2020 against 2024): (2020) says big brand suppliers generally do not drop ship, while says big box brands should still be added and contacted, since they can open a share of the range. Both apply at different levels: the first describes the brand as a whole, the second the part of its range that is not in the big box channel.
  • Mixed cases: sources that sell both online and in stores are not automatically excluded, as described in the rules above.

suppliers whose own distribution is online-only are the highest-yield starting point (the best return for the effort). Also, from 2020: suppliers that already sell to online-only stores are much more likely to approve you than suppliers that only work with brick-and-mortar retailers.

suppliers that sell only online already work with online retailers. If a supplier refuses new stores or asks for a brick-and-mortar location, set it aside, first get approved by others, and return to it once you have traction (early results and momentum).

Wayfair, Overstock and other large online retailers

2 min

Big online stores such as Wayfair and Overstock are not treated as competitors. Their product catalogs (full lists of what they sell) can be used to find brands to contact. Search each brand on Google. This usually shows other online stores that sell it.

Two terms first. A dropship item is a product the store does not keep in stock. The supplier ships it straight to the buyer. A supplier lead is a possible supplier to contact.

The share of dropship items on these platforms is reported differently by source. The figures are:

Source Year Value Note
Wayfair drop-ships 2021 more than 95% of what it sells suppliers found there are strong candidates
Large drop-shipping retailers such as Wayfair 2024 about 90 percent sourced directly from brands a useful source of supplier leads in niches they already sell in; their pop-up stores (shops open for a short time) hold only a small part of their range (all the products they sell)

Use the 2021 figure for Wayfair specifically. Use the 2024 figure for large drop-ship retailers in general. Both show that Wayfair's brand list is a strong source of leads.

Build the supplier list from the following:

  • Wayfair suppliers. A supplier is a company that sells you the products.
  • Two to three other popular online retailers that have no physical stores or warehouses (buildings where they keep stock) and publicly list the brands they sell for.

Wayfair sellers are almost all dropshippers (sellers who use dropshipping), so treat them as dropshipping competitors.

Sourcing means finding suppliers. A listing is a product page shown for sale. Source supplier brands from Wayfair listings where prices are equal to or lower than Amazon. Do not source suppliers from Amazon.

Amazon and eBay are the wrong places to find dropshipping suppliers. Middlemen are companies that sit between you and the maker and take a cut. Look for brands and approved suppliers who sell directly, meaning without a middleman.

Walmart: large US retailers such as Walmart also use drop shipping. They allow third-party sellers (other businesses) to list products on their platform. A niche is a narrow market, such as one type of product. The fact that a competitor drop ships does not decide whether it counts as a direct competitor. What matters is that it resells other brands in your niche.

Google Shopping and search results

1 min

Google Shopping is Google's product search page. The steps below are the local procedure. A manufacturer makes products. A distributor sells them to shops.

  1. Build a list of brands and suppliers that sell the products in the chosen market. Use Google Shopping listings and searches such as "electric bike manufacturers," "electric bike suppliers" or "electric bike distributors."
  2. Find each brand's website, phone number, email address and any online dealer or retailer application form (a form to apply to sell their products).
  3. Record each supplier in a tracker (a simple list or spreadsheet) with the product type and contact details.
  4. Call each supplier and introduce your business. Then ask whether you can sell their products through your website.
  5. If the call does not produce a clear result, send an email or submit the online dealer application, and record the date.
  6. Log each call with the date, the person spoken to and the outcome.
  7. After approval (the supplier says yes), the supplier shares product data (details such as descriptions and specifications), pricing and images. Upload them to your website so the products go live (appear for buyers).

Contacting suppliers from the most visible Google Shopping results is a valid starting point. Starting with the least popular suppliers can reduce fear of rejection (hearing no) and make early outreach (reaching out to contact them) easier.

Trade shows and exhibitor lists

5 min

A trade show is an event where companies show their products to buyers. An exhibitor is a company that pays for a booth (a display space) to show its products. A keyword is a word you type into a search box. An industry is a type of business, such as furniture.

Trade shows let you see many products in person in a short time and connect with brands face to face. They can also help you get approved by brands that had put you on a waiting list.

Trade-show sources:

  • TSNN.com (Trade Show News Network): lists trade shows by industry keyword. It is a worldwide trade-show directory (a list of shows). You can find upcoming shows by country and industry keyword.
  • Trade Show Network: lists trade shows for almost any industry. Open each show's website, go to its exhibitors list, and copy the brand names. Where available, also copy the URL (web address), contact name, phone and email for a supplier list.
  • Search options: you can search TSNN.com by industry keyword or by geographic location (a country or place) to find in-person events for your industry or a group of related industries.
  • Exhibitors: exhibitors are the companies that pay to show their products at trade shows. They are often looking for new retailers. Their exhibitor lists are a direct source of supplier leads.
  • Directory of shows: a trade show directory lists trade shows across industries. Each show's exhibitor list gives companies you can contact as potential suppliers.
  • Source link: the directory address appears as tsn.com in one place and tsnn.com in others. The name used most often in these sources is TSNN.com.

Setup procedure, for finding suppliers through trade shows:

  1. Open tsnn.com and use the search box.
  2. Select the country where you plan to do business.
  3. Enter a basic industry keyword, such as furniture.
  4. Review the list of upcoming trade shows in that country.
  5. Check whether each show is actually taking place, since events can be cancelled or postponed.
  6. Open the website of a relevant show and find its exhibitor list.
  7. Contact the exhibitors directly to build your list of at least 20 suppliers in the niche.

Sidebar procedure, when only a few suppliers approve. A sidebar is the menu column at the side of a web page.

  1. Open the trade show news network website (tsnn.com).
  2. Use the sidebar to enter your niche and find trade shows.
  3. Open the exhibitor lists, which show companies exhibiting their products.
  4. Contact those companies as potential suppliers.

Trade show attendance in person (procedure at the event):

  1. Search TSNN.com by industry keyword or location to find upcoming shows.
  2. Attend the show. It is typically free or cheap. Walk the hall (the main floor with the booths), where there are often hundreds of companies you could sell for.
  3. Bring business cards (small cards with your contact details). Introduce yourself to exhibitors, collect their cards, and tell them you will email them.
  4. Follow up a few days later by email. Mention the expo (trade show) where you met.

A cold email is one sent to someone who did not ask for it. A mass email is the same message sent to many people. Meeting a supplier in person makes approval easier than cold emails alone, because the contact is a face and a name and the supplier sees you as more serious. Personal contact also builds trust faster than a mass email.

Trade shows typically take place several times a year, often coast to coast (all the way across) in the USA. A new supplier search can be timed to upcoming events.

Trade shows take place every year and new brands appear regularly. Repeat exhibitor list research regularly, even for established stores.

Procedure without an upcoming trade show:

  1. Open the trade show's own website and go to its Exhibitors section, which lists every company attending.
  2. Copy the exhibitor names.
  3. Search each name on Google and open their websites.
  4. Contact them directly, the same way you do normal supplier research.

Filtering (narrowing a list to matches): trade show exhibitor lists can be filtered for companies that offer drop shipping. Use them as an extra source of supplier leads when your normal supplier search runs short.

Backup source: TSNN.com lists trade shows by niche and country. Open the exhibitors list of relevant shows in your country of business to find additional brands to contact. Use it as a backup when suppliers are out of stock (no items to sell right now) or not onboarding (not signing on new retailers).

Stock shortages: when suppliers in a niche stop taking new retailers because of stock shortages, the TSNN.com trade show network can reveal suppliers that are still approving retailers and have inventory (products held in stock). Start with those approvals. Add the shortage-hit suppliers later, once they restock (refill their stock).

Slow supplier: when a popular supplier has delayed inventory or long lead times (the wait between your order and its shipping), look for similar alternatives that can ship fast. Trade show exhibitor lists from TSNN.com are a source for new brands.

Doubling the product count (the number of products your store offers): TSNN.com is a source for finding suppliers. Also search for your niche with "trade show" and look at exhibitors, then contact those brands. The aim is to double the product count so more products can ship quickly.

Trade shows as a later stage: advanced supplier strategies can require attending trade shows. Treat them as a later-stage option, not a starting step.

Trade shows as optional: attending trade shows in person is optional. Trade-show directories and exhibitor lists should be used as a supplementary (extra, not main) way to find suppliers alongside other sourcing methods.

Competitor directories, Thomasnet and other external sources

2 min
  • Thomasnet: an online directory of manufacturers. It lists many brands useful for finding extra suppliers. Its listings do not show whether a brand works with dropshipping, so contact each brand to confirm.
  • Supplier directory blogs: blogs that list suppliers by country and niche. Use them to build a shortlist (a short, chosen list) of non-China suppliers (suppliers based outside China) before testing each one.
  • Online supplier research tools: these can show which suppliers larger brands in a niche use, mainly in the US. This is a faster route than a blind search for reverse sourcing. Reverse sourcing means working backwards, finding the suppliers that bigger brands already use. Use this route before a blind search.
  • AI-generated lists: supplier lists made by AI prompts (instructions typed into an AI tool) are acceptable only after you check that each brand is drop-ship friendly. Use them to expand a list over time after the main sourcing methods have been used up, not as the starting list (2024).
  • Middlemen platforms: build supplier lists mainly from websites that already drop ship. Avoid middlemen platforms such as Doba. Middlemen mark up products (add extra price on top) and leave almost no profit margin (the money you keep from each sale) (2024).
  • Alibaba and AliExpress: these are large online marketplaces (sites where many sellers list goods). They are not recommended as supplier sources for these stores. For more brands, search trade show exhibitor lists on TSNN.com for your niche in the country where you do business. Then contact the exhibitors directly.
  • Community forum: a forum is an online discussion board. To find suppliers in your own country, ask experienced sellers in a community forum for your region which suppliers they have approved and how they got them.
  • Crowdfunding: crowdfunding means raising money from the public before a product is made. Search Kickstarter and Indiegogo (crowdfunding websites) for projects from five years ago that became legitimate brands. Many crowdfunded projects that succeeded are now offering wholesale to retailers. Never pre-sell products that are still in crowdfunding development. Pre-selling means taking orders before the product exists.
  • Competitor brand pages: competitor brand pages on sites in your niche show which suppliers they sell. This is an alternative source of supplier leads to trade show exhibitor lists.
  • Suppliers from many sources: suppliers can be sourced from many different types of lists and products. Check the margin and shipping cost before accepting a supplier, because both determine profitability (whether you make money).

Local, international and special-case suppliers

1 min

Local suppliers are approved using the Google Shopping procedure above. For a local market, use the same steps to find and approve local suppliers.

International suppliers found through competitor stores often have stock or fulfilment (packing and shipping orders) in the target country (the country you sell to). Calling them through Google Voice or Skype (free calling apps), or emailing them, is fine.

Free bonus product: first decide which product to give away. Then source suppliers for it the same way as for the main products. Get their wholesale price (the lower price a supplier charges sellers, compared with the shop price) and use them to fulfil the bonus orders.

Outside the standard criteria, in order:

  1. Exhaust (use up fully) standard supplier research first.
  2. In a niche with many product variations (versions of the same product), check whether the top two or three suppliers make most of the sales. Deeper research often reveals more sellers.
  3. Search marketplaces such as Etsy (an online market for handmade and unique goods) for unique products from sellers with many reviews. Many reviews show they have proven sales.
  4. Contact those sellers and ask to become an authorized retailer (a seller the brand has officially approved) for their products.
  5. Ask for wholesale pricing and confirm the numbers make sense before committing.
  6. Look for independent craftspeople (self-employed makers) who sell through eBay, Facebook Marketplace, or Etsy and can make custom designs, colours, or patterns.

In niches with very high product variety, such as wallpaper or horse saddles, the number of patterns, colours and textures is nearly unlimited. Buyers often search for exact names of patterns or materials. Small craftspeople and niche sellers can add many products. They can also bring long-tail traffic (visitors who find you by searching very specific phrases) beyond what the top brands offer.

Prioritisation and choosing which brands to contact first

2 min

Use this order:

  • Gold, silver and bronze: rank each brand as gold, silver or bronze. Base the rank on its reputation (how well it is known and trusted) and its reseller stance (how open it is to sellers). A reseller is a seller who sells another company's products. Contact gold and silver brands first. Log every response in the notes column. Follow up on each brand according to the reason and timing given.
  • Gold suppliers on the list: gold suppliers are often already on your research list from the start. Keep these names in your list. They become realistic partners once you have traffic and sales.
  • Best sellers first: sorting means the order a page lists items in. A category page shows one group of products. Use the brands that top the Best Sellers or Recommended sorting on future competitor category pages first. Stores that know what they are doing place their best sellers at the top.
  • Lesser-known suppliers: large, well-known suppliers often have thin margins (small profit per sale). Lesser-known suppliers with less competition can be far more profitable. Look past the familiar names when choosing suppliers.
  • Newer brands in a competitive niche (a crowded market): approach newer or up-and-coming (new and growing) brands that the largest competitors are not focusing on. Still apply to the established brands, but do not rely on them to get started.
  • Margin and shipping check: before accepting a supplier, check the margin and shipping cost, because both determine profitability.
  • Start with approvals: start sales and traffic (visitors to your store) with suppliers who approve you.
  • Online-only sources first: source from companies that sell only online, since they already work with online retailers.
  • Competitor count versus quality: a site having 20 brands is not proof of a market (enough buyers for a product). This is true unless the brands are actually distinct and sell products in your niche.
  • Demand validation: nearly 3,000 ratings (customer scores) with four stars on Amazon for a comparable product type show proven demand. Use this as a validation signal (proof that people want it) before testing. This is a demand signal, not a supplier source.
  • Completeness versus shortlist: the master supplier list should contain every supplier in the niche that works on the dropship model, not a selected top group. Stopping at a handful of suppliers limits later sourcing and comparison options.
  • Earlier sourcing and the quality check: the list must hold all qualifying suppliers. Outreach order is set by the ranking above. These are two different stages.

Older note (2020 and 2022): a supplier's large size does not mean good margin. A list of 20 brands at a chosen price level remains the threshold (minimum line to pass) for the quality check.

A master supplier list is your main list of every supplier you have found. The sources set the number of suppliers needed in several places:

These figures apply at different stages:

  • 20 is the threshold for completing the first list.
  • The 2022 two-source rule is the sufficiency rule for a list that is thin (small or weak).
  • The 2025 step is the minimum for the supplier-contact stage.

Size depends on the niche. A niche like chandeliers (hanging ceiling lights) may hold 500 to 1,000 suppliers across all competitors. A smaller niche may hold 10, 20 or even 2,000. The aim is to find every supplier in the niche that works on dropship terms, not a shortlist of the best ones.

Example counts:

  • Chandeliers: the first competitor, a dropshipping store, gave 32 supplier names. The second added about 20 more after duplicates (repeat names) were removed.
  • Outdoor kitchens: the starting point was three future competitors (stores you plan to track): Wayfair, Zorro and Home Outlet Direct.
    • Wayfair's brand list in the outdoor kitchen category (a group of similar products) gave 29 brands in about 2 minutes.
    • Zorro's category, with 407 items, raised the total to 46 in about 1 minute.
    • Home Outlet Direct brought it to 54 in about 7 to 8 minutes overall.
    • After sorting and deleting duplicates, 52 brands remained.
  • Air purifiers: about 16 to 17 manufacturer brands (companies that make the product) from one retailer, as listed in the research section above.
  • Australian 4x4: at least 20 brands from one reseller's brands page.

A single site with 20 brands is a starting point. It does not prove a market on its own. Check the brands across several sites.

Validation checks for the niche:

  • A traffic rank (a site's popularity score, where a lower number means more visitors) of under 100,000 needed for at least one competitor.
  • At least 20 brands need contact information.

Do not build the initial list of 20 brands from grandfathered suppliers (old, long-established suppliers), as described in the channel section above.

Keep the list large enough before building the store. Make sure the list holds enough brands before the store is built, since many brands may refuse online retailers (stores that sell online).

Two to three sources: a master supplier list should contain two or three sources. A single source with 20+ brands plus a second with 10+ brands is enough. To grow a thin list, look at non-competing sites that sell similar products. Check which brands they carry in the niche's subcategory (a smaller group inside a niche).

Keep the research list as a source of future options: each source can lead to more brands. Track future competitors and add every brand found to the master list. Continue beyond the first few brands, because the list grows as more sellers are checked.

Ongoing tracking and refresh

1 min
  • Keep tracking: after a niche passes the dropship friendly test (a check that dropshipping works there), keep tracking every future competitor. Add every brand found to a master supplier list. Tracking should continue beyond the first few brands, because the list grows as more sellers are checked.
  • Revisit the competitor list: revisit the list of future competitors and check their current brand lists for new brands to contact.
  • Repeat regularly: trade shows take place every year and new brands appear regularly. Repeat exhibitor list research regularly, even for established stores.
  • Subscribe to newsletters (regular emails): subscribe to the email lists of competitors, suppliers and industry trade shows. This shows which publications and newsletters select and share content. Watch what they cover so you know which outlets are relevant for your content. This serves content research (finding ideas for what to post or write), not supplier sourcing.
  • Competitor pages stay open: do not close a competitor site until the niche has been fully evaluated.
