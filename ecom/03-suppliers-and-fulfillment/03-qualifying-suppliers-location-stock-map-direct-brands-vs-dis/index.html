Playbook › 3. Suppliers and fulfillment

Qualifying suppliers: location, stock, MAP, direct brands vs. distributors

Section 3.3 · 3 of 22 in this chapter · 44 min read

In short

  • Buy from suppliers that keep the product in stock in the country where you sell. Orders then ship fast, and customers are happier.
  • Contact the brand itself first. Use a middleman (distributor) only when no brand will approve you.
  • Do not dropship from China or from overseas when local stock exists. Long waits and surprise import fees hurt customers.
  • Ask every supplier directly where it keeps its stock. Do not say you are building a competing store.
  • MAP is the lowest price a shop may show for a product. Check first whether the supplier really enforces it.
More key points
  • Search the product on Google Shopping. If sellers' prices differ a lot, the supplier probably does not enforce MAP.
  • Sell a brand that also sells straight to buyers only if you gain an advantage, such as a better price for you or a weak supplier website.
  • Skip suppliers that do not answer, have no return policy, or fail the price check (the 2x rule).
  • Start with suppliers in the country where you sell, who already keep the products in stock there.
  • Check where the product is stored, not where it was made. Shipping from overseas is the problem.
  • Go to the brand first. Use a middleman (distributor) only if it enforces MAP (the lowest price you may show) and leaves you enough profit.
  • Check prices at other sellers before you sign up. If the supplier does not control prices, skip it or be very careful.

Order of checks before you contact a supplier

1 min
  1. Check how dropship friendly the niche is before you build the store. Dropshipping means the supplier ships each order straight to your customer, so you never hold the stock. Suppliers may refuse retailers that are not physical stores, or that do not hold their own stock.
  2. Count how many online stores already sell each brand. Prefer brands with fewer competing sellers, especially if you depend on paid ads.
  3. Check whether other online-only stores already sell this supplier's products. That shows the supplier is dropship friendly. Not every supplier will approve you. But enough will approve if you follow every criterion.
  4. Check whether competitors have a retail store, showroom or warehouse. Suppliers with a retail presence often refuse dropshipping.
  5. Verify the stock location (see the stock verification section below).
  6. Identify whether the seller is the brand, an importer, a manufacturer or a distributor (see the distributor section below).
  7. Check the MAP policy and whether it is enforced (see the MAP section below).
  8. Check the direct-to-consumer conflict (see the direct-to-consumer section below).
  9. Check marketplace and channel restrictions, and product shipping restrictions.

  • Customers in a country expect delivery within about one week. Local stock matches that expectation.
  • Fast fulfillment (packing and shipping orders quickly) makes waiting feel shorter.
  • Fast fulfillment also makes the same product feel worth more. The customer's effort/sacrifice (the time, effort and cost they give up to buy) is lower.
  • Review shipping time before you choose a supplier.
  • Slow suppliers with long shipping times can hurt the customer experience. Weigh them against product, price and margin (the money you keep after costs).

Stock verification procedures

Find where a supplier is based when you only know its product from another store:

  1. Find the supplier's product listed on a competitor's store.
  2. Google the supplier's name to find its own website and the country it operates from.
  3. Contact the supplier and ask whether it holds stock or uses a fulfillment center in the target market.

Check a foreign supplier's US stock before building a store:

  1. Find US retailers that sell the supplier's products.
  2. Contact them as a normal customer and ask whether the product is in stock and where it ships from.
  3. Alternatively, ask the supplier directly whether it has US distribution or a fulfillment center that ships to US customers.

Ask a supplier about its stock location by phone or email:

  1. Contact the company by phone, email, live chat, or Facebook message.
  2. Ask whether they keep the specific product in stock themselves and, if not, where it ships from.
  3. Phrase the question as a shopper's question, not as a competitor's question.
  4. If other sites seem to ship the same product from one location, mention it and ask whether they do the same.
  • Do not tell a company you are building a competing store when you ask whether they dropship. It makes the answer less reliable and can end the relationship.
  • If a supplier's location is unclear, check directly instead of assuming it holds stock.
  • Most dropshipping websites can be identified from their site with about 80 percent certainty.
  • When you contact a foreign supplier, first ask whether it has a U.S. warehouse or fulfillment center.
  • Asking this is polite (not rude). Do it before you request permission to sell their products.
  • A lead-time gap matters.
  • Contact the supplier, ask for its shipping and fulfillment location, and check whether it holds stock in your country.
  • Overseas phone numbers do not prove stock is overseas. Ask directly before you decide.
  • For a foreign-based supplier selling into a market, confirm that it stocks inventory in that country, for example through a local distribution center.
  • Ask directly by phone or email. This is common among international brands.
  • A store that says some brands may not ship the same day is usually dropshipping those items.
  • It uses third-party warehouses (other companies' warehouses) that it does not control.
  • Only the brands it ships itself are stocked in its own warehouse. Those brands are the exceptions in its notice.
  • Suppliers may hand out an international product catalog without stock in your market.
  • Check whether they have stock locally before committing to them.
  • A first order that takes 8 to 10 weeks is unusual when repeat orders ship within 4 to 10 days.
  • Question the supplier about where its stock is before you accept the arrangement.
  • If a supplier says it is US-based but ships from overseas, dropshipping from there causes slow shipping. That is a problem.

Canada

  • A US business address is not required to sell to US customers from Canada.
  • If you display an address on your website, a forwarding address from a US location (one that forwards mail to Canada) is recommended for legitimacy (looking trustworthy).

Procedure for a supplier in Canada (or another country) that sells to US customers:

  1. Contact the supplier individually.
  2. Introduce yourself and your location.
  3. State that you sell in the US.
  4. Ask whether the supplier has a US distribution center and whether there are extra fees or shipping charges for US delivery.
  • For Canada-based suppliers, first check whether they hold US inventory.
  • If not, ask which carrier they use to ship to US customers and how they avoid border delays.
  • Ask suppliers if they ship to Canada before you add Canada to your store.
  • Ask whether they already use a go-to shipper (the carrier they always use) for Canada.
  • Start with the US market first, because it is the bigger market.
  • If products are already warehoused in the US, they can be sold to both the US and Canada.
  • Choose Canadian suppliers only when the business sells only in Canada.

UK, Europe and other markets

  • Products from UK suppliers are acceptable only when no US source exists, or when US sellers all source from the UK.
  • Product pages must state clearly that the products are made and shipped from the UK.
  • Product pages must also state the estimated shipping time to the US.
  • Brexit (the UK leaving the EU, which changed trade rules) and similar trade barriers make supplying UK customers from European warehouses more complicated.
  • Check current import and export rules before sourcing across that border.
  • Operators in the UK may need to adjust the standard dropship model.
  • They may need to explain the process to local suppliers who are not familiar with it.
  • Successful UK sellers have documented those adjustments and shared them with their supplier partners.
  • The same MAP reseller rules (rules for stores that resell a brand's products) are described as applying in the UK, Germany, Australia and the US.
  • If a market appears to have no MAP for a brand, research how experienced resellers in that market handle the brand.
  • Do not assume the brand cannot be sold.

MAP policy

1 min

Definition and purpose

1 min
  • Minimum Advertised Price (MAP) is a pricing floor, meaning the lowest price a supplier lets its approved sellers advertise.
  • A supplier sets MAP to stop sellers from advertising below that price.
  • MAP protects fair profit (margin) for all sellers.
  • MAP prevents destructive price competition, a price war where sellers undercut each other until no one earns money.
  • Minimum advertised price (MAP) is the lowest price a retailer (a shop that sells to buyers) may advertise a supplier's product for.
  • When a shop lists below MAP, a supplier that enforces MAP contacts it.
  • The shop must raise its price or lose its permission to sell the product.
  • MAP is a contract. An authorized retailer signs an agreement not to advertise the product below a set price.
  • The set price is usually wholesale cost (the price the supplier charges you) multiplied by a stated factor.
  • Some suppliers have no MAP at all.
  • In some countries, MAP policies cannot be enforced by law.
  • Check whether a supplier enforces MAP before judging it.
  • Brands use MAP to protect their brand value and their retail partners.
  • If one retailer sells below the floor, others lose sales and the product's perceived value drops.
  • MAP therefore keeps prices stable across all sellers.
  • MSRP is the manufacturer's suggested retail price, the price the maker recommends to buyers.
  • MAP is the minimum advertised price.
  • A supplier that sells online at MSRP while letting you sell at MAP gives you a price advantage over the supplier's own website.
  • Silver and gold are partner levels some suppliers use. Silver and gold suppliers expect you not to list their products below their MAP.
  • Pricing below their MAP is called undercutting. Undercutting breaks the supplier trust relationship.
  • As an Authorized Retailer (an approved seller of the brand), you represent the supplier's brand.
  • Poor customer experiences with your business create negative associations that damage the supplier's brand reputation, not just your own.

Testing MAP enforcement

2 min
  • Search for a supplier's products on Google Shopping (Google's product price search) before you call.
  • Widely varying prices for the same product suggest MAP is not enforced. That is a red flag (a warning sign).
  • Procedure to check whether a supplier enforces MAP:
    1. Search Google for the supplier's company name.
    2. Look at the supplier's most popular products in Google Shopping.
    3. Compare the prices sellers list for the same product.
    4. If prices vary widely, the supplier probably does not enforce MAP.
  • Procedure for a brand whose products unauthorized sellers (shops not approved by the brand) list at MAP:
    1. Keep advertising your own authorized status and the brand's warranty.
    2. Ask the brand to contact the unauthorized sellers and have their listings removed.
    3. If that does not work, the brand can file a DMCA notice (a legal takedown request) with the host company (the company that runs the listing site).
  • Sellers should check a supplier's MAP enforcement before approval.
  • Being approved without a MAP policy to sign is a red flag. It means the supplier probably does not enforce MAP.
  • A supplier that approves you without sending a MAP policy to sign is a red flag.
  • That supplier likely has no MAP policy at all.
  • Check whether a supplier enforces MAP before relying on it.
  • A supplier that says it has no control over MAP prices is not a true MAP supplier.
  • With such suppliers, keep selling only if products sell at MAP and traffic (visitors to your store) is profitable.
  • When a supplier with a MAP policy does not enforce it, it is usually not worth staying with them.
  • First contact them directly and make the problem clear before you decide to drop them.
  • Competing stores should not be allowed to break MAP with discounts.
  • A non-competing store can use your brand keywords (the words people search to find your brand) in ads and sell other brands at low prices.
  • You can deal with it by contacting the brand you sell for.
  • The brand can ask Google to block that advertiser from bidding on its brand name. Bidding means paying for ad spots.
  • Ideally, every supplier carries a MAP policy.
  • A small number without one, such as 2 out of 20, can be accepted.
  • Those brands must be excluded from your price match policy (a rule where you match competitor prices).
  • Check the competitor prices of those brands before any ads run.
  • Suppliers that enforce MAP and let you ship on your own account (use your own shipping account) are better than non-MAP suppliers.
  • Shipping on your own account can feel scary at first.
  • It becomes routine once you work with several suppliers.

Non-MAP suppliers

1 min
  • A supplier without MAP pricing is not an automatic rejection.
  • MAP suppliers are preferred, but the decision depends on what competitors charge for the same products.
  • Supplier MAP policies are enforced, but many MAP suppliers are workable.
  • Non-MAP suppliers can also be profitable.
  • Some countries do not allow MAP, so the decision depends on the market and what products are available.
  • Non-MAP suppliers can be a valid first step.
  • Live products (products already on sale) on your store increase trust. That can help you get approved by other suppliers.
  • Non-MAP supplier research, in order:
    1. Apply to every brand in your niche (your product area) that you can get approved for. Example: 50 brands means contacting all 50. Collect their price lists.
      • Being an authorized retailer does not oblige you to sell their products.
    2. Identify which suppliers are MAP and which are not, from the price lists.
    3. For each non-MAP supplier, build your own price list with columns for Wholesale, MSRP (manufacturer suggested retail price) and Competition.
    4. Search each product name on Google Shopping as a customer would.
      • Record the average competitor price in the Competition column.
      • Use established, legitimate-looking stores for the average.
    5. Check each supplier's average competitor price against its wholesale cost using the 2x rule (the price check, explained further on).
    6. Upload only products that pass the 2x rule. Skip the rest without regret.
    7. Monitor KPIs (key performance indicators, your main result numbers) in your Google Ads account (Google's paid ad system) on a fixed schedule (see below).

Direct-to-consumer conflict

1 min

How common and how to judge it

2 min
  • Direct-to-consumer means selling straight to buyers, not through shops. Roughly 20% of suppliers also sell this way on their own website, and most do not.
  • Drop shipping means you sell and the supplier ships. A supplier's direct website only leaves room for profitable drop shipping when the site is weak.
  • A weak site is outdated, slow, has poor images or has poor product descriptions.
  • If a supplier's own site is polished and offers too little discount for you to afford paid traffic (paid visitors from ads), skip that supplier.
  • If the site is weak, you can still compete with a bonus gift, expiring coupon codes (codes that stop working after a date) or by matching the price.
  • A supplier's own online store is not a red flag if it sells at MSRP and allows you to sell at MAP.
  • Weak supplier websites (one photo, poor descriptions, missing features) are an opening to take sales with a better product page.
  • A manufacturer (a company that makes products) that sells direct is not automatically a problem.
  • It is harder to compete with when its site uses advanced pixel tracking (a small piece of code that follows visitors) and is clearly built for online selling.
  • It is fine when online sales are only an add-on to a brand website.
  • Meta Pixel Helper is a free Chrome extension (a browser add-on) for the Meta pixel (tracking code from Meta, the company behind Facebook and Instagram).
  • It lights up with the number of Meta pixels on any site you visit.
  • Several pixels, such as a page view plus an AMP page view (AMP is a fast-loading mobile page format), show that a supplier or competitor is investing in direct online sales.
  • Check whether a supplier advertises directly to consumers:
    1. Search the brand name on Google.
    2. Check Google Shopping for the brand's listings.
    3. Check the search text ads (plain written ads) at the top of Google.
    4. Check whether you see their Facebook remarketing ads (ads shown again to people who visited before).

Conditions for selling a brand that sells direct

2 min
  • Sell for suppliers who sell direct only if one of two conditions is true.
  • Condition 1: they sell at MSRP and allow retailers to sell at MAP.
  • Condition 2: their direct-to-consumer experience is poor, and you can provide better user experience (UX, how easy a site is to use), more information and an easier buying process.
  • A supplier that sells direct to the public can still be acceptable if its direct price equals MSRP and it lets retailers sell at MAP.
  • Example: MSRP of 1,500 USD for direct sales and MAP of 1,200 USD for retailers.
  • A supplier that sells direct can also be acceptable if its own online store shows clear neglect (clear lack of care).
  • Signs of neglect include outdated design, slow loading, broken links, poor product images or weak information.
  • In that case, a retailer can provide a better shopping experience.
  • Suppliers that sell direct to the public at MSRP while allowing retailers to sell at MAP are workable.
  • Avoid suppliers with strong direct websites that compete with your store, unless their site looks outdated and shows little effort.
  • Suppliers selling directly to the public at MSRP that allow retailers to sell at MAP are acceptable.
  • Suppliers selling direct where e-commerce (online selling) is weak can still be profitable.
  • Supplier with a sale on its own website: list products only if it sells at MSRP and allows retailers to sell at MAP.
  • Or list products if it sells direct but shows little investment in its e-commerce site.
  • A supplier who sells direct to consumers is acceptable if it sells at MSRP while retailers sell at MAP.
  • A direct site is also acceptable if it is weak and unmarketed (not promoted by ads).
  • If they run a strong direct business with ads and remarketing, expect your profit margin to shrink.
  • Sell a brand that also sells direct only if you gain an advantage.
  • The advantage is either: the supplier sells at MSRP while you sell at MAP, or the supplier's site looks outdated so you look more trustworthy.
  • Otherwise, do not sell that brand.
  • Sell a brand that sells direct only if the supplier sells at MSRP while you sell at MAP.
  • Or sell if it sells direct from a weak site.
  • Avoid suppliers with strong direct-to-consumer websites that compete with your store.
  • Suppliers that sell both online and through physical retail locations (real shops) are still viable.
  • Cross-check their prices on competitor stores and Amazon.
  • Make sure they are not undercutting your pricing significantly.
  • Let them through your approval process unless you find specific margin or MAP policy issues.
  • A supplier that sells direct at MSRP, with a physical showroom (a space for displaying products), is acceptable if you can still sell at MAP.
  • Supplier retail pricing should leave you room to sell below it.
  • Choose suppliers whose competitors have no retail store, showroom or warehouse (storage building).
  • Check competitor websites for this before you approach suppliers.
  • Suppliers with retail presence often refuse drop shipping.

Undercutting risk

1 min
  • Suppliers can sell to the public directly and undercut retailers. That is a warning sign.
  • If a supplier's recommended retail prices or its own site sell below what you can offer, do not list that brand.
  • Do not negotiate price parity (equal prices) before advertising it.
  • Avoid suppliers that sell directly to the public and would undercut your MAP price.
  • Prefer suppliers who sell only to businesses and let you sell at MAP.
  • If a supplier sells directly to the public at a lower price than your planned selling price, do not upload its products when you know this in advance.
  • If you find out later, keep the products on the store but do not include them in Google Ads.

Marketplace and channel restrictions

1 min
  • A marketplace is a large shared shopping site, such as Amazon or eBay. A channel is any place you sell.
  • If a supplier forbids selling on Google, Amazon, eBay, Facebook or Bing, ask for clarification, because most suppliers only restrict marketplaces.
  • Ask how the store is expected to advertise before deciding to work with them.
  • Suppliers that sell on Wayfair (an online home goods marketplace) are not a problem.
  • Large marketplaces often deliver poorly and treat their suppliers badly. Suppliers may then prefer to send business directly to the seller.
  • An inquire-only button is a button that asks a question instead of selling.
  • If a supplier requires one and no cart (shopping basket), do not list its products.
  • Google Shopping ads need products that can be bought directly.
  • Some suppliers require that orders are shipped using the retailer's own shipping account.
  • Some suppliers do not accept customer returns at all.

Product restrictions that affect supplier choice

1 min
  • Battery-powered items such as Electric Bicycles, Electric Scooters, Go-Karts, Golf Carts and RC Cars, RC Planes, RC Boats and RC Helicopters (RC means remote control) can face shipping restrictions for their batteries.
  • Confirm the carrier's (shipping company's) and supplier's lithium battery rules (lithium is a battery type) before committing to these items.
  • Products with a built-in battery often cannot be shipped to the US.
  • Confirm with the supplier or carrier that a battery product can reach the target country before sourcing it or running ads.
  • Discovering a shipping restriction after ads are live (running) wastes test budget (the money set aside for testing).
  • AliExpress is a large online marketplace where suppliers list products.
  • A product needs at least one listing with a 4.6+ rating (a score of 4.6 or higher out of 5) and at least 50 reviews.
  • Ideally, all listings are at 4.6+.
  • If no seller reaches 4.6+, the product is probably low quality.
  • Skip AliExpress products with more than 10,000 orders.
  • High order counts mean the product is already heavily scaled (sold in very large volumes), which makes it riskier to sell.
  • Check consumer reviews for damage before choosing a brand:
    1. Search for the brand's products on Google as a customer would.
    2. Read the consumer reviews.
    3. Look for a repeated pattern of items arriving damaged.
    4. Avoid or adjust products where damage is a trend, especially if you must ship on your own account.

Supplier vetting procedures

1 min
  • Vetting means checking carefully before you commit.
  • Procedure to confirm a brand is legitimate (real and trustworthy):
    1. Research the brand by searching on Google.
    2. Search the brand name alone.
    3. Search the brand name with 'reviews'.
    4. Evaluate search results for supplier reliability and reputation issues.
    5. If problems appear, avoid listing their products.
  • Procedure to confirm a brand's own website and independence:
    1. Search Google for each brand name in Column A (the first column of your brand list).
    2. Find and visit the brand's official website.
    3. If it is a private label (a product made for one seller and sold under its name) or marketplace-exclusive product (sold on only one marketplace), delete the row.
    4. Confirm the supplier operates independently.
  • Procedure to confirm a supplier serves only online stores:
    1. Search Google for the niche (your product area).
    2. Use only the websites of brands that sell exclusively online. Skip brick-and-mortar retailers (shops with physical stores).
    3. Confirm the company has no showroom and no warehouse. An office alone is acceptable.
    4. Source supplier names from the websites of your true future competitors. Suppliers found there are more likely to work with online-only stores.
  • Supplier traffic rank (how popular a website is compared with others) does not matter for choosing a supplier, since suppliers usually do not try to attract traffic.
  • A competitor's traffic rank is a useful signal instead.
  • Search for a supplier's products on Google Shopping before calling. Treat widely varying prices as a red flag.
  • Before judging a supplier, check whether it enforces MAP.
  • Some suppliers have no MAP at all.

Supplier responsiveness, returns and support

1 min
  • Before approval, make sure a supplier answers the phone and emails.
  • A brand that never answers may not support products after approval.
  • If a supplier cannot answer technical product questions, such as steel type or weight capacity, treat that as a red flag.
  • If a question is about product selection (such as which barbell, a long weight-lifting bar, suits a given body weight and lift), create a buying guide on your site.
  • Do not dismiss that question.
  • A supplier that offers no return policy (rules for sending items back) at all is a warning sign.
  • It is more worrying when every approved supplier has the same policy.
  • Investigate those suppliers before building products on them.
  • Do not buy an expensive supplier demo product (a sample to test) just to learn their customer support. Such requests are unusual.
  • Only consider it when there is a strong resale market (many buyers to sell it to).
  • Check first how quickly the product can be resold.
  • Avoid suppliers that expect the retailer to provide local support, repairs or solutions to customers' technical problems.
  • These demands move a heavy support burden onto the store.
  • Some suppliers request an opening order (a first purchase) before granting catalog access (permission to see their product list).
  • Reply that you need assurance you can sell their products at the required price.
  • Keep contacting other suppliers in the meantime.
  • A store that says products from certain brands may not ship the same day is usually dropshipping those items from third-party warehouses (storage run by another company) it does not control.

Choosing a supplier by motivation

1 min
  • Choose a supplier based on your motivation (your main goal): speed, quality or price.
  • These three priorities lead to very different supplier choices.
  • Shipping time is a factor to review before choosing a supplier.
  • Weigh shipping time against product, price and margin.
  • Partner with US-based suppliers achieving 2 to 3 day delivery.
  • Avoid international dropshippers with 21 to 30 day turnaround (time from order to dispatch).
  • Faster fulfillment (processing and shipping orders) reduces the perceived time delay and the effort and sacrifice (what the buyer has to give up while waiting).
  • This dramatically multiplies the value of the same product.

Where sources disagree

3 min
  • Overseas sourcing (finding and buying from overseas suppliers): the strict position is no dropshipping from China or from abroad by default. Dropshipping means you sell the product, and the supplier ships it straight to your customer.
    • Exception: a custom, high-demand single overseas maker, when delivery and the margin (the money you keep after costs) both work.
    • Exception: a nearby country such as Europe, when shipping is affordable and no high import taxes (fees charged when goods enter a country) apply.
    • Exception: an outside-US supplier whose product is unique and of good quality.
    • Exception: a UK supplier only when no US source exists.
    • Older: the 2019 position says long delivery can be a benefit. It applies only when the margin holds after shipping.
  • Origin versus stock location: a product made in China is not a problem when a brand or importer holds it locally (an importer brings goods into a country and sells them there). The problem is shipping from overseas. Use stock location (where the products are kept on hand) as the test, not the country of manufacture.
  • Delivery numbers: the 2 to 3 day figure for US suppliers comes from Source C (an online business course). The 2 to 7 day figure for a high-ticket (expensive product) US manufacturer comes from Source B to 2022, so it is older. Both describe US stock. Use the number from your own supplier's stated process.
  • Non-MAP suppliers: MAP is the minimum advertised price, the lowest price a shop may show. Non-MAP suppliers have no such rule.
    • Red flags: an approval with no MAP policy to sign (approval means the brand says yes, you may sell its products), and a supplier that has no control over MAP.
    • Non-MAP suppliers can still be workable as a first step:
      • when competitor prices support your margin (competitors are other shops selling the same thing),
      • when only a small share of brands lacks MAP,
      • in markets where MAP cannot be enforced.
    • The key question is whether the supplier enforces MAP (actually checks prices and acts), not whether it has a policy on paper.
  • Distributors (middlemen between the brand and shops): the default is direct brand approval first.
    • Distributors are accepted when they enforce MAP and leave enough margin.
    • They are also accepted when they are the only route to the brand.
    • They are accepted as a fallback (a backup option), which is not the goal.
    • Older: the 2019 statement that distributors often have no MAP still applies as a check.
  • Direct-to-consumer (DTC, a brand selling straight to shoppers from its own website):
    • The 2021 position: a strong supplier store that would match your price is not worth the time.
    • The 2023 position: omnichannel suppliers (brands that sell in shops and online) are viable unless specific margin or MAP issues appear.
    • Both apply. Check prices on competitor stores and Amazon, and decline a strong direct store that would match your price.
  • Showrooms (a brand's own physical shop):
    • A showroom selling at MSRP (manufacturer's suggested retail price, the list price the brand recommends) is acceptable when you can still sell at MAP.
    • On the same topic, choose suppliers whose competitors have no retail presence, because retail presence often leads to refusals.
    • Treat showroom presence as acceptable only after you have checked whether the supplier approves drop shipping (shipping orders straight to customers).
  • Distributors for region versus MAP: a regional distributor requirement (the brand insists you buy through a distributor for your area) is not a barrier if the supplier does not enforce MAP. Distributors that do enforce MAP are the exception that justifies working through them.

Supplier Location and Domestic Stock

2 min

Start Local

  • Work only with suppliers based in the country where you sell, who already keep stock there (stock means products on hand). This can be direct, or through a local distributor or a subsidiary (a company owned by a bigger company) of an overseas parent company. For the EU (the European Union), the products should ideally already be in the EU, possibly in several EU countries.
  • Do not start by shipping products across borders, for example from China into your sales market. Cross-border shipping can come later, but it is not the starting model.
  • Work only with local suppliers in the same country as your target customers (the shoppers you aim for), not with overseas manufacturers, so the product ships domestically (within the same country).
  • Local, independent, established suppliers make operations much easier in many ways. Suppliers are usually experienced and open to working with new sellers.
  • Local suppliers in a market with a strong e-commerce (online selling) and retail industry often have established, tested, high-quality products. They are looking for online retail partners to sell them.

Overseas Manufacturing and US Stock

  • Products may be manufactured overseas, for example in Italy, as long as the supplier keeps the stock in a US warehouse (a storage building) that orders ship from. The headquarters location does not matter.
  • Products from suppliers are usually from US-based brands that hold stock in the US, not products made in the US. Brands can be from the same country as the store's shipping base. The store's suppliers do not need to manufacture locally.
  • Source from suppliers with stock or a warehouse in the United States. Dropshipping from Europe to the U.S. is generally too expensive to be workable.

Checking Stock Readiness

  • When a supplier cannot ship a product from US stock right away, check whether it has a set of bestselling products that are always kept in its US warehouse.
  • Only list those in-stock bestsellers as US-ready products.

Brand, Distributor and Importer

1 min

Source Direct

  • Source directly from the brand rather than from wholesalers (companies that buy in bulk and resell), because direct margins are much better.
  • Work directly with manufacturers rather than distributors or middlemen. Manufacturers usually carry several sub-categories (smaller groups within a category) of products that you can select from.
  • Buy from the brand itself rather than from a large retailer that resells its products. The closer you are to the actual brand, the better.
  • A brand that sells direct to consumers will often refuse a dropshipper at first, which is normal. Use the same approval steps, and expect the relationship to be slightly different.

When a Distributor Is Acceptable

  • Use a distributor only when the supplier sells exclusively through that distributor (only through it, nowhere else). Also confirm the distributor enforces price policies. Avoid a middleman that adds a markup (an extra amount added to the price) and has no pricing controls when you can contact the supplier directly.
  • Only work with a distributor if it enforces MAP. Brands that sell outside the distributor can often be approached directly.
  • In a niche (a narrow market segment) where most brands sell only through a distributor, around 5 to 10 percent of the brands may still be available for direct approval. Check MAP enforcement before signing up with the distributor.
  • Suppliers that sell direct to consumers and use distributors in the US may raise prices or limit stock for resellers during shortages. Distributors are therefore not a preferred source.

Conditions for Online Sellers

  • Some suppliers only approve stores that can show they sell online. Suppliers that also sell to brick-and-mortar retailers (shops with a physical building) are acceptable if you found them through online-only retailers.

Direct-to-Consumer Conflict

1 min
  • A supplier that already sells direct to consumers on Google Shopping (Google's product price listing service) is only worth approaching if:
    • it sells at MSRP and allows you to sell at MAP, or
    • its own product pages are weak enough that you can beat them.
  • Otherwise, skip it.

MAP Policy and Enforcement

2 min

What MAP Is

  • MAP stands for minimum advertised price, the lowest price a retailer may advertise for a product. After approval, the supplier gives you a MAP price list. You may never list a product below its MAP.
  • Example: if supplier ABC sets the MAP at $1,000, no retailer may advertise its products below $1,000, and that includes you.
  • Track supplier price lists and MAP policies in writing from the supplier. Verify them before listing products (putting products up for sale), because enforcement varies by supplier.

Why MAP Matters

  • A supplier often already has about five other retailers selling its products. Those retailers depend on stable prices to keep their margins, so MAP violations (breaking the MAP rule) hurt all of them.
  • Undercutting MAP (selling below the MAP price), such as selling a product with a $1,000 MAP for $700, may take all the sales from competing retailers. Those retailers then lose margin, get upset, and may stop carrying the brand. This damages the supplier's market.
  • MAP protects the profit margins of retailers, so good retailers keep stocking the brand. Suppliers want as many quality retailers as possible, and will act against anyone who breaks the pricing policy.

Checking Coupons and Discounts

  • Check each supplier's MAP agreement for whether discounts or coupons (codes that lower the price at checkout) are allowed. If coupons are not allowed, the agreement will say so. MAP software (programs that scan online prices) usually checks the displayed price, so a discount shown on the page is what gets flagged.

Testing Enforcement in MAP Markets

  • MAP rules are country-specific, but most countries outside the United States have no MAP. In those markets, apply for approval with every supplier you find, not only with suppliers who enforce MAP.
  • In MAP markets such as the United States, test whether a supplier enforces MAP by checking whether its retailers all sell at the same price, not below the minimum threshold. This test does not apply in non-MAP markets.

Procedure: Check MAP Enforcement Before Contacting a Supplier

  1. Search the brand names from your niche research on Google.
  2. Find each brand's best-selling products on the websites of the sellers that appear.
  3. Take the product names and SKUs (SKU means stock keeping unit, the product's code number) of those best sellers and search them on Google Shopping.
  4. Compare the prices that different sellers list.
  5. Consistent prices suggest MAP is enforced. Widely varying prices, such as $500, $550 and $800 for the same product, suggest it is probably not enforced, even if the supplier has a MAP policy.
    • If a product sells for $1,000 at five stores while one store sells it for $600, treat this as a red flag (a warning sign). Avoid advertising that supplier, because its prices are not controlled.

Regions with Restricted MAP

  • In regions where MAP policies are restricted, such as parts of Europe and possibly Australia, drop the price match policy (the rule about matching other sellers' prices). Screen every supplier more carefully to make sure no one is selling their products at a loss.

Marketplace Restrictions

1 min
  • A brand already sold on large online-only retailers such as Wayfair (a big online store) can still be a dropship-friendly supplier (one that allows dropshipping). Being listed on a big marketplace does not on its own disqualify a brand.
  • Products sold on AliExpress (a large overseas online marketplace) may be near-copies of a legitimate brand's product. This can lead to a supplier being shut down and to legal trouble.
  • A product suddenly removed from AliExpress may have been taken down after a rights holder (the legal owner of the brand or design) complained.

Drop-Ship Friendliness Checks

1 min

Quotes, Pricing and Fulfillment

  • Source and validate the supplier before selling. Get proper quotes (price offers), confirm pricing, and confirm that the supplier can fulfill (ship) the product.
  • Get proper quotes, confirm prices, and confirm the supplier can fulfill the item before selling. Contact several suppliers as backups to compare prices and quality, and to make sure there is after-sale support and a return policy.

Pre-Upload Quality Check

  • Before uploading a supplier's products (putting them on your shop), search the brand online for reviews and product quality.
  • Check that the listing (your product page) is worth the effort, since you will rely on organic traffic (free visitors from search results, not paid ads).
