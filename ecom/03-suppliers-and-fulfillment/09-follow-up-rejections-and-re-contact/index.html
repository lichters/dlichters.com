Playbook › 3. Suppliers and fulfillment

Follow-Up, rejections and re-Contact

Section 3.9 · 9 of 22 in this chapter · 41 min read

In short

  • Suppliers get busy in November and December. Replies slow down, so start early.
  • Follow up again and again. Most suppliers say yes only after repeated contact.
  • A "no" usually means "not yet." Write down the reason and a date to try again.
  • Ask exactly what a supplier needs, such as traffic or sales, then work toward that number.
  • Never drop a supplier. Come back later with proof that your store is growing.
More key points
  • Before you restart with an old supplier, ask for their current stock, products and prices.
  • Contact every supplier. Do not stop after one try.
  • When a supplier says yes, upload its products to your store, then go back to your list.
  • Write down every answer and set a next date to follow up.
  • A "no" is not the end. Try again later, when your store has more visitors and sales.

Tracking and records

  • Record three things for every supplier: the date of first contact, the contact person's name, and detailed notes from each conversation with its outcome. This prevents accidental duplicate outreach, coordinates several supplier processes at once, and keeps follow-ups on time.
  • Keep a Master Supplier list that tracks all outreach and contact attempts. For each supplier, record the contact person's name, the conversation date, the details discussed, and the outcome.
  • Record the contact person and the date of first contact in your notes. A later follow-up can then address that person by name and refer to the earlier conversation.
  • Detailed notes matter because with many brands it is easy to forget what happened on an earlier call. Notes show exactly where you stand with each supplier.
  • Failing to log notes across a large list leads to repeated or inconsistent calls and emails. Record the outcome of every contact immediately.
  • Log every supplier response in the notes, including reasons for refusal and conditions such as "contact again in three months" or visitor and revenue thresholds. Schedule follow-ups from these notes instead of relying on memory.
  • Record every supplier response, including rejections, in a supplier tracking sheet and schedule a later follow-up date.
  • Log exact requirements, such as a monthly visitor minimum or a monthly sales level, together with a re-contact date such as 3-6 months. These notes decide when and how to follow up.
  • Typical conditions to record, and the trigger each one sets:
    • "contact again in 3-6 months"
    • "requires 10,000 monthly visitors"
    • "only works with stores doing $20,000+ per month"
  • Keep a supplier tracking spreadsheet with the exact date you must contact each supplier again. A supplier that is out of stock or asks you to come back later gets a dated entry, such as the end of Q3.
  • Use a supplier spreadsheet to track every supplier you contact, with a notes column for minimum requirements and the reason for each rejection. The spreadsheet from the Drop Ship Blueprint can serve as the starting template.
  • Record every supplier conversation with the contact person, the date, and the planned follow-up date. Circle back later, because a rejection in a new niche often means the supplier wants proof that you are a real store.
  • Track each conversation in a sheet with the date and what was said, and follow up within a set period.
  • Note the contact date, the person and the outcome in the supplier sourcing worksheet.
  • Record the requirements for each supplier next to its name in your supplier spreadsheet.
  • Record a refusal and its date in your supplier spreadsheet.
  • Record the stated reason for a rejection and the follow-up plan that goes with it.
  • Do not list an out-of-stock supplier's products yet, and record its status so you can return to it later.

First contact and the first follow-up

Sequence for each supplier on the list

  1. Start with the first supplier on your list and work down one supplier at a time.
  2. Day 1: if the supplier has a phone number, call first. Ask for their application process and for the decision maker who handles new accounts.
  3. Day 1: if the supplier asks you to email during the call, send the day-one email from the contact plan.
  4. Day 2: if the day-one email gets no reply, send the follow-up email. It restates your earlier request, gives a near launch window, and asks for the application steps.
  5. Day 3: make another phone call.
  6. Days 4 to 7: continue the mixed calls and emails in the plan until you get a clear yes or no.
  7. Stop the sequence early once you reach the right person and get a clear answer.

Email or form requested by the supplier

  1. Send a short, simple email to the dealer or retailer contact address on the supplier's website. Introduce yourself and your store, and include a condensed version of your pitch on why they should work with you.
  2. If there is no reply by the next day, call in the morning.
  3. On the call, introduce yourself and the store, mention the email sent the day before, and say you want to confirm it arrived.

Before ending the call, ask when you should expect a reply. Submit the email or form right after the call, and if nothing arrives within a couple of days, call the same contact again.

- Suppliers with online dealer applications expect the form to be filled out. If there is no reply after a couple of days, call to follow up. **
- If a supplier does not reply within about two days after a promised follow-up, call or email again and stay in regular contact. **
- Follow up with a supplier about three days after a call or email if you have not heard back. Keep the follow-up short, for example by confirming they saw your message.
- If there is no response to an email or contact form, wait one day, then follow up. Suppliers often have no phone number listed. **
- Email visibility: two to three days after the initial email, reply in the same thread. This creates two emails from you in the recipient's inbox on different days.
- Do not sort or prioritize suppliers when reaching out. Contact all of them, keep notes on those that decline, and circle back later.
- Start supplier outreach early, even before the store is ready. A supplier that is low on stock can still add you to a list for later. **
- Starting outreach early in the autumn avoids the busy and slow periods described in the holiday section.

**Warm leads first**

When suppliers say "email me" or "fill out our form", prioritize them, because a dialogue has started.

Procedure for a warm-lead batch: *(2023)*

1. Reach out to 20 or more suppliers as your minimum starting pool.
2. Prioritize suppliers that said "email me" or "fill out our form".
3. Follow up on these warm leads daily or every other day before contacting the remaining suppliers.
4. Once a supplier sends approval documents, drop other priorities and complete the paperwork immediately.
5. Upload their products and integrate them into your store.
6. Return to contacting the remaining suppliers on your list.

**Old onboarding forms**

Do not simply answer an old onboarding form or email that has sat unanswered for a long time. Contact that supplier again as if the relationship were new, using the channel you originally used (phone, contact form or email), because contacts and forms may have changed. *(2024)*

- Follow up with suppliers every two to three days until you get a clear yes or no. Persistence shows you are serious, and busy suppliers forget you if they are not reminded.
- With a list of 300 or more, keep following up every 2-3 days. Persistent follow-up is not spam when it is backed by genuine business intent.
- Keep following up until you receive a yes or a no. A clear no is better than no information. **
- Keep following up by phone or email, because suppliers are busy and repeated contact often gets a response. Do not stop after one attempt. *(2023)*
- Follow-ups are normal and expected. Suppliers receive many calls and do not treat a second attempt as a nuisance.
- If a supplier does not answer within two to three days, follow up by phone. *(2024)*
- Follow up after a supplier's stated timeline, not before. A supplier that says approval takes 4-8 weeks gets its reminder at 4 weeks.

**Gold-tier suppliers**

- Gold suppliers can take 3 months, 6 months, or more than a year to approve a store. Plan follow-ups on that timeline, while many other suppliers can be added immediately.
- You can contact gold suppliers early, but expect "not yet" until you reach a certain traffic or sales level. Use the time to build the store and track results so you can reapply later.

**Procedure: supplier who said approval takes 4-8 weeks**

1. Document the contact person's name, the contact date, and the timeline in your supplier tracking spreadsheet.
2. Set a calendar reminder for exactly 4 weeks after initial contact.
3. Follow up with the supplier on the reminder date.
4. For email-only suppliers that have not responded after nearly 2 weeks of follow-ups every 2 days, stop following up on Fridays before long weekends, when staff take extended time off.
5. Resume follow-ups on Wednesday or Thursday of the following week, once decision makers are back in the office.

### Unresponsive suppliers

**Basic checks first**

- If no supplier responds, check the basics: that your email address receives mail, that your contact details on voicemails are correct, and that the store is not password-protected when you point suppliers to it. **
- If suppliers do not respond despite outreach, follow up persistently and check whether the outreach strategy itself is failing before changing anything else. **
- When many suppliers reject your outreach, the root cause is usually the approach: the wording of your message, the way you contact them, or the time of day you reach out. It is rarely the number of suppliers or the niche itself.
- Switching niches or contacting many more suppliers after repeated rejections wastes time if you have not first diagnosed why suppliers say no. Review your message, contact method and timing first.
- If hundreds of supplier applications produce only one approval, the application approach or the way suppliers are contacted is likely broken. Approval rates that low are not normal and must be investigated. **

**Silence is not rejection**

- Suppliers are typically busy, and a lack of immediate response does not indicate rejection. Consistent follow-up through the entire 7-day schedule significantly increases approval rates.
- Do not interpret silence as rejection. Assume the supplier is busy with other matters and keep following up every day or every other day by email or phone. *(2023)*
- Treat a silent supplier as a busy schedule. Follow up every few days rather than assuming rejection. **
- Suppliers that go quiet while approval is pending are often busy, not hostile. Follow up on the same channel first. **
- When suppliers stop responding after initial interest, it may mean they were not confident in the store's viability or have internal issues. Following up after getting the first two or three approvals from other suppliers often works better, because the store appears more established. *(2025)*

**Procedure: unresponsive supplier, phone and email**

1. Send a follow-up email to the unresponsive supplier.
2. Find a phone number on the supplier's website or through a Google search.
3. Call the phone number.
4. If the individual contact is unresponsive, email the main support address (support@, sales@, or info@ at the supplier's domain).
5. Continue with other suppliers while you wait. You may retry after getting the first two or three approvals.

**Procedure: follow-up after silence (2019 routine)**

1. Call the supplier rather than relying only on email. **
2. If the call goes to voicemail, leave a message asking for a callback. **
3. If nobody calls back within a week, call again. **
4. Keep following up with calls and voicemails until you get a clear yes or no. **

**Procedure: call at different times of day**

If suppliers consistently do not answer during your usual calling time, try other times of day: early morning (8:30 AM), mid-morning (10 AM), afternoon (2 PM), and late afternoon (4 PM). Small supplier companies often have aligned schedules, scheduled meetings, or lunch breaks that follow their time zone or company culture. *(2023)*

- Call suppliers on business days. If a supplier does not answer, note it and call again on the next business day.

**Procedure: LinkedIn fallback**

1. After following up by email on Wednesday or Thursday and receiving no response by Friday, switch to LinkedIn as an alternative contact method.
2. Search for the supplier company on LinkedIn.
3. Identify employees of that company and review their job titles.
4. If you do not have a LinkedIn account, create one and list yourself as owner of your company.
5. Send a direct message to an identified employee using this text:

Hi [Contact Name], I've been following up for a few weeks now, and we're really excited about listing for your brand. I haven't heard back yet and wanted to make sure my emails are getting through. Are you the right person to contact? If not, could you let me know who I should reach out to? Really appreciate your help, and thanks in advance.


6. Keep a friendly, professional tone throughout, because friendliness increases the likelihood of a positive response.

**Procedure: unresponsive large supplier**

1. Before cutting ties, reply to an email from the contact person. Say that messages seem unanswered and ask whether someone else at the company can be contacted. *(2024)*
2. Call the company again, ask to be connected to another person, and voice your concerns.
3. If the new contact is just as unresponsive, stop selling that supplier's products.

- A supplier whose sales contact does not answer emails or calls for weeks is a serious warning sign for later order processing. Move on, and revisit only after a long time, when the contact may have changed. **
- If a supplier does not answer a sourcing request within a few days, escalate through the account manager on WhatsApp or the public community channel. Public escalation often gets a faster reply than a private message. **
- If an order is delayed or a supplier does not respond, ask the supplier directly through its contact channels and keep your own record of the communication. Do not assume a supplier is reachable just because it is listed. **
- Approval timing from suppliers varies widely, from about an hour to many days. If a supplier does not respond, follow up consistently, since slow responders are often less reliable suppliers. **

**Out-of-stock suppliers**

- If a supplier is out of stock, it will usually tell you during outreach. Do not list its products yet. Work with suppliers that have stock, and circle back to the out-of-stock supplier after a few months. **
- A supplier that is out of stock or pressed by many inquiries: record the contact, ask when to follow up, and record that time. **

### Objections and declines

### "We don't work with new online stores"

- Suppliers often use "we don't work with new online stores" as a **standard rejection** (a routine no). They use it to test whether you are serious and trustworthy.
- **Push back** by asking which thresholds would qualify you for approval. Thresholds are minimum numbers, such as traffic (visitors to your site), Alexa score (a website popularity ranking where a lower number means more popular), or revenue (money earned from sales). **

Procedure when a supplier says it does not work with new online stores:

1. **Respond:** "I totally understand. What would it take for us to get approved?" **
2. The supplier may specify traffic per month, an Alexa score above or below a number, or a minimum revenue. **
3. **Work toward those metrics** rather than accepting "no" as final. **

- Alexa Score (alexa.com) ranks website popularity.
- Some suppliers require a minimum Alexa score to approve new retailers.
- **Research and improve yours** before approaching premium suppliers (top-end, high-price brands). **
- If a supplier says it does not currently work with online retailers, **do not drop the outreach.**
- Use a pitch (a short sales message) built around how dropshipping increases the supplier's sales, online reach and revenue.
- Present the arrangement as a **benefit for both sides.**
- Supplier unfamiliarity with dropshipping was a bigger obstacle in the past than it is now. E-commerce (buying and selling online) has matured.
- **Only spend extra time explaining** the model when a supplier actually does not know it.

### Suppliers that say they are already working with a retailer, or are not taking new retailers

- **Still start the conversation.** Explain how selling with you benefits them. *(2023)*
- Ask to follow up **three to six months** after going live (the store opening to customers), once you have traffic and results. *(2023)*
- Many suppliers approve retailers only in a **second round**, after they see proof of the store's performance. *(2023)*
- Plan for that group. Keep them on your follow-up list. *(2023)*
- When a supplier declines, **end politely with a clear follow-up plan.** For example, say you will get back in touch after launch, once you have traffic and sales. *(2023)*
- Ending on a hard no **closes off a future approval.** A polite follow-up keeps it open. *(2023)*
- If a supplier turns you down because the store is new, you may briefly mention relevant past business experience. Do this only if the conversation leaves room for it, and give no detailed information. *(2023)*
- If the answer is a firm no, **end politely and do not push further.** *(2023)*
- Suppliers that are not accepting new e-commerce accounts are not a dead end.
- **Note the date** they gave for a follow-up.
- Return with sales results or traffic data at that time, since results raise the chance of approval. **
- When a supplier says it will not take new dealers (resellers), **ask when to follow up** and record that time. **

### Capacity-based rejection

Example: "we are not taking on more internet retailers right now".

1. **Do not accept the rejection passively** with a polite goodbye. The supplier will not reach out to you on its own.
2. Present a **launch strategy** (your plan to open the store). It should show what you are doing to start the store: which suppliers you already work with or are onboarding, which marketing channels you will use, and which promotions you will run.
3. Use **fear of missing out (FOMO)** by showing you are about to build a meaningful position in the market. The supplier should not want to be left out of your growth.
4. Use this approach **only if your launch strategy is real** and well researched. A supplier that senses a bluff (a pretend plan) will not be persuaded.

### Standards-based rejection

- A supplier may reject you by saying your store does not meet its standards.
- **Treat this as a signal to improve** the store and your pitch before calling again.
- It is not a final answer.

### MAP policy decline

Hi [Supplier name], thanks for reaching out. Right now we only work with suppliers that enforce their minimum advertised price, so we will not be moving forward with your products at this time. If your MAP policy is enforced across retailers, please let us know and we can revisit this with you.


### Responding during a follow-up call

1. **If they decline, ask what you can do** to make it happen. Show genuine interest in their concerns.
2. Highlight how this project is already benefiting your company. Explain the growth potential of adding their product line.
3. If they continue declining, **ask for the real underlying reason.** State that you genuinely want to understand their hesitation.
4. On your next follow-up call, **address their concerns** with specific solutions or workarounds (other ways to solve the problem).

I totally understand where you're coming from. Here's what I want to make sure you're thinking about though: we're putting together something really significant in your product category, and your brands would be perfect for our customer base. If you decide to pass, you're going to be missing out on what could be really meaningful order volume coming your way.

I genuinely don't want us to lose this opportunity because of process issues or timing. Who do we need to get involved to make this partnership happen? Is there a different decision-maker, or is there a specific approval process we should follow?


- **Expect rejections** from high-ticket dropshipping suppliers (suppliers of expensive products), especially domestic manufacturers (makers in your own country) or distributors (companies that buy from makers and sell to stores).
- Treat them as a **routine part of outreach.** They are not a judgment of you personally.
- Suppliers often refuse new online retailers for reasons unrelated to your store's quality, such as capacity or company policy.
- Some suppliers **refuse at first.** That is part of the normal process. **
- **Work through the list** and keep following up. **
- Some individuals cannot be reasoned with, whatever your professionalism or preparation.
- If a supplier unfairly rejects you despite clear, professional communication, **continue with other suppliers.**
- **Do not take it personally.** For example, a supplier dismissed a demo site (a trial site shown before launch) as a "scam site" after you explained its launch timeline.
- This reflects their attitude, not your business model's viability (whether the business can work).
- When a supplier refuses a new or inexperienced dropshipper, **accept it as normal.** **
- **Do not force the issue.** **

Procedure for refusal by a new dropshipper:

1. **Record the refusal and the date** in your supplier spreadsheet (your list of suppliers). **
2. Once you have traffic and sales from other brands, **reapproach the supplier.** This may take three to six months. **
3. **Show your results** and request approval again. **

- Early refusals from brands to a brand-new store are normal.
- The **minimum of 20 suppliers** gives you enough chances for approvals.

Handling a refusal by an online-only reseller (a seller that sells only online): *(2025)*

1. **Ask what would change their mind.** Examples: a traffic level, a sales level, or a business history requirement.
2. Record the answer as a note on the supplier tracking sheet.
3. Ask whether you may **follow up after a set period**, for example three months after launch, to share results and check whether their policy has changed.
- Treat the refusal as **"not yet" rather than "no".**

- Newer stores are often rejected, or told to return once they have more traffic or more time in business.
- **Keep applying to other suppliers.** Approval rates vary, and early rejection is normal. *(2023)*
- A supplier may postpone approval until a new store reaches a set level of sales or traffic. This is a normal outcome.
- **Ask for the exact threshold.**
- Keep pursuing approvals with other suppliers in the meantime.
- Some suppliers approve only after the store has sales, traffic and business history (a record of past trading).
- Follow those up later **rather than dropping them.**
- **Ask for the reason for each rejection.** **
- Suppliers that decline early often approve later, once the store shows more traffic or revenue.
- **Re-contact them with proof.** *(2025)*

### Reasons for rejection and the next step

### Next step by reason (2020 routine)

1. If the supplier is busy (for example during the holiday season), **call back after that period.** **
2. If the supplier requires proof of revenue or traffic, **follow up once you reach those thresholds.** **
3. If the supplier says it is not opening new accounts, **follow up every month.** Ask for another contact, restate your interest, and repeat the original pitch. **
4. Keep detailed notes of every supplier conversation. This keeps the follow-up well timed and consistent. **

### Minimum requirements

- A common reason for rejection is a minimum requirement, such as an Alexa score above 100,000, or around **$20,000** in expected monthly business.
- A new store usually cannot meet these.
- A large company will rarely make exceptions, however well you pitch.
- Some suppliers only authorize stores with at least **10,000 monthly visitors.**
- Others require at least **20,000 euros** in monthly revenue.
- **Record these requirements** in the supplier notes, so you can follow up when the store reaches them.
- Reference point from 2025: about **20,000 monthly visitors** and around **200,000 USD** monthly revenue as proof when re-contacting.
- If a supplier requires a minimum Alexa ranking under 100,000 or a marketing plan, **note the requirement.**
- Respond that the threshold will be met.
- **Reapply once the store reaches that traffic level.** **
- Some suppliers approve only at a set level of sales or traffic. For example, the approval threshold can be **10,000 monthly visitors** or **$50,000 in revenue.** *(2025)*
- A supplier that imposes minimums can still become a customer later.
- **Mark it as a follow-up target.**
- **Return once you have proof** of the required numbers, rather than dropping it.

### Procedure: handling minimum requirements

1. **Ask each supplier** that gives a minimum requirement exactly what it needs: analytics access (a view of your traffic reports), screenshots, Alexa score, or monthly sales volume.
2. Record these requirements in your supplier spreadsheet next to that supplier's name.
3. Keep working on the other suppliers on your list while you build toward the stated numbers.
4. When you reach the required numbers, **contact the same person** you spoke to before. Remind them of the earlier conversation, send proof such as screenshots, and ask who to speak with about setting up your account.

### Procedure: convert a rejection into an approval (2025)

1. **Prepare a list of 20 or more brand suppliers** during niche selection (choosing your product market), to account for rejections.
2. When a supplier says no or claims it does not work with online stores, understand that this means it **lacks trust in the store yet.**
3. Ask: "What would it take for us to eventually sell for you? Do you require a certain sales volume or monthly traffic?"
4. **Record the supplier's specific approval threshold,** for example 10,000 monthly visitors or $50,000 in revenue.
5. When that threshold is reached, **follow up with the original contact person.** Reference the previous conversation and the new metrics (your new numbers).
6. Use this systematic (step-by-step, repeatable) follow-up to convert rejections into approvals.

### Procedure: handling a first rejection caused by being unprepared

1. Note the supplier in your supplier list, along with the reason for the rejection.
2. Prepare properly, including research and a clear pitch.
3. **Call again roughly one month later.**

### Procedure: refusal with no reason given (2024)

1. Note the reason for the refusal and when to follow up.
2. Keep working the rest of the supplier list.
3. **Follow up after three months,** and again three months later if the supplier still refuses.
4. The goal is **approval from every supplier.**

### Recontacting after growth

### When to recontact

- Suppliers who turned you down earlier can be contacted again once: the store is live, real products are sold, other brands have approved you, and traffic systems are running.
- Name current supplier relationships when it fits the conversation.
- Recontact timing differs by source. **Use the number the supplier gave you first:**
  - Supplier-stated window: **follow up on that date**
  - **3-6 months** after going live, once you have traffic and results *(2023)*
  - **Every 3 months** after a refusal with no criteria, with an update on growth *(2023)*
  - **Every 3-6 months,** with the reason noted and concrete progress shared **
  - **3 months** after refusal, then again 3 months later if still refused *(2024)*
- Track each declined supplier with its reason and a dated follow-up.
- Each rejection has a **re-contact date,** such as 3-6 months later.

### Procedure: recontact a supplier that declined the store (2020 routine)

Procedure for a supplier that refuses a new dealer: **

1. Accept the refusal as normal.
2. Record the refusal and date in your supplier spreadsheet.
3. Once you have traffic and sales from other brands, **reapproach the supplier.** This may take three to six months.
4. **Show your results** and request approval again.

### Procedure: reapproach an out-of-stock or busy supplier (2022)

1. Note the contact date, person and outcome in the supplier sourcing worksheet.
2. When they say they are not taking new dealers, **ask when to follow up** and record that time.
3. On follow-up, call the same contact. State how the store launched, its sales progress, and the brands already sold. Also state your desire to add them.

### Follow-up call after launch (about 2-3 weeks after the date given)

1. **Plan the follow-up call for 2-3 weeks** after your stated launch date.
2. Call and attempt to reach the same person from your initial call. Use their name to remind them of your conversation.
3. Ask how their business has been. Bring up anything important from the first conversation.
4. Tell them your launch **exceeded your team's expectations.** Say you are even more excited about the project.
5. Mention that you wanted to call sooner, but needed time to smooth out internal systems and processes (your daily ways of working).
6. Share that your initial results **beat your projections** (your forecasts).
7. Add that all your other supplier partners are equally thrilled with their product performance.
8. Inform them you are **scaling up your advertising campaigns** significantly.
9. **Ask directly** if they are ready to partner with you now.
10. If they decline, ask what you can do to make it happen. Show genuine interest in their concerns.
11. Highlight how this project is already benefiting your company. Explain the growth potential of adding their product line.
12. If they continue declining, ask for the real underlying reason. State that you genuinely want to understand their hesitation.
13. On your next follow-up call, **address their concerns** with specific solutions or workarounds.

### Ongoing outreach routine

### Aim and pacing

- The **first five suppliers** to approve you is your first goal. The real aim is approval from every supplier on your master list (your full list of suppliers to contact), including the gold-tier brands (the top-level, most prized brands).
- Do not sort or prioritize suppliers when reaching out. **Contact all of them.**
- As soon as a supplier approves you, **upload** its products to your store (add them to your online shop), then return to outreach for the next supplier. Alternate between outreach and product uploads until every supplier on the list has been worked through.
- When a supplier approves you, move to the product upload step. **Upload** that supplier's live products (the ones currently for sale) to your store, then return to the contact plan and continue working down the list.
- Approval rates differ by niche (the market your products belong to) and store maturity (how established your store is). Expect a **partial approval rate** with a new store, meaning only some suppliers say yes.
- Repeated contact with suppliers is part of the routine. **Do not stop after one attempt.**

- Plan supplier outreach around holiday closures using the closure plan in the holiday section.
- After submitting a supplier application, **call the next day** in a friendly way to confirm they received it. *(2023)*
- Supplier approvals: **call during normal business weeks**, since the decision maker is available then. **

### Daily routine sequence

4. If a supplier approves you, **upload** its products, then return to the list.
5. **Follow up** on warm leads (people who showed interest) before contacting the rest of the list.
6. **Keep** the Master Supplier list current so no supplier falls out of the rotation (the cycle of contacts).

### Supplier lists and the offline period

- Expect **slower replies** around Black Friday through Christmas and New Year, when many suppliers close for a break. *(2025)*
- **Build** a complete supplier contact list while offline, so you can start calling the moment you are back online.

### Follow-up cadence

- **Follow up** at a steady pace until the supplier gives a clear answer, rather than treating a single unanswered message as the end.
- Supplier relationship calls should not be **outsourced** (handed to someone else), because relationships are central to this business. If time is short, send emails during the day with a scheduling tool, such as the Boomerang Gmail extension (an add-on for Gmail that schedules emails), so they arrive at a business-hours time.

### Unresponsive suppliers

- A supplier can go silent (stop replying) after an account setup or order. This is a form of ghosting (going silent on you). If a supplier does not respond, **escalate** (take it to a higher contact) to their sales or distribution contact early.
- If a supplier stops answering after being responsive, **keep following up** instead of giving up. Silence often comes from busy periods, such as holiday weekends or back-to-school season. (2025.)

### Holiday and seasonal timing

- Silence during holiday weekends and back-to-school season is often caused by busy periods, not by a refusal. So **keep following up** rather than closing the lead (the possible deal).

### Objections and declines

- Expect that not every supplier will approve a new reseller account (an account that lets you buy and resell their products). So **contact several suppliers** at the same time. In one case, one supplier approved within two minutes while the next one refused. A refusal alone is not a reason to stop.
- If one supplier declines, tell competing suppliers that demand for their products is high and that you have turned down sales for their brand. Once you have one supplier, word of mouth (people talking about you) in the industry, especially in niche markets, often brings in other suppliers who reach out to you.

### Paused supply and reasons for rejection

- When a supplier pauses new retailers because of inventory (stock on hand) or supply chain (the path products take from maker to seller) problems, work through this procedure:
  1. **Ask** the supplier when its inventory is expected to stabilize (become steady again), and record the answer in your tracking sheet.
  2. **Follow up** on that date, or at the interval the supplier gives.
  3. Meanwhile, **source** backup suppliers from a trade show directory (a list of suppliers met at trade shows, which are industry fairs), such as TSNN.com (an online trade show directory).

### Re-contacting after growth

- A "we are not taking new retailers right now" answer usually means the supplier wants more authority, traffic (visitors to your store), sales and a track record first. **Return later** with a live store and history, and ask again. (2023.)
- Use this template when reopening a conversation that was declined earlier:

Hi [supplier contact name], this is [your name] from [store name]. We spoke on [date of last contact] and you mentioned you were not taking on new online retailers at that time. Since then we have [specific progress, e.g. grown traffic by X percent, added channel Y, reached Z email subscribers]. We are still very excited to list your products and would love to reopen the conversation. When would be a good time to talk, or when do you expect to take on new retailers?


### Tracking and ongoing outreach routine

- **Record** every supplier answer, including any expected restock or reopening date (when stock comes back or they open to new stores), in a tracking sheet. The paused-supply procedure and the follow-up dates depend on it.
- Keep supplier outreach as a recurring routine. **Follow up** on the recorded dates, escalate silent contacts early, and keep parallel contacts open (talking to several suppliers at once).
