High Probability relationship building and discovery

Section 21.20 · 20 of 26 in this chapter · 7 min read

In short

  • Do not talk about your product in the first meeting. First learn who the person is and whether you trust him.
  • If you do not trust him, leave politely. Do not spend time on someone you do not trust.
  • Listen more than you talk. Keep your talking to 25 percent of the meeting.
  • Ask the discovery questions in the same order, in the same words, every time.
  • Do not move on until an unclear answer is fully clear.
This section has 8 parts. Tap one to open it.

Relationship phase: purpose and sequence

1 min

  • Do not talk about your product during the relationship phase (the early part where you get to know the person, before any sales talk).

  • Your purpose is to learn three things:

    • Who the prospect (a possible customer) is.
    • What motivates him.
    • How he reached his current role.

  • Use this to decide whether you trust and respect him.

  • If you do not trust him, leave politely, even if you cannot yet say why.

  • The relationship phase usually happens in the first meeting.

  • There is no fixed script or question list.

  • Let the conversation give you the information you need.

  • So the exact questions depend on what the prospect says.

  • Prospects also judge you during this phase.

  • Trust shows in how you behave, not in what you say.

  • Trust and respect give you an advantage over competitors (other sellers).

  • Most buyers prefer to deal with people they trust.

  • Do not stay long in the relationship phase if he has not shown trust or respect.

  • Leaving early costs little.

  • Investing time in someone you do not trust wastes the rest of the process.

When the prospect wants to skip the relationship phase

1 min

Use this in a flat, unemotional tone (calm and neutral, with no excitement) when a prospect in the relationship phase wants to jump straight into business:

Before we talk about my product, I like to learn how my customers operate, because that tells me how to serve them. I also need to find out whether I trust and respect the people I work with, and you have the same chance to judge me. So, are you willing to continue this kind of conversation, or would you rather I leave?
  • If he chooses to leave, end the visit politely.
  • Treat him as a low-probability prospect (someone unlikely to buy).

Opening the relationship

1 min

  • To open the relationship, make a neutral observation (a calm, factual comment) about something visible or known.

  • Good subjects are the building, the location or the company.

  • Attach a question to that observation.

  • Base each follow-up on the prospect's last answer, not on a fixed list.

    • Example: The loading area here is unusually wide. Was it built for this kind of product flow?
    • Example: You mentioned the new site opened last spring. What made you move the team there?

  • Use open-ended questions (questions that need more than yes or no) while building the relationship.

  • Examples: why, what did you do, how did you handle that, and was that something you wanted to do.

  • Open questions let the prospect explain himself.

  • Prospects tend to answer almost any authentic (genuine) question truthfully.

  • This happens when they sense you really want the answer and are not manipulating them.

  • Sincere interest cannot be faked.

  • Prospects will cut a conversation short if they sense you are only going through the motions (acting, not caring).

  • If he shares something personal, do not answer with unrelated small talk (light chat, such as about family or hobbies).

  • That shows you are not really interested, and it damages trust.

  • Instead, follow up on what he just said, such as asking why it happened or how he handled it.

Listening and note-taking

1 min

  • Ask a question, then listen to the full answer before you think about your next question.

  • If an answer is incomplete, ask a follow-up to clarify it for yourself and sometimes for the prospect.

  • Keep your own talking to about 25 percent of the meeting.

  • If you talk more than that, the meeting is probably going badly.

  • Ask why the prospect is unresponsive.

  • End the meeting if you cannot fix it, unless he is naturally quiet.

  • Take complete notes at every contact.

  • Write down the prospect's facts and opinions in his exact words.

  • Exact wording matters later when you define his requirements (what he needs).

  • It also shows him that you value what he says.

  • Missing details from a first meeting are hard to recover later.

Handling negativity

1 min

Deal with anger, sarcasm (mocking remarks), threats, abuse or any other negativity (hostile behavior) before anything else. Speak in a flat, neutral tone, without judging and without sympathy.

  1. Say neutrally that he seems upset.
  2. If he is still upset after explaining why, repeat the observation in the same flat tone, without stressing any word.
  3. If he is still upset, suggest that you discuss the matter at another time.
  4. If he calms down, continue the conversation. If he becomes upset again, return to step 1 and keep your tone even.
  5. If he stays upset, tell him you are not willing to meet right now, because it would not serve either of you. Then ask whether he wants to schedule another appointment or not.
  • Do not respond with anger to a low-probability prospect.
  • He is simply not a fit.
  • Recognize it, end the meeting politely and move on to the next prospect.

Discovery and disqualification questions

2 min

Discovery questions find out what the person really needs. To disqualify him means to rule him out as a buyer.

Use the wording below in this fixed order. Swap "this product" for your own product or service. Do not mention your company by name.

  1. Need: Why do you need this product?
  2. Want: Do you want it? If yes, ask why; if no, end the meeting.
  3. Financial: This product will cost about [price]; are you prepared to spend that?
  4. Time: If you decide to proceed, when would you want this to start, and what happens if it doesn't start then?
  5. Decision-makers: When you make a decision like this, who do you usually talk it over with? Then ask whether he'll let you speak with them before you prepare a proposal.
  6. Authority: If you went ahead, who else would have to agree? Handle this the same way as decision-makers.
  7. Commitments: What would happen if you don't buy this product?
  8. Brand preference: If you had to decide right now, without talking to anyone, which brand would you buy?
  9. Supplier preference: Is there someone you'd rather do business with?
  10. Internal procedure: What is your process for issuing a purchase order (the official form a company uses to buy)? Have him write it down and read it back before you move on.
  11. Personal motivation: What would it mean to you personally if you didn't purchase this product?
  12. Personal prejudices: Is there any reason you wouldn't want to do business with me? Anything we haven't covered yet, or any emotional reason?
  13. Hidden obstacles: What could be lurking in the background that would prevent this from happening?
  14. Close (the final question that asks for the sale): Once questions 1 to 13 are answered satisfactorily and he hasn't disqualified himself, ask: if I can meet all your criteria for this product, what will you do?

  • Discovery questions are general, not about your brand. So the answers describe the prospect's own needs.

  • Ask each question in the exact same words every time.

  • Write down the answers precisely, since the wording and tone of his answers can matter later.

  • The prospect can disqualify himself at any of the fourteen questions.

  • Each answer he gives without disqualifying himself builds the relationship and the mutual commitment.

  • Each problem you handle early prevents a deal-breaker (a problem that kills the sale) later.

  • Do not disqualify a prospect after an objection (a reason he gives against buying), such as a price limit, without first asking why he holds that position.

  • The reason may be solvable.

  • Asking why is better than guessing at his thinking.

Decision-makers and approvers

1 min

A decision-maker is the person who makes the final choice. An approver is the person who must sign off. Before you prepare a proposal (a written offer), request to speak with the decision-maker or approver.

I need to talk with [name or role] before I prepare a proposal. Are you willing to arrange that?

If he refuses, reply in a flat tone:

That's not the way I work. I won't prepare a proposal without understanding their concerns and objectives, because I don't want a proposal rejected over something we could have handled up front. What would you like to do?
  • If he still will not arrange it, disqualify him.

Unclear, evasive and conflicting answers

1 min

When an answer is unclear or evasive (it avoids a straight answer), do not move on until you are fully satisfied.

  1. Ask for clarification. For example, say you do not understand what he meant, and ask what he means.
  2. If he is still unclear, say that his answer seems evasive, and ask whether there is a problem.
  3. If he still avoids answering, tell him you will not move on until the question is answered.
  4. Carry on only when you have a clear answer. An unresolved issue usually returns later as a problem.
  • When a prospect gives conflicting timing (for example, urgent on the phone but six months away in the meeting), ask him directly about the discrepancy (the mismatch) before going further.
  • Then either reach a clear commitment for the timing, or set a specific return date that he chooses.
