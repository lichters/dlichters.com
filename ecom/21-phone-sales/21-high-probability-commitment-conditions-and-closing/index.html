High Probability commitment, conditions and closing

Section 21.21 · 21 of 26 in this chapter · 8 min read

In short

  • Ask questions first. Show the product or price only after the buyer agrees to buy if it meets his conditions.
  • Closing means asking for a clear yes or no. Never push for the order.
  • Accept either answer. A no is fine.
  • Make every condition measurable, such as a time limit or a signed sample. If the buyer will not commit, stop working with him.

Sequence of the Sale

1 min
  • Do not show a product, quote, or proposal (a written offer) yet.
  • Show it only after the buyer has committed to buy, if the product meets his stated conditions.
  • The order is: questions first, then agreement on conditions, then commitment, then the demonstration or proposal.
  • Selling here means reaching a series of agreements.
  • The prospect (the potential buyer) first agrees that he needs the product, wants it, and can afford it.
  • He then commits to buy at a set time, if his conditions are met.
  • Selling is not about persuading anyone to act.
  • A deal exists only when both sides have agreed.

Closing Through Mutual Commitment

1 min
  • Closing (the final step, where the buyer answers yes or no) means negotiating mutual commitments.
  • Never ask for the order.
  • The prospect chooses yes or no.
  • You are equally satisfied with either answer.
  • Your commitment is to the process, not to making a sale.
  • Ask only questions whose answers you need.
  • Avoid rhetorical questions (asked for effect, not for an answer) and trial closes (small test questions that push toward a sale).
  • This includes small either-or choices meant to push a sale.
  • Prospects recognize these tactics and resent them.
  • They undermine trust.
  • Traditional selling offers a choice of yes or yes.
  • The seller argues for a sale no matter the prospect's view.
  • High Probability selling (a sales method built on mutual commitment) offers a choice of yes or no.
  • It accepts either answer.
  • That is why it produces commitments that hold.
  • Example of a real decision: "Does a one-week delivery window work for you, or not?"
  • Compare "Shall we get you set up?", which only invites a reluctant yes. (Own illustration.)

Defining the Conditions of Satisfaction

2 min

Discovery means the early questions that find out what the buyer needs. Conditions of Satisfaction means the exact list of requirements the buyer sets, every one of them. Open the conditions phase with this template once discovery is complete:

It is time to define your Conditions of Satisfaction. I would like us to write down exactly what you require, every requirement, because that is how I determine whether we can provide it. Are you willing to do that?

After he agrees, read back what he said during discovery and ask whether it is correct and whether he wants to add anything.

Establish the Conditions of Satisfaction in this order:

  1. Confirm that the discovery questions are fully answered. Check that every decision-maker (the person who decides) and approver (the person who signs off) is involved.
  2. Tell the prospect that you now need to define exactly what he requires. Ask whether he is willing to do that.
  3. Ask what he will do if you return with a proposal that meets his conditions.
  4. If he does not give a clear commitment, tell him that you will only prepare a proposal with a commitment to buy if the conditions are met. Disqualify him (stop working with him) if he refuses.
  5. Once he commits, read back what he said during discovery. Ask whether it is correct and whether he wants to add anything.
  6. Ask for each condition in his exact words and record them. Raise any conditions he has not mentioned that need addressing.
  7. Specify exactly what he will and will not accept. Agree how each condition will be measured, such as a timed test or a signed sample.
  8. Tell him clearly what you can and cannot provide. Offer alternatives where possible, without hiding the negatives.
  9. Negotiate any conditions you cannot meet. If an unmet condition cannot be negotiated, disqualify him. If you agree on conditions you can meet, you have a deal.
  10. At the next meeting, present a proposal that shows you meet all conditions, and complete the transaction.

Making Conditions Measurable

1 min
  • Make each condition measurable so both sides can check it.
  • "Easy to set up" becomes a time limit for a shipping clerk to set up the package, including taping.
  • "Vivid four-color art" becomes a signed sample of the minimum quality the buyer will accept.

References

1 min

When a prospect wants references (reviews from existing customers):

  1. Ask how many references he wants.
  2. Ask whether he will call those references while you are present.
  3. Ask what he will do if the references give positive endorsements (strong praise).
  4. Record the agreed answers in his exact words as a condition to be met.

Budgetary Quotes

1 min
  • When you present a budgetary quote (a rough price estimate, not a firm offer) to a prospect who has not committed, ask in the same breath what he will do if the price is acceptable.
  • This ties the estimate to a real commitment.
  • Without it, you send a free number he can shop around with.

Testing the Commitment

1 min
  • After a prospect commits, test that commitment.
  • Ask whether he is sure, and why he wants to proceed.
  • His reasons often come out, and the answer strengthens his commitment.
  • Testing also brings hidden doubts into the open before you leave.

Handling Requests You Cannot Meet

1 min

When a prospect asks for something you cannot deliver, such as a finish (a surface coating) you do not offer:

  1. State plainly that you do not offer it, and give the reason in one sentence.
  2. Offer your available option, and ask whether he will accept it.
  3. If he insists on the unavailable option, end the meeting instead of hoping he changes his mind.

Use this template for follow-up replies to question 14. Deliver it in a flat tone (calm and neutral, with no pressure), depending on his response:

Follow-up replies to question 14 are delivered in a flat tone, depending on his response. If he answers 'What do you mean?', reply: What do you think I mean? If he says he will seriously consider it, reply: I'm not willing to prepare a proposal unless I have a commitment that we will do business if I meet your criteria. What would you like to do? Unless he clearly states that he will give you his business if you meet his criteria, disqualify him.

Pushback and Disqualification

1 min
  • Commitments tend to hold when they are freely made.
  • A prospect who says yes will usually honor it.
  • An order that rests on pressure is likely to be cancelled.
  • A prospect who breaks a commitment should be disqualified in most markets.
  • In markets with few buyers, you may have to keep working with him.
  • When a prospect pushes back hard against being disqualified, still disqualify him if you have real doubts about his commitment.
  • A serious buyer rarely accepts disqualification without a struggle.
  • But the struggle alone does not prove that he is qualified.

Common Mistakes

1 min
  • Do not move from discovery straight to the prospect's requests without first asking for commitment.
  • Otherwise the prospect takes the lead, you end up doing what he asks, and the process loses its structure.
  • If you find yourself answering the prospect's questions instead of asking your own, go back to asking questions and asking for commitment.
  • It is harder to regain control once you have lost it.
  • But a commitment request can still restore it.
  • Do not assume the sale is made because discovery went well or the prospect seems friendly.
  • Until he commits in writing or aloud to the conditions, he has not committed.
  • Acting as though he has leads to lost orders.
  • Do not try to convince a prospect to change his mind after he has declined a commitment or set a limit you cannot meet.
  • Ending the meeting saves the effort.
  • Trying to talk him out of his conditions creates resistance.

Worked Cases

2 min
  • Toy manufacturer, testing the commitment: A seller for a large toy manufacturer reviewed the buyer's conditions.
    • He then asked what the buyer would do if a demonstration on the following Tuesday met all of them.
    • The buyer gave a noncommittal reply, meaning no clear yes.
    • The seller said he would not prepare a proposal without a commitment, and that a no was acceptable.
    • After the buyer pushed back, the seller held his position calmly.
    • The buyer then committed to an order if the demonstration met the conditions.
    • The seller asked whether he was sure, then asked why.
    • The buyer gave his reasons and said there was no doubt that he would order if the conditions were met.
  • Packaging supplier, existing buyer for Star: A packaging supplier had an existing buyer for a brand called Star.
    • He learned that a rival supplier was failing on another brand, Sun.
    • Sun had 17 packages needing new artwork (new printed designs).
    • The seller did not pitch. He asked about the problem and what the buyer needed.
    • The product manager wanted four packages within five weeks.
    • He said the other 13 could come later, with an expected lead time (time from order to delivery) of 8 to 15 weeks.
    • The seller agreed to complete artwork for the four in one week.
    • He agreed to deliver them four weeks after artwork approval.
    • He agreed to fax proofs (test printouts to approve) every morning, because the work would run on overtime.
    • He agreed to quote the price the next day, in line with the Star line.
    • The product manager was not going to cancel his existing order.
    • He promised at least half of his packaging requirements if delivery was met.
    • He required top quality, on-time delivery, no shortages (missing units), and no more than 2 percent overage (extra units beyond the order).
    • The buyer's purchasing manager (the person who buys for the company) signed the first order the next day, after the product manager approved the quote.
  • Advertising manager, point-of-sale packaging: A seller had established a relationship with an advertising manager.
    • He asked whether the packaging would be worth it if it gave attractive point-of-sale advertising (ads at the place of sale) for about one dollar more per unit.
    • The buyer asked about the cost, then asked for samples.
    • The seller said samples of similar packaging could be shown.
    • He said custom samples would only be made against a commitment.
    • He offered to credit the cost of a sample run against the first production order.
    • The buyer agreed. The two wrote the purchase requisition (an internal form to buy) together.
    • While writing it, the seller went through each condition the buyer had for the packaging. This confirmed the order.
