High Probability prospecting calls

Section 21.19 · 19 of 26 in this chapter · 7 min read

In short

  • Work in three steps, in this order: find possible buyers, then sell, then close the deal.
  • Keep your opening offer under 45 words, and say it calmly and neutrally.
  • Ask questions instead of pitching. If you feel stress or the call goes past 3 to 4 minutes, stop pitching.
  • Treat a "no" as a clear answer and end the call politely.
  • Set a meeting only with someone who wants your offer and plans to buy.

Where prospecting fits in the approach

  • The approach has three phases, in this order: High Probability Prospecting, High Probability Selling, and High Probability Closing. These are the three steps of the method.
    • A prospect is a possible buyer. Prospecting means finding them with a short phone contact that states a clear offer.
    • Let prospects remove themselves (called disqualifying yourself) if they are not a fit.
    • Set a meeting only with someone who wants the offer and plans to buy.
    • Selling means first building trust and respect in a relationship phase.
    • Then ask the discovery questions (questions that learn what the prospect needs) and the disqualification questions (questions that show whether he is not a fit), in fixed order.
    • The prospect may end the process at any point.
    • Closing means defining the prospect's Conditions of Satisfaction (the things he needs to see before he says yes).
    • Get a commitment to buy if those conditions are met.
    • Only then present a proposal (a written offer with the details) or a demonstration (showing the product working).
  • There are usually far more prospects than a sales team can visit. At any given time, only a small share are ready to buy.
    • Contact as many prospects as possible, briefly, to find that small group.
    • Spend meeting time only on that small group.

Writing the opening offer

  • Keep the offer under 45 words, including the greeting and the introduction.
    • Write it in your own words so that it makes sense to you.
  • Deliver it in a neutral, unemotional tone. A convincing tone creates resistance, even when the words are right.
  • Benchmarks (reference numbers from real practice) show how this works in the field:
    • One seller's offer went through three rounds of trial calls and settled at 34 words.
    • It was later sharpened further based on prospect responses.
    • Another seller cut a 59-word offer to 42 words and saw better results.
    • Shorter offers generally perform better.
  • Opening offer template for a cold call (a call to someone who did not ask for it), filled in and kept under 45 words:
Hello, this is [your name] from [company name]. We [concrete description of what you provide, with one specific benefit or measurable result, e.g. the product type and what it does for the buyer]. Is that something you want for [the prospect's product line, business or situation]?

Deliver it in a neutral tone and take the prospect's answer as it comes.

  • Formula for a cold-call offer:
    1. Name yourself and your company.
    2. State concretely what you provide.
    3. Add one specific benefit, coverage detail, or measurable result.
    4. Ask a yes-or-no question about whether the prospect wants it.
  • The question keeps the prospect in control. It also makes a clear no easy.
  • Own examples of the formula:
    • This is Mark from Cleanline Office Services. We clean commercial kitchens after hours, which in our clients' experience cuts grease-related inspection failures roughly in half. Is that something you want for your kitchen?
    • This is Lena from Pressgrid Print. We produce custom labels in runs of 500 to 5,000 with a proof sent the same day. Is that something you want for your product line?

Running the call

  • Follow this sequence on a High Probability prospecting call:
    1. State your offer clearly in no more than 45 words, counting the greeting and your introduction.
    2. Check whether the prospect wants what you are offering.
    3. If he says yes, ask whether he would like to set up a meeting aimed at doing business.
    4. If he is not ready at that point, book a new call for a time he chooses. Get his commitment to move forward then.
    5. End the call on any negative answer.
    6. Arrange a callback (a later call) only if he states he will be a qualified prospect (someone who wants it and can buy) at a specific later time.
    7. Make clear that it is completely fine for him to say no whenever he hesitates.
    8. Disqualify anyone who does not want your offer or will not buy it from you.
  • Two signs that you have slipped into selling on a prospecting call:
    • You feel stress during the call.
    • The call runs longer than 3 to 4 minutes.
  • When either happens, stop pitching. Return to asking questions.

Qualifying questions

  • Collect the following from the prospect before any appointment, in this order:
    1. Does he want this product?
    2. Does he want to arrange an appointment?
    3. If not now, when?
    4. If not now, does he want you to check back later?
    5. Why does he want to meet at this time?
    6. What would he like to accomplish at the meeting?
  • Add further disqualifying questions when the situation calls for them.

Handling vague and negative answers

  • If a prospect answers with "that sounds interesting", "maybe", "I don't know", "I don't think so" or "I'll consider it", reply in a flat tone (calm, without emotion):
Does that mean you want what I'm offering, or not?
  • Treat a no as a clear answer. Close the call politely.
    • Do not argue.
    • Do not add benefits.
    • Do not sell harder.
  • If a prospect asks a question you cannot answer well yet, such as why he should switch when he is happy with his current supplier, use one of these replies in a flat tone:
I don't have a satisfactory answer to that yet. What answer would make sense to you?
It doesn't sound like this is a good fit for you. Is my offer something you want, or not?

Choose the reply that fits the situation. Then let the prospect answer.

  • A statement that does not answer your question, such as "I'm not interested", is noise (talk that carries no real answer). Do not respond with a sales pitch.
    • Ask a clarifying question he can answer, such as whether he is the person who decides on this.
    • Keep the conversation on your questions.
    • Disqualify him only when he clearly says he does not need, want, or can afford the offer.
  • Interest has no sales value, because it is not a commitment.
    • Enthusiasm is a better sign of a qualified prospect than polite interest.
    • Direct, quick answers are a better sign than polite interest.
  • Ask yes-or-no questions that include the option of no. For example: is this what he wants or not?
    • A clear option to decline reduces resistance.
    • It makes the prospect responsible for his own choice.

Information requests and materials

  • If a prospect asks you to send information, reply in a neutral tone:
When someone asks for information, it often means the conversation is ending politely. If that is your intention, just tell me and that's fine. If you do want the details, tell me what you would need to decide so I can see whether it makes sense to send them.
  • Send materials only when all three are true:
    • The prospect wants the offer.
    • He needs the information.
    • He is likely to buy after reviewing it.
  • Do not send unsolicited (not asked for) brochures or samples to cold prospects.
    • Keep unsolicited mail for a mass mailing (one message sent to many people) sent two or three times a year.
    • Send individual material only to prospects who have confirmed interest.

Finding the right contact

  • When you need the right person at a company, first ask the front desk for whoever handles the product area, such as packaging design.
    • Many switchboards (the company's phone operators) only release the marketing or sales manager's name.
    • Ask for that person. Let them point you to the correct contact.
    • If you still cannot reach the decision-maker (the person who decides), the sales department is a usable alternative route.
  • In companies with several product lines, each line's product manager often owns packaging or purchasing decisions for that line.
    • Identify the manager of each line separately.
    • Do not rely on one central contact.

Scheduling the meeting

  • Never try to sell the appointment.
    • Ask whether he wants to meet to see if your offer fits his needs.
    • Then let him propose the time.
    • Ask him what he wants to accomplish.
    • A prospect who does not commit to a meeting has disqualified himself.
  • Scheduling a sales meeting by phone:
    1. Set a tentative date (a date that is not yet final) for the meeting. Usually a few weeks ahead, if the prospect names that timing.
    2. Ask what the meeting should accomplish and whether the decision-maker should attend.
    3. Call the day before to confirm the time and the attendees.
    4. If the prospect does not confirm, treat the appointment as disqualified. Do not chase it.

Ending a call with a disqualified prospect

  • Close politely. Do not argue.
  • Say you will only call again if new developments fit his stated needs.
  • Do not try to convert him.
