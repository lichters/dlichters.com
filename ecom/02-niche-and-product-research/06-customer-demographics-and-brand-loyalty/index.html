Customer demographics and brand loyalty

Section 2.6 · 6 of 24 in this chapter · 27 min read

This section has 21 parts. Tap one to open it.

Target segment and why it matters

1 min
  • In Source A X, target the upper middle class exclusively. A demographic is a particular sector of the population, so the niche must appeal to a specific segment, not everyone.
  • Decide which income segment a niche serves before researching it.
  • A target market should be a small segment of a country's population. Even in the US, with about 330 million people, pick a specific subsection to sell to.
  • Demographics fall into three broad income classes: lower income, upper middle class and wealthy. These classes apply across most product types and online businesses, anywhere in the world.
  • Target only the upper middle class, not lower-income households and not wealthy buyers.
  • Niche ideas must appeal to upper middle class or affluent buyers and have low brand loyalty to be suitable for dropshipping research.
  • The best niche sits in a sweet spot where product price and target demographic match, typically the upper middle class at a mid-range price.
  • Niche quality depends on many combined criteria, not on any single factor.
  • Demographic rules have exceptions: some people outside their usual income bracket will still buy at any price point.
  • Some niches are highly saturated, so a sample product type should be treated as an illustration of the method, not a recommendation to enter that market.
  • Demographics are the first thing to get right: the right offer shown to the wrong group will fail.

Why the upper middle class is the sweet spot

1 min
  • Upper middle class buyers have strong disposable income and need less pre-selling than other demographics, with fewer calls and emails before purchase.
  • They complain less, return less and need less customer service, so operations stay low-maintenance for a lifestyle business with limited time.
  • They are very comfortable buying high-ticket products online without hand-holding or consultation.
  • They already shop online regularly, have realistic expectations and buy without messaging the seller first.
  • Their purchases are routine rather than a major decision, which keeps interaction with the seller low.
  • Households in this group are accustomed to online shopping, have discretionary spending capacity and make substantial online purchases without significant price resistance.
  • Wealthy buyers also have disposable income, but the lower interaction costs of upper middle class buyers outweigh this advantage.
  • The upper middle class label describes the level of service a customer needs, not a price range. It does not apply to business buyers, who are used to spending money.

Why lower-income and wealthy buyers fail

2 min

Lower income

  • Lower-income buyers spend a large share of their resources on purchases, which creates very high expectations despite low prices.
  • This leads to higher returns and customer support costs, with minimal profit margins and financial reward.
  • Expensive products sold to them lead to heavy pre-selling, frequent complaints, more service work and more returns, because a large purchase takes a big share of their savings.
  • If lower-income buyers are targeted at all, the product should be less expensive to limit these costs.
  • A product around $200 appeals to college students or budget-limited families and usually has lower quality. Buyers who spend most of their money or use credit on it have high expectations, more back-and-forth contact and a much higher return rate.
  • Avoid the lowest-priced tier of any niche, such as budget sleeper sofas rather than quality leather couches, where lower-income customers concentrate. This tier produces significantly more inquiries, complaints and returns that demand constant management.

Wealthy

  • Wealthy buyers expect high-priced custom or luxury products to be perfect and require significant pre-selling: emails, calls and hand-holding.
  • Returns are frequent if the product is not perfect, which makes this segment unsuitable for low-maintenance lifestyle businesses.
  • Very expensive products sold to wealthy buyers tend to have high return rates. These buyers ask many questions before purchase, expect a tailored experience and are used to getting exactly what they want.
  • Very expensive custom products attract wealthy buyers with high expectations who want calls and emails before buying. If the item does not match what they pictured, they return it. The model is then harder to run as a lifestyle business, even if it can make money.
  • Do not build the business around luxury products or the extremely high-priced sub-categories within niches, such as crystal chandeliers versus standard chandeliers. These require extensive pre-selling and individual nurturing, which does not fit a minimal-touch model.

Price points and demographic fit

2 min
  • Sofa examples: a roughly $200 cheap metal futon is typically bought by lower-income buyers. A roughly $1,500 average sofa fits the upper middle class, and a roughly $7,500 custom designer sofa is typically bought by wealthy buyers.
  • Alternative sofa values: a $200 futon, a $2,000 three-seater fabric sofa (realistic price, proportional expectations, fewer issues, strong profitability) and a $10,000 custom Italian leather sofa (high profit, intensive pre-selling, high return rate).
  • The $1,500 product or the $2,000 fabric sofa is the best fit for an upper middle class target, depending on which example set you use.
  • A high-priced product with a high margin, such as a $7,500 item at a 30% margin, is not automatically the best choice. Price must match the target demographic, because a product that appeals to the wrong buyers creates more service and return costs.
  • Do not choose a niche because a very expensive version looks attractive. A high-priced niche aimed at wealthy buyers can produce sales, but it brings heavy pre-selling and high return rates.
  • Chandeliers with an average search-term price of about $400 pass the test. For households earning $100,000 to $400,000, that amount is not a big decision.
  • For real crystal chandeliers, the first organic row averaged about $1,000, while later rows showed prices from about $4,000 up to over $450,000. Many of the most expensive listings were presumed to be real crystal pieces.
  • When organic listings show a very wide price spread for one search term, do not treat the whole range as one price point. Wide spreads often come from premium versions of the same product, so judge the typical buyer by the more mainstream listings.
  • An average price point around $10,000 to $20,000 generally means a wealthy demographic rather than upper middle class. Upper middle class households rarely spend at that level unless they are overextended on credit.
  • Futons with an average price above $200 can pass seasonality and demand checks but still fail the demographics test if their buyers are mostly first-apartment, dorm-room or parents' basement purchasers.
  • Stand-up desks pass by logic: lower-income households would not spend at that price point, and very wealthy buyers of custom pieces, such as a $15,000 mahogany desk, are not the core group. The core group is the upper middle class.
  • Stand-up paddleboards and paddles average over $700 in customer spend and appeal to upper-middle-class buyers seeking recreational products.
  • A very expensive outlier in a niche, priced far above the rest, can be listed for organic buyers. Keep it out of paid campaigns and off the ad budget, because it appeals to a different audience.
  • When a niche contains products across all price tiers and demographics, select one price tier that aligns with the target audience. Upper middle class customers are the most hands-off to serve.
  • Keep price tiers in separate stores when they serve different buyers, because a luxury buyer and a budget buyer shop differently. Put products in the same store only if they fit the same customer.

Demographic test

1 min
  • The target demographic is the upper middle class, defined as households with an income of $100,000 to $400,000. A niche passes when its typical buyer falls in this income band.
  • The demographics test has no single data source or fixed score. It relies on judgement: estimate who actually buys the product at its price point and whether that buyer fits the upper middle class.
  • The demographics test is a separate filter that comes after the price point, seasonality and demand checks. A niche can pass all of those and still fail if its typical buyers are not upper middle class households.
  • Run the demographics test only on the current winners in your own niche list, not on rejected niches. Record the result in the demographic column of your Niche Research Worksheet.

Procedure: demographics test

  1. Check the average price of the niche search term.
  2. Ask whether a purchase at that price is a major financial decision for a household earning $100,000 to $400,000.
  3. If the price points sit in the wealthy range, or the buyers are mainly first-apartment, dorm or temporary buyers, consider pivoting sideways.
  4. If the niche still fails, look for a related sub-niche that fits the upper middle class.

Procedure: checking price points for a niche

  1. Search the niche term on Google.
  2. Open the Shopping results.
  3. Scroll down to the organic listings.
  4. Read the price of each listing.
  5. Compare the prices to estimate the average price point and the spread across listings.

Recording results

  • A green demographic box in the research tool means the niche passes the demographic test, meaning it appeals to the upper middle class. Record it as "pass" and do not write out the demographic label.
  • A green brand loyalty box means little to no brand loyalty, so the niche passes the brand loyalty test. Record it as "pass".

Cleaning the niche list

1 min

Procedure: sorting the list against upper middle class criteria

  1. Open your research worksheet with your current niche list.
  2. Go through the list from the top and mark any niche that is too broad or covers a very wide price range.
  3. Copy the broad niche name and move it to the bottom of the list, so it is kept for reference but not researched further.
  4. Delete the original row of the broad niche.
  5. Ask an AI tool for sub-types of that niche that sell at a higher price point, for example at $500 or more.
  6. Insert each useful sub-niche as a new row, placed above the bottom reference section.
  7. Scan the rest of the list for niches that appeal to lower-income or wealthy buyers, and move them to the bottom.
  8. Replace low-price niches, such as futons, with higher-price alternatives in the same category, such as leather sofas, fabric sofas or sectional sofas.
  9. Keep niches you are unsure about on the list so they can be checked later with a demographic tool.

Procedure: refining niches in the research worksheet

  1. Open your Source A X Research Worksheet.
  2. Review each niche and assess whether it appeals to lower-income, upper-middle-class or wealthy demographics.
  3. Remove broad or ambiguous categories.
  4. Add niche variations that more clearly target upper middle class buyers.
  5. Use AI tools (ChatGPT, Gemini or similar) to expand product variants using prompts like the one below.
  6. Keep any uncertain niches. The DSLX Software will automatically show which demographic each niche appeals to.
  7. Mark the lesson complete and move to the next one.

Niches that are still unclear should stay on the list rather than be deleted, so they can be checked later with a demographic validation tool. Judging by intuition alone is acceptable at the early stage.

Pivoting broad niches

1 min

Case: the rug niche

  • The niche "Rugs" was too broad. It included $30 to $500+ options appealing to all income levels. The niche was moved to the bottom of the research list without evaluation.
  • To refine it, the AI prompt "What type of rugs typically sell for $500 or more?" was used. It yielded upper-middle-class-targeted variations: woven rugs, oriental rugs and Persian rugs, which are naturally priced higher and more specific to the target demographic.

Prompt

What type of [product category] typically sell for $500 or more?
  • Replace broad niche categories with upper-middle-class-specific variations. For example, instead of "futons", use leather sofas, fabric sofas or sectional sofas, which appeal more strongly to the target demographic.
  • Pivot sideways or to a related sub-niche when a niche fails the demographics test.
  • Dropping an entire product does not always make sense. A product that is too narrow may be expanded or repositioned; see the Source C units in the audience section.
  • If a niche is dominated by a few big brands sold in large chains, choose a related sub-niche. Golf clubs from Titleist, Callaway, TaylorMade, Mizuno and Srixon are weak for this model, so choose sub-niches such as golf carts, electric golf trolleys or golf bags.

Brand loyalty: what it means

3 min
  • A niche has brand loyalty when shoppers think of a few specific brands first when they want to buy a product in it, and those brands dominate the market. Avoid niches with brand loyalty for dropshipping.
  • Brand loyalty is a problem because established brands already occupy consumers' minds, leaving too few suppliers with enough sales potential to build a stable dropshipping business. The concern is the supplier side as much as the shopper side.
  • The Source A X methodology excludes niches with high brand loyalty, where shoppers strongly prefer specific brand names over product type.
  • High brand loyalty niches fail because approval from dominant brands is hard or impossible, and you cannot compete with major retailers such as Best Buy, Target or Costco. Big brands control distribution and attention, and competition is high while margins are low.
  • Strong niches have low brand loyalty: customers buy based on product type and features, multiple manufacturers compete evenly, and no single company spends hundreds of millions on advertising to control preference.
  • Select products where customers lack established brand loyalty. Customers in brand-loyal niches research extensively and default to trusted brands, which gives established retailers price and relationship advantages that dropshippers cannot match.
  • Choose products that meet all three criteria. Customers are comfortable buying from any manufacturer, they lack a default brand they automatically associate with the category, and no few manufacturers hold a monopoly. When these conditions exist, customers base decisions on price, reviews and personal preference rather than brand identity.
  • Target niches with low brand loyalty so new stores can compete. High brand loyalty niches require an established reputation and customer trust that new entrants cannot quickly overcome.
  • Target shoppers who have little to no brand loyalty and would be comfortable buying from any manufacturer. Shoppers should care about the product type, not the brand name on it.
  • Brand loyalty is one filter applied after the other niche criteria. A product should first pass the basic drop-shipping fit checks; only then does the brand loyalty check decide whether it stays on the list.
  • Customers will purchase from any manufacturer if the product is well represented online, the price matches their budget expectations and the product does not require a pre-existing brand preference.
  • Customers in non-branded categories often do not know which manufacturers produce what they buy. This absence of brand awareness is an advantage for e-commerce sellers, because buyers decide on design, price and features rather than loyalty.
  • Only sell products where customers have no established brand loyalty.
  • Brand presence alone does not rule out a niche. It matters only if the brand dominates the category so strongly that buyers think of it automatically. An established brand that merely exists in a category is not a reason to avoid it.
  • Avoid niches where buyers insist on one specific brand, such as tablets where buyers want an iPad. Niches where buyers choose by specifications such as size, color, voltage, material or number of components work well even when no brand is famous. (2023)
  • Choosing a product because you picture a particular brand when you imagine selling it is a warning sign. If your first thought is to sell a specific brand's product, the niche is probably brand-driven and should be avoided.
  • When researching a product, if nearly every recommendation, review or guide names the same single brand, treat the niche as brand-dominated. Consumers pointed to one brand first show high brand loyalty.

Personal judgement

  • Test your niche by instant brand association. If the first brands that come to mind are specific company names, the niche has high brand loyalty and should be avoided. If you instinctively think of the product type rather than one or two brands, the niche has low brand loyalty and is acceptable.
  • Your own experience as a buyer can reveal which brands dominate a category, so it is a reasonable starting point for removing obvious brand-driven niches. Products you have not bought yourself still need a proper brand loyalty check before a final decision.
  • Intuition is the first check: if no brand comes to mind when you would buy a product yourself, that suggests no brand loyalty. Google Shopping data should then confirm or correct this impression.
  • A brand you recognize only because you bought from it yourself is not evidence that the wider market knows it. Treat recognition from your own purchases with caution.
  • A niche where intuition and search data both show no dominant brand is a stronger candidate. Such niches leave room for new stores, because consumers do not default to one brand.

Brand loyalty: categories to avoid

2 min
  • Avoid niches with high brand loyalty: tablets (Apple, Samsung), headphones (Sony, Bose), digital cameras, gaming consoles, laptops and speaker systems. These meet other selection criteria but fail the brand loyalty test.
  • Avoid televisions. TV buyers research picture quality, warranties and reviews, then purchase the brand they trust. Manufacturers grant big-box retailers exclusive terms and pricing that a dropshipper cannot obtain.
  • Avoid tablets, televisions, laptops, video games, cameras and headphones, because customers already have predetermined brand preferences in these categories.
  • In the tablet market, most buyers choose iPad, followed by Microsoft Surface, then Android devices. Brand loyalty is set by manufacturer marketing before the purchase search begins.
  • Headphones priced at $200 and above are dominated by Bose, Beats, Sony and V-Moda. Customers in this range already have a brand preference due to manufacturer investment in brand building.
  • Video game console buyers choose between Xbox and PlayStation. No third option has viable market share because brand loyalty in gaming consoles is absolute.
  • A shopper who needs a tablet will most likely buy an iPad or a Samsung. A shopper who wants good noise-cancelling headphones will most likely buy Sony or Bose. Both niches are dominated by a few manufacturers and are not suitable for a new store.
  • Three-dimensional printers are dominated by Bambu Lab with strong brand loyalty. Remove them from the active list or move them to the bottom.
  • 3D printers are a high-brand-loyalty niche because the market is dominated by Bambu Lab. Buyers researching 3D printers are often pointed to this one brand first.
  • Treadmills from around 20 brands with roughly 100 products between them can offer enough variety, but buyers may still prefer the dominant brands.
  • A niche controlled by a few well-known brands, or one that is seasonal or saturated, is a weak choice. The niche needs enough variety in products and brands for buyers to choose a non-dominant brand.
  • A niche with few brands or too few products to sell online is weak, even when the product is heavy or large. Ask whether a supplier's niche gives enough variety.

Brand loyalty: categories that pass

2 min
  • Persian rugs show low brand loyalty. Customers care about quality and style, not the manufacturer, so the niche is acceptable even though rugs are sold in big-box stores.
  • Stand-up paddle boards have no dominant brand equivalent like Apple or Bose. Customers buy on appearance and price fit, with no requirement for brand loyalty.
  • Stand-up paddle boarding is a product niche with low brand loyalty and a good fit for a niche list. Its buyers ask for the product itself, not a specific manufacturer.
  • Chandeliers are expensive products without brand loyalty that appeal to upper-middle-class customers. Numerous variations in style, size, color and light count make them suitable for online dropshipping.
  • Entryway chandeliers work well online because customers search by specific style and dimensions rather than manufacturer. A homeowner who cannot find a preferred 30" x 30" design locally will buy online when they find an exact match, regardless of brand. Upper-middle-class homeowners renovating create sustained demand.
  • Wall lighting fixtures are an effective niche and a strong cross-sell product for chandelier stores, because customers do not require specific brands.
  • Table lamps sell well because customers seek appearance and design, not brand identity.
  • Crown molding is suitable for dropshipping because it comes in many styles and designs. Customers choose on aesthetic fit, not brand.
  • Wallpaper is brand-generic. Customers buy multiple rolls for whole houses, generating multi-thousand-dollar orders, because style and pattern, not manufacturer, drive the purchase.
  • Interior designers are a key buyer segment in wallpaper-type niches. They buy for clients and search for exact pattern, color and texture names. Products that match these specific searches can attract buyers who would not find them at large retailers.
  • Mirrors and decorative vases sell effectively at appropriate price points. Customers do not prioritize brand when these products match their aesthetic vision.
  • Wooden flooring customers select by color, width and pattern. Brand identity does not influence the decision, which makes wooden flooring suitable for dropshipping.
  • Coffee tables, daybeds, fireplaces, chairs, shelving and other furniture items are suitable for dropshipping because customers do not require specific brands.
  • Low brand loyalty home and décor items with multiple manufacturers include stand-up paddle boards, chandeliers, wall lighting, table lamps, crown molding, wallpaper, coffee tables, sofas, fireplaces, mirrors, vases, bookshelves, dining tables and chairs, and wood flooring. Customers focus on style, function and price rather than brand.
  • Standup desks show wide manufacturer variation and are marked no for brand loyalty.

Brand loyalty test with Google Shopping

2 min
  • Google Shopping is the data check for brand loyalty. Reach it by searching the niche keyword on google.com and opening the Shopping results. It shows which brands appear on the first results page.

Procedure: brand loyalty test for each niche

  1. Ask yourself whether any brand comes to mind immediately when you imagine buying a product in the niche. If none does, this points toward no.
  2. Copy the niche keyword, for example "chandelier", and search for it on google.com.
  3. Open the Shopping results.
  4. Review the brands shown on the first results page, including the brand list in the sidebar.
  5. Check the product names for manufacturer names.
  6. Decide: if the same few brand names keep repeating, mark yes; if many different and unfamiliar companies appear, mark no.
  7. If you marked yes, move the niche to the bottom of the list and continue researching the other niches. If you marked no, the niche can stay at the top as a candidate.
  • Judge the companies that manufacture the products, not the online stores that sell them. Ignore store names on Google Shopping when judging brand loyalty.
  • Do not confuse the retailer selling a product with the brand that makes it. Counting store names as brands gives a false picture of brand loyalty.
  • The sidebar on Google Shopping can list both retailers and brands. For standup desks, retailers such as Standup Desk Store, Vivo, Luxor, Simple Stuff and Costway appear alongside brands, so retailer names must be filtered out.
  • There is no fixed number of repeated brand names that defines brand loyalty on Google Shopping. The judgement rests on whether the first results page repeats the same brands or shows wide variation across many companies.
  • The brand loyalty column on the niche research worksheet takes only a yes or no answer. Mark yes when the same brands dominate the results and no when there is wide variation.
  • Switch Google Shopping from list view to grid view to scan many brand names quickly on the first results page.

Retailers and big-box stores

1 min
  • A product sold in big-box stores is acceptable for niche selection. A niche controlled by a few powerful brands is not.
  • Being sold in big-box stores does not disqualify a niche by itself. Persian rugs sold at Home Depot or Lowe's remain acceptable because buyers ask for the rug, not a brand.
  • Treating any product sold in big-box stores as a bad niche is a mistake. Look at whether buyers ask for a specific brand, not at where the product is sold.
  • Large retailers selling a product category do not disqualify it, because individual brands are often sold in only a few stores.
  • Niche validity is judged per product. A niche with many brands but few stores selling them is fine. If few advertisers are in Google Merchant Center or the free listings, that is an advantage. (2024)
  • Exclude a niche where big established retailers sell most of the brands. Focus on the brands those large retailers do not sell.
  • Avoid brands that are mainly sold by large retailers. Choose suppliers among the many makers of a product type rather than the few brands big retailers stock.
  • Local stores that stock only a few top brands do not rule out an online niche. If online-only retailers in the niche collectively list hundreds of brands that cannot be bought locally, there is enough variety to support online demand.

How the sources fit together

  • The 2019 units say big-box presence and large retailers do not disqualify a niche. The 2020 and 2021 units say to exclude a niche where big retailers sell most of the brands, and to avoid brands mainly stocked by large retailers. Apply the niche-level rule (big-box presence is acceptable) and the supplier-level rule (choose brands the large retailers do not carry).

Variety, competition and failed tests

1 min
  • Before choosing a niche, verify it is not dominated by a few large brands. Tablet buyers go directly to Apple.com or Costco, so the market has little variety. Good niches have many products and brands, so customers search broadly within the category.
  • A niche where only big brands sell and no dropship-friendly competitors exist is a failed test. Niche up, sideways or pivot. (2023)

Validating how people buy

1 min
  • Validate a niche by checking whether people buy out of pocket or through third parties. Hospital beds and mobility equipment are often purchased through insurance companies, which reduces profitability. Ask suppliers or competitors to understand actual purchase behavior.
  • Medical and mobility items such as stairlifts, lift chairs, CPAP machines and mobility scooters are often bought by older adults or caregivers. Health or medical claims in ads may face platform or regulatory limits in some markets.

Consumer and business buyers

1 min
  • Research B2B niches the same way as B2C: check competitor traffic, Google Shopping competition and monthly search volume. Buyers still search and buy online, even if product names return few results. (2024)
  • Whether a niche has business buyers should not be a factor in niche selection. Consumer-only and mixed markets can both be profitable, so judge a niche on its other criteria. (2025)
  • The upper middle class label does not apply to business buyers, who are used to spending money.
  • Business buyers should not be ruled out by the demographic label; the 2025 approach judges by other criteria, while the 2018 label is a service-level description only.

Repeat purchases and cross-sell

1 min
  • Repeat purchases are rare in most niches, and most traffic comes from first-time visitors. Use upsells, cross-sells, referral discounts for family and friends, and affiliate content for indirectly related products to get additional value. (2023)
  • Wall lighting is a strong cross-sell for chandelier stores.

Audience segments beyond income

2 min
  • Track buyer demographics in the sales data of winning products. In the example, the winning products mostly sold to women aged 45 and older, which helps refine audiences.
  • Find the customer segment that converts best in your past stores and write the research brief around its needs. In one tested case, the best audience was women over 45, so every product search was framed around their needs.
  • A dropshipping owner who has tested products, niches and countries over several years may find that one audience clearly outperforms the others, such as women over 45 in one documented case. Product research can then be filtered to that audience's needs, which gives a team member a precise target.
  • Women over 35 were the most profitable audience for fashion, jewelry, home decor and fitness items, while products aimed at men performed worse. Test this audience first when researching products for Germany and Australia.
  • Some sellers found that men aged roughly 35 and above were the easiest customers, generating few support emails, while women over 45 were another reliable group. The choice depends on which audience your own sales data shows.
  • If a product has two different buyer groups, such as partners buying gifts and parents buying for children, reach each group through creators whose followers belong to that group. Look at the followers of creators who already post content with that buyer type.
  • For Valentine's Day, focusing only on women as buyers is a common error. Men and parents also buy gifts on that day.
  • A jewelry store offered only one product at a time to organic traffic. About 30 percent of its viewers turned out to be men, so men's products were added to the store and purchases from men increased.
  • A product can be repositioned by changing who buys it. A product first aimed at women was marketed to men as a gift, and that switch in buyer and angle opened new demand. Test different buyer groups with the same product before dropping it.
  • A food vacuum sealer is more likely to sell to people storing dry goods in bulk, such as nuts, oats or coffee beans, than to daily cooks who can use jars. Target the bulk-storage buyer and check whether these buyers would need several units.
  • A product with a niche audience, such as alcohol-related items, can be limiting and may need a broader angle or a different target group to scale.
  • A product tied to a cultural or national identity, such as a map necklace of a home region, can reach a large audience of people who feel connected to it. Target that diaspora in several Western countries such as the US, UK and France. Offer a second item the buyer chooses for their home country as a free gift to raise the perceived value of the order.

Prompt

What is the demographic of buyers of [horse saddles]? Tell me where they are located, their average household income, their hobbies, the pages they follow and the magazines they read.

Broad targeting and narrowing

1 min
  • Start with broad interests and a broad audience. Narrow to the age, gender and interest that win once a winning audience, age, gender and interest are found, and do not niche down before a winner exists.

Enthusiast markets

1 min
  • An enthusiast market is one where people buy because they want the products and enjoy them, not only because they need them. Enthusiast markets are easier and cheaper to market than need-based ones, such as fridges, which the lesson treats as not enthusiast.
  • A niche is an enthusiast market when buyers want the products for a hobby or recreation rather than need them. Products people buy to create things always count as enthusiast.
  • Survival and off-grid gear is classed as a want, not a need, because buyers can still live on the grid. For this reason it counts as an enthusiast market.
  • Target an enthusiast market: customers who buy products for a hobby, passion or activity they choose, not something they must do daily. Examples include surfboards, stand-up paddle boards and kiteboarding gear.
  • Enthusiast buyers usually have more disposable income and want the product, so a high price is less of a barrier. They also haggle less, which makes customer service and marketing easier.
  • Fitness equipment such as exercise bikes and cardio machines is a good fit: enthusiasts recognize several brands from gyms and fitness media, while outsiders do not. If you already recognize every brand in a niche without researching it, the niche is probably too mainstream.

More rules worth knowing

1 min

  • If uncertain whether a niche has high or low brand loyalty, do not delete it yet. the niche check step software automatically analyzes brand loyalty for all niches and identifies which ones fail this test.

  • Launch your niche research worksheet. Survey your niche list and note which products instantly call to mind specific brand names. Demote or remove niches that are plainly dominated by one or two major brands. Retain niches where dominance is unclear-they're candidates for the niche check step testing. Finalize and check off your list.

  • Remove niches where a few big brands dominate and shoppers pick a specific brand by default, such as tablets, headphones, digital cameras, gaming consoles, laptops and speaker systems. These niches usually pass other product filters but fail the brand loyalty test.

Mark this lesson step as complete and move on to the next task.

  • Do not delete a niche when you are unsure whether brand loyalty is high. Keep uncertain niches on the list and check them later with a brand-loyalty test in the research process, which will show whether the niche has brand loyalty.

  • Deleting niches you are not sure about wastes good options early on. Only remove niches that are clearly dominated by a few brands, and let a later check decide the rest.
