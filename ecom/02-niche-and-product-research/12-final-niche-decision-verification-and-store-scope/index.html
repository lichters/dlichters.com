Final niche decision, verification and store scope

Column letters (A, B, C ...) refer to the research worksheet from section 2.3.

Grading each niche against the criteria

6 min

Source A validation tests (current)

  • A niche is ready to build on only if it passes every check on the Niche Research Worksheet, not just most of them.
  • A niche must pass all six validation criteria at once: pass every test on the Niche Research Worksheet; be dropship-friendly; have at least 20 viable suppliers; have at least one future competitor with a traffic rank under 100,000; have 20 or more suppliers with real contact information; and meet all thresholds for pricing, profit, target demographics, brand loyalty and seasonality.
  • Passing all four checks together is the threshold for treating a niche as likely profitable: drop-ship friendly, at least 20 suppliers, at least one competitor with a traffic rank under 100,000, and more than 20 reachable brands. Passing only some of them is not enough.
  • Judge a niche on measured data (supplier count, competitor traffic rank, reachable brand contacts), not on gut feeling. An expert's approval confirms data you have already gathered; it does not replace it.
  • Pick niches from the data, not from what sounds interesting. A niche that seems appealing may earn far less per sale than a data-backed alternative, as the woven rugs versus Persian rugs comparison shows.
  • Check every candidate niche against all criteria before building a store. A niche that fails the criteria should not be used as a store.
  • A niche qualifies as a winner only if it has enough search demand, has other stores already selling in it (drop-ship friendly), and can supply at least 20 distinct suppliers or brands. Those suppliers together must offer at least 100 products. Competitor stores in the target country should have a traffic rank below 1 million.
  • Traffic rank limits differ between sources. The stricter 100,000 limit appears in the six-criteria test and the four-check threshold; the 1 million limit appears in the winner definition. Apply the 100,000 limit when the test is the verification-stage check, and treat 1 million as the looser screen for qualifying a winner.
  • Approval checks the whole niche, not each individual product. Product-level questions are answered by the niche criteria and supplier approvals.
  • Before committing, a niche must be checked by a manual procedure:
    1. Find competitor stores selling the niche or a broader category that includes it.
    2. Look in the target subcategory of those stores.
    3. Confirm at least 100 products and at least 20 brands you could source from.
    4. Test search demand with Google Keyword Planner or Ubersuggest and confirm enough search traffic.
    5. Disqualify the niche if the subcategory has only a handful of brands and products.
  • Six-step check from the 2020 material:
    1. Check demand for the niche.
    2. Check the level of competition.
    3. Check saturation.
    4. Check seasonality.
    5. Check whether the niche is dropship-friendly.
    6. Check whether the dominant sellers are online-only or sell both offline and online. Heavy offline dominance is a reason to avoid a niche even when competition looks manageable.
  • Five-step validation from the 2021 material:
    1. Search the niche keyword on Google Shopping and count the products on the results pages, aiming for at least 100 products available from suppliers before committing.
    2. List the brands behind those products and confirm at least 20 brands exist.
    3. Check which brands are sold by many retailers and avoid those.
    4. Check demand with keyword research and competitor traffic.
    5. Submit the top three candidate niches to the niche verification process and decide.
  • Compare the number of brands, the number of products and the search demand before choosing. A niche must pass all three checks. Failing one means widening or narrowing the category.
  • A niche should pass three checks: evergreen demand, a workable price point, and enough search demand. For a US-sized market the demand check used a target of about 10,000 in the demand data. The niche should offer at least 20 possible brands and about 100 products to stay specific.
  • A niche should be evergreen, have demand, have at least 20 suppliers and 100 products in the target country, and face no excessive competition on the first page of Google Shopping. All criteria must hold before a niche is accepted.
  • Niches are checked against a Google-based competition test and a drop-ship friendliness test. A niche is only pursued when all criteria pass.
  • A niche idea should pass checks for seasonality, good price points, drop-ship friendliness, a healthy number of Google searches, and competitor websites that are popular enough. Failing one check can be enough to drop the idea. (2025)
  • Minimum niche viability: at least 20 different suppliers, at least 100 total products across those suppliers (not per supplier), non-seasonal demand, sufficient search volume, low-to-moderate competition, and appeal to upper-middle-class buyers.
  • Approval requires at least 20 suppliers, at least 100 products from those suppliers, and enough buyers searching for the product every month. If a niche falls short, broaden it into a related set of products rather than abandoning the store concept.
  • Before building a store around a niche:
    1. Confirm at least 20 suppliers.
    2. Confirm at least 100 products across those suppliers.
    3. Have the niche reviewed by an experienced person for a go or no-go decision.
    4. Build the store only after the niche passes the review.

Source B approval procedure

  • Choose the target country first, because the competitor and supplier limits depend on it. Then run the following for each researched idea, in this order:
    1. Check average price: the average across the niche's products must be 800 USD or more.
    2. Check shipping: the products must be suitable for shipping and not too large.
    3. Check the enthusiast market: buyers should want the products for hobby or recreation rather than need them.
    4. Count suppliers: more than 10 is needed for an Australian target, and the US target is 20.
    5. Count direct competitors: at most 15 for the US and at most 10 for Australia.
    6. Check recurring revenue: one customer should be able to buy several different products from the niche.
    7. Check traffic volume: add up the monthly searches across all products in the niche.
    8. Apply the strikeout rules: competition above the limit or average price below 800 USD means not approved, regardless of the other results.
    9. Fill in the Approved? column: green if all boxes are ticked, red if there is a strikeout, orange or yellow if borderline.

  • Competition is a strikeout criterion. If the number of direct competitors is above the limit for the target country, the niche is not approved, whatever the other criteria show. This is the most important rule in the approval step.
  • For a US target, a niche with up to 15 direct competitors passes the competition check.
  • For an Australian target, the aim is a maximum of 10 direct competitors. A niche with 13 direct competitors fails there but could pass for the US.
  • Only competition and price are direct strikeouts. Weak recurring revenue, a low supplier count or lower traffic lower the grade toward maybe but do not reject a niche on their own.
  • A niche without recurring revenue can still be profitable, but it cannot grow to the same size. For this reason it is graded maybe instead of approved.
  • Seven-criterion market check, in order (local currency applies; the size rule has an exception):
    1. Average product price of at least 800 in your local currency.
    2. Products are not too big or heavy, unless they are valued at 3,000 or more.
    3. The market is an enthusiast market, not everyday household items.
    4. There is the right amount of competition: some, but not too much.
    5. Established, local suppliers exist, but the brands are not household names.
    6. There is enough online search traffic for the market.
    7. The market has recurring revenue potential. A market idea that meets all seven criteria is viable.
  • Compare all brainstormed ideas against the six questions and choose the one with the most yes answers. An enthusiast market with a yes on every other question beats a market with weaker answers, even if the other market was researched first.

Comparing graded niches side by side

2 min
  • Compare candidate niches side by side in one worksheet, judging each on average price, average profit, customer demographic, brand loyalty, seasonality, demand, dropshipping friendliness and the estimated number of suppliers before choosing one.
  • Complete the research worksheets in this order:
    1. Fill out all remaining columns in the Niche Research Worksheet.
    2. Complete the Future Competitors worksheet.
    3. Complete the Master Suppliers worksheet.
    4. Narrow your initial niche list down to your top candidates.
    5. Submit your top niche ideas for verification.
  • Select exactly five niches for the top list. Each must meet all of these criteria: meets your pricing and profit requirements; appeals to upper-middle-class demographics; has low brand loyalty; sells year-round (passes the seasonality test showing all four seasons); and demonstrates strong average monthly demand.
  • Filter and rank the worksheet to find the top 5 niches:
    1. Right-click the demand column and choose sort sheet A to Z (smallest to largest).
    2. Highlight every niche under 10,000 monthly searches and change its text color to red, marking them as losers.
    3. Sort by the seasonality column A to Z and mark every niche that failed the seasonality test red.
    4. Right-click the average profit column (column D) and sort Z to A (largest to smallest).
    5. Cut the red rows and paste them at the bottom of the list, then delete the blank rows left behind.
    6. Remove further weak niches that failed enough tests, such as off-grid solar kits in the example.
    7. Keep only the top 5 niches at the top of the sheet.
    8. Color the top 5 green, using a shade that is easy to read.
  • Winner list procedure:
    1. Open the main research workbook and list every niche that has already been tested in the research phase.
    2. Check each niche's average price, average profit, demographic, brand loyalty, seasonality, demand and whether it is drop-ship friendly.
    3. Keep only the niches that meet all of these criteria; these are the winners.
    4. For each winner, open its Master Supplier list and count the suppliers found so far.
    5. Enter that count in the column labelled Estimated Suppliers for the niche.
    6. Compare the winners and choose the one to build the first store in.
  • Demand and results: demand is not the only predictor. A niche with lower demand can still outperform, and the actual outcome only shows after the store is built.
  • Grading scale: a niche can be a base hit (a modest, profitable store), a double, or a grand slam (a store that can reach very high annual sales). Early research can confirm a niche is profitable but not how big it will become, which only shows after months of running it. (2024)
  • Traffic against competition: when one niche has more search traffic but more competition, and another has less traffic and less competition, both can still be approved. Choosing between them is a judgment call, not a rule.
  • Several niches passing every criterion means there is no single best niche. Run the numbers for each one in the tracking sheet before choosing, and gut feeling should not replace the data.

Choosing the first niche

3 min

Ranking and tiebreakers

  • Prioritize niches with a higher average selling price and a large number of possible suppliers. These two measures help narrow a long niche list.
  • For a first store, choose the niche with higher average price points and more available suppliers when several niches pass the criteria. (2023)
  • If a niche has many suppliers and several niches pass the research test, pick any one that passes and keep the rest as a list for future stores. Higher-priced products are a reasonable tiebreaker.
  • If several niches meet all criteria, choose the one with the most suppliers and a price point that feels right to you. Other qualified niches can be built later.
  • A niche can contain a wide price range, so you can sell all tiers under one store. Example: a 3D printer niche could include a beginner kit under 300 dollars, a consumer model at 500 to 700 dollars, and a prosumer model at a few thousand dollars.
  • Top niche rankings and example lists show what makes a niche strong; they are not a pick-one list. Choose a niche that meets the same criteria, not simply one of the listed names.
  • Published lists of profitable niches are useful for widening the range of ideas, but a niche should be chosen only after it passes your own demand and supplier tests. A niche should not be picked simply because it appears on a list.

Decision rules

  • Research niches until one passes every criteria test. One niche that passes everything is enough to move forward. (2023)
  • If several niches meet all criteria, there is no single best niche.
  • Once several niches pass all criteria, they all have a real chance to become strong stores, so no winner is clearly better on paper.
  • Choose based on the collected data and on your own confidence in the niche. Take a day or two to decide, and do not spend a week re-reviewing the same data.
  • If three niches pass all criteria, they are usually good candidates to choose from. Submitting two of them for verification is acceptable. (2023)
  • Brainstorming dozens of niche ideas usually produces several viable winners, so the goal is to validate the most promising few rather than find the single perfect one. Submitting two or three top ideas for validation gives extra confidence. (2023)
  • If a niche passes every validation test, choose it and start building rather than waiting for a perfect niche, because thousands of niches can work with this model. More stores can be built later to grow revenue and profit.
  • Social media reach is not required when choosing the first niche. It becomes more relevant later when building more niche-specific stores or a brand around a niche with fewer suppliers.
  • Once a niche passes the viability tests and meets the criteria, it is good enough to start with, so further research should not stall the launch. Some niches can be confirmed in as little as five minutes, so overthinking the choice is a common waste of time.
  • Limit the time spent choosing a niche: shortlist the three niches you feel most confident about, submit them for verification, and let the verification help you pick one. Do not rush the decision, but do not stay stuck on it indefinitely.
  • A niche that passes all the criteria should not be dropped because of saturation rumors or a recession. Once you have doubts after passing the test, a downward spiral of changing niches tends to cost more than it saves. (2023)

Tiebreaker order for approved (green) niches

  • Procedure for choosing between several approved niches:
    1. Remove the red niches from the active shortlist and set aside the ideas that were never researched.
    2. Compare the remaining greens on average price, supplier count, direct competitors and traffic. With approved niches these differences are usually small.
    3. Use personal interest as the tiebreaker: which niche would you feel most excited to sell day to day through your website.
    4. Pick exactly one niche and start the next phase with it.
    5. Keep the full list with its scores for later use.
  • Sources differ on the role of interest. The Source B procedure uses it as the last tiebreaker after the measured comparison. The Source A material says gut feeling should not replace the data (see above). Use the data first and personal interest last.

Niche-specific store rules

6 min

One niche per store

  • Keep one niche per store. Add a second niche to the same store only when fewer than 20 suppliers or fewer than 100 products can be found for the first one.
  • Each store should target one niche. A niche can hold many products and SKUs as long as they stay within that niche and meet the niche criteria.
  • Build separate stores for each niche that passes all criteria, because separate stores convert better, allow more targeted ads and give a better return on ad spend. Combine niches only if neither had enough suppliers on its own or one of them failed the criteria.
  • A niche can stand as its own store when it passes all criteria and at least 100 products from at least 20 brands or suppliers can be found for it. If one niche does not reach that level, combine it with another niche.
  • Build one niche-specific store per niche only if you can find at least 20 brands and 100 products that meet all blueprint criteria. If not, move up one level, for example from motorized wheelchairs to all wheelchairs.
  • Before moving a niche up a level, check whether a sub-category such as chandeliers already has at least 20 brands and 100 products. If it does, a dedicated sub-niche store can be built.
  • Build a store around one niche that passes all validation criteria. Put several categories in one store only when they are closely related and naturally bought together. An example is a sofa sold with matching loveseats, ottomans and chairs.
  • Only put products in the same store if they are logically related, such as a sofa with a matching loveseat and chair that buyers might purchase as a set. Otherwise, build separate stores, each with at least 20 suppliers and 100 products per niche.
  • Build a store around a whole niche rather than one product, and include all legitimate brands in that niche that are based in the US and sell only through online-only stores. Multiple products in the same niche give more room for winners than a single item. (2025)
  • Only broaden the niche when a niche cannot pass the research criteria. More niche-specific stores generally convert visitors better than general stores that sell many unrelated items. (2024)
  • Keep a store as niche-specific as possible, as long as it still meets the minimum of 20 suppliers and 100 products. General multi-category stores are not recommended.
  • Niche-specific stores convert better than broad multi-category stores, which is why the niche structure is kept whenever the supply criteria are met. Adding several categories is possible, but it is best kept close to the blueprint.
  • Niche-specific stores often outperform a mixed-niche store with the same products. Build a second store once the first runs smoothly and the easy wins are captured.
  • Single-niche stores consistently converted visitors into customers at a higher rate than multi-niche stores, according to the research author's reported decade of store building. The reasoning given is that a single-niche store can offer a more tailored experience for the product being sought.
  • Always build niche-specific first due to higher conversion rates. Multi-niche stores can be used only if a single niche fails to meet module two criteria and needs to be broadened to reach minimum demand or competition thresholds.
  • A more specialized niche (for example office chairs instead of office furniture) lets you become the category authority and prevents customer distraction. Customers searching for a specific product type are more likely to buy from a specialist store than from a generalist. (2023)
  • A kayak-specific store has more SEO authority and customer trust in that category than a broader water sports store that sells kayaks, paddle boards and surfboards together. The more specific niche ranks better for category keywords and builds deeper expertise perception. Broaden only when supplier count requires it.
  • A small, narrow product type can compete well when it is built into a standalone store with its own site and experience. Such niches should still pass the demand tests before launch.

Store scope and combining

  • For a second store, avoid seasonal niches and apply all the full research criteria to it, as you would for the first store.
  • Conflict on the second niche: the first-store rule requires year-round demand, and the second-store rule repeats the avoidance of seasonal niches. Both apply.
  • If the store domain is tied to one niche, related niches can be added to the same store when the current store is established and the new products sell well under the domain. A separate domain (around 12 USD for a new domain purchased through Shopify, per 2022 pricing) with a new logo is an option for brand-new projects.
  • If several products in different types all meet the criteria, start the first store with one product type and add more stores later.
  • A store can be pivoted to a different product type within the same niche without renaming it or building a new store.
  • A store with one niche may use the category that already generates the best sales. Narrow the store to one product category rather than mixing unrelated products such as children's gadgets, men's accessories and dresses. Mixed categories reduce credibility when the brand is built around a person's expertise.
  • Specializing in one category works because visitors expect a personal brand to be an expert in one area. A mismatch between the person's stated expertise and the products sold creates a disconnect that undermines trust.
  • A jewelry brand can sell its bracelets through a fulfillment center instead of dropshipping and still keep a homepage focused on its top seller. Even when the store has a few minor categories, such as a small blanket section, the homepage should show mainly the best-selling product, here bracelets.
  • The first filter to add to a store should be by product type, matching the main niche category in the top navigation dropdown. This is the recommended single starting filter for a new store.
  • Adding decor or lighting to an established dining furniture store is not recommended. Cheaper, unrelated categories lower the average price point and average order value and can reduce the conversion rate.
  • A shopper buying a dining table usually does not buy lighting at the same time, so a lighting category attracts only a small share of customers. This is one reason not to merge closely related but different products into one store.
  • A single niche such as dining furniture can have well over 100 supplier brands, so there is usually enough depth within the main niche. Expanding into related categories is generally less valuable than working through the existing niche's brands.
  • A product in the same niche across several products (for example face, hair, shaving and personal care) keeps the store focused. Staying in one niche makes it easier to build a consistent brand and audience.
  • Subniche counts decide store structure:
    1. A subniche with many suppliers and products (for example around 40 suppliers and about 1,000 products) can get its own store.
    2. Subniches with fewer than 20 suppliers and few products should be combined.
    3. Where some subniches are strong and others weak, build the strong ones as their own stores, build the best one first, and skip or merge the weak ones.
  • Choose a store name broad enough for future expansion, such as a name built on a larger theme like surfing or watches, instead of a narrow product name like leather watches.

Brands

  • No sector is excluded by default, including beauty. Any niche that passes all the niche criteria can be used, and no extra restriction applies to a specific category.
  • Working with one to three well-known brands that people already search for brings traffic through brand searches. These brands also help sell the other products in the store and can be used for monthly promotions.
  • Conflict on brands: the Source A material favours one to three well-known brands for store traffic, while the Source B market screen requires established local suppliers whose brands are not household names. The first applies to the store build; the second applies to the market screen.
  • Single-product stores or funnels are not recommended. Each product page should be treated as a sales funnel inside a full store, because one-product businesses depend entirely on that product.

Catalogue size and product selection

2 min
  • Upload only the products you want to sell, even if a supplier offers hundreds. A supplier with 100 products may have only 10 you need; uploading all dilutes niche focus. (2023)
  • Do not upload all products from a supplier's list. Upload only the products that fit your niche and are profitable.
  • Minimum product count per store: at least 100 products; no defined maximum.
  • Aim for as many sellable products in the niche as the market supports, not for a fixed number. About 300 products can be enough to launch, but more should be added whenever the niche has thousands of products available.
  • Start a new store with about 10 products, then test them one by one rather than building a large list. Products that are not yet proven can be kept as a collection on the store.
  • Conflict on launch size: about 10 products to start and test one by one; about 300 to launch when the niche supports it; at least 100 products per store as the minimum. Use 10 to start and test if proof is still missing, and the larger counts when the niche is verified and the supply supports it.
  • Prioritizing products when there are many supplier products:
    1. Go to the top future competitors' sites and open the category or subcategory. (2024)
    2. Sort products by best sellers. (2024)
    3. Upload the top 20 best sellers first, from brands you are approved to sell, then add the rest over time. (2024)
  • Adding products to a store increases the chance of finding new winners that may be more profitable than the current winning product. Do not rely on one or two winners; keep expanding the offer and adding products.
  • Each new category can be tested as a separate product with its own ads. Any category that performs well can become an additional winner to scale on its own.
  • A broader product range increases the chance of finding a new winning product that can outperform the current best seller. Stores should keep adding products instead of relying on one or two winners.
  • Where a niche has a wide price range, sell the tiers under one store.
  • Check whether product models differ in a meaningful way, such as size, power or technology, before building separate pages for them. Models that differ only in color rarely justify their own category page.
  • If suppliers and products are sold as a pair or set, upload them as sold, with the photos showing the pair or set.
  • In niches where buyers want matching sets, such as furniture, offer complete sets rather than individual pieces, because buyers prefer to purchase the matching items together.
  • Choose the collection list before creating products. Build the collection list from competitor stores before creating anything in Shopify:
    1. Open two to three future competitor stores.
    2. Hover over the main categories to see their subcollections.
    3. Note how they split the category, for example by type, by brand or by feature.
    4. Open a document in Google Drive, Notes or similar.
    5. Write each main collection first and indent its subcollections below it.
    6. Keep the list short at the start and skip brand collections until suppliers are approved.

Demo products before supplier contact

2 min
  • Demo store preparation: 5 to 10 demo products are sufficient before contacting suppliers. More than 50 products is excessive and delays supplier outreach.
  • Demo products only need to look professional: 4 to 5 products with complete pages, no blank images and no dummy text. Full catalogs are not needed before contacting suppliers.
  • Conflict on demo count: 5 to 10 products are sufficient, and 4 to 5 per collection is the working limit. Both stop well short of a full catalogue. Use 4 to 5 when you need the fewest pages, and up to 10 if you want more coverage before supplier contact.
  • Demo products are added in the Shopify admin under Products in the left sidebar. The Products page opens the product list; the Find products button is not useful for this task and can be ignored. To add one, click Add product, which opens the product editor.
  • Find demo products from another store that sells the same product type but carries brands you do not plan to target, so suppliers on your list do not see their own products on your site. A supplier seeing its own products can create awkwardness.
  • Demo product procedure:
    1. Open your initial supplier list from the earlier step.
    2. Turn on a VPN and set it to a country that is not your target market.
    3. Run a Google search for the product type you plan to sell.
    4. Pick a store from the results that carries brands not on your supplier list.
    5. Choose 4 to 5 products from that store's collection for your demo products.
  • A VPN is used to search from another country so that Google returns e-commerce stores from that market. The video recommends NordVPN and notes that many other VPNs can be found through a Google search for good VPN.
  • Choose the VPN country as a market where the brand is sold but not in your own country. For a store in Australia, set the VPN to the US and look for brands sold only there. For a US store, use Australia, the UK or a European country. This way there is no confusion with local suppliers about the products.
  • Upload only products that fit one of the product-type collections you already created. Choose one collection and upload 4 to 5 demo products for it, not more than that.
  • Before supplier contact, the demo store is a sample of the look and feel, not the catalogue. Use it to show suppliers the store style and the product type.

After approval

2 min

Starting the build

  • Approval is the signal to start: a green light means you start the store build and supplier contact. (2024)
  • If a niche passes all checks and you have confirmed it, move on to store building.
  • Once you have confirmed the niche or completed the submission, move on to store building.
  • If a niche passes every validation test, proceed to store building without additional validation.
  • Once the niche is verified, the next step is the store-building step, where the store is built.
  • After confirmation, the niche becomes the store niche. Store build and supplier contact follow the collection list in the procedure above.
  • Niche verification requests on the Source D course come back with the approved product. Wait for that ticket result, which names the approved product, before starting any store work. (2026)
  • Once a niche is confirmed, a new product type within that niche does not require a new verification. (2024)

Using the verdict

  • If the verdict is a pivot, use the suggested related niche. The reviewer either confirms the niche or suggests a related niche that fits the selection criteria better. (2023)
  • Use the verdict and reasons when stuck between several viable candidates.
  • A niche that passes all criteria should not be dropped because of saturation rumors or a recession. (2023)
  • A niche that passes the research can still be a base hit, a double or a grand slam. Early results show profitability; scale becomes clear only after months of running it. (2024)
  • Higher demand does not automatically mean higher results. The actual outcome only shows after the store is built.

Brand, reviews and affiliate content

  • Bulk-uploading manufacturer reviews is acceptable if a supplier agrees in writing that you can use their product reviews on your store. You can then bulk-upload reviews from the manufacturer's website in one day without risk of Google account suspension. Always confirm supplier permission first. (2024)
  • Affiliate links belong in the blog or content section of a store, not on product pages. Only link to directly related products that the store does not sell, such as a heating pad alongside a massage chair, or a drum lesson subscription alongside a keyboard. (2025)
  • Affiliate examples for related niches: a gun safe store can promote a firearms safety subscription; a keyboard store can promote online drum lessons. (2025)
  • The first filter and the collection list (see above) set the store structure before the first products go live.

Choosing the first niche

1 min
  • Launch one niche first. Start a second niche only after the first is profitable, and reinvest profit into it. A niche with much higher search volume and a niche with much higher prices can both become successful stores over time.
  • A store built around one motif or theme across several product types can work if buyers of one item are likely to buy related items. It is less focused than a single high-ticket niche store, so it needs clear categories and strong differentiation.

Niche-specific store rules

1 min
  • Build niche-specific stores around one specialized product category rather than a general store that sells everything. Core products are the main niche products the store is built around.
  • Avoid general stores that sell random items with no shared identity. Focus each store on one niche where the audience is passionate and willing to spend.

After approval

1 min
  • Once the niche feedback confirms your niche, you can order the store build. If the feedback recommends a pivot, the reasons are given with it.
  • Product approval comes before store building. It prevents the costliest early mistake, which is investing in a store and ads around a weak product. Run four checks on each shortlisted product: margin, demand, competition and timing.

Product approval workflow:

  1. Complete the product research module.
  2. Submit the top product picks to the team through a support ticket.
  3. The team checks margins, demand, competition and timing.
  4. The team answers either go-ahead or suggests a better product.
  5. Do not build the store until the product is approved.
