Fixing failed niches: niching up, sideways and down

Section 2.11 · 11 of 24 in this chapter · 18 min read

This section has 12 parts. Tap one to open it.

Failed Tests: First Decision

1 min
  • A niche that fails the Demand Test can be either niched up (narrowed to a more specific subset) or removed from the candidate list. Note that this unit uses "niche up" to mean narrowing, while most other units in this section use it to mean broadening.
  • A niche that misses one criterion can often be adjusted sideways to meet all criteria, which is generally better than starting over from scratch.
  • A niche that almost passes all tests but fails one should be pivoted to a closely related niche, which keeps the good parts of the original idea instead of abandoning the research.
  • A niche that has real potential but fails the research criteria does not have to be dropped. You can pivot it into a different niche that passes the tests, or broaden it one level so the niche list gets more candidates.
  • A niche must pass every niche criterion. If demand is too low, widen it by moving up one category level, or sideways by adding related products that serve the same customer.
  • A niche is successful only if it passes all research criteria: minimum 20 suppliers, minimum 100 products, sufficient search volume, and realistic margins. If these are not met, niche up one level instead of adding unrelated product categories.
  • If a rejected niche has few brands, the usual response is to tweak it rather than start over: keep the store and adjust the niche, for example by moving up a level. A rejected niche does not mean the whole store has to be deleted and rebuilt.
  • Do not stop a niche after one weak store. Check the supplier pricing tiers and organic traffic first before moving to another niche.
  • In a niche with a busy season and few suppliers, stay with the core niche and keep following up with suppliers instead of expanding into related products.
  • Use the same analytics to compare lateral options as you used for the original niche.

Failed Test Procedures

1 min

Narrow-niche recovery:

  1. If a narrow niche fails, niche up to the broader product type.
  2. If the broader type still fails, niche sideways to a related product group, such as ergonomic desks.
  3. Test each new niche against all criteria before committing.

Supplier-count recovery:

  1. Count the suppliers for the niche, including suppliers with only a handful of matching products.
  2. If there are fewer than 20, broaden the niche one level, for example from gaming chairs to a complete game room setup with desks and chairs.
  3. Repeat until you can find at least 20 suppliers.

Generic-to-specific retest:

  1. Research the niche against the criteria.
  2. If it fails, broaden it one level, for example brown leather chairs, then leather chairs.
  3. Repeat until the niche has at least 20 brands and 100 products.

Niche-up procedure for narrow niches:

  1. Research the narrow niche, for example electric fire pits, and count the possible products and brands that sell it.
  2. If products or brands are below the thresholds (100 products, 20 brands), search the next broader category, for example fire pits.
  3. Repeat the count for the broader category.
  4. If it passes both thresholds, build a store in that category. If not, move up another level, for example fireplaces or patio furniture.

Rejected-idea rescue:

  1. Review the full list of dismissed niche ideas and look for similar products that could be combined.
  2. Check the reason each one failed, such as too few suppliers or low demand.
  3. Retest the combined version against the criteria instead of generating new ideas.

Niching Up (Broadening)

4 min

Definition and purpose

  • Niching up means broadening to a related, larger category when a narrow product, such as standing desks, fails validation, for example moving to ergonomic office furniture.
  • Niching up means widening the product category to a broader group that includes your original product and related items. The combined search volume of the broader group can exceed the 10,000 minimum even when the single product does not.
  • Ergonomic furniture is a broader niche that includes stand-up desks, ball chairs and other products, so its combined volume can pass 10,000 in a smaller market where stand-up desks alone fail. In the UK, the search term "ergonomic chair" had a volume of 27,000, enough to stand as a niche on its own.
  • Broaden step by step as a normal part of research. Example: if only three brands make brown leather watches, broaden to leather watches. If that gives only six brands, broaden to all watches, which had 25 brands in the example.
  • Example chain: brown leather chairs, then leather chairs, then leather furniture, until the 20-supplier minimum is met.

When to niche up: supplier and product shortfalls

  • If a niche has fewer than 20 suppliers, niche up one level (add a closely related category) rather than restart. Example: fire pits plus fire tables instead of only fire pits. Stay within the same suppliers where possible to avoid re-approval.
  • When niching up, choose the closest adjacent category already available from your suppliers. Example: if a supplier has 100 fire pit products and 20 are fire tables, upload those 20 fire tables the next day without re-applying for approval.
  • If a niche has fewer than 20 suppliers or fewer than 100 products, move up one level (niche up) to a broader related category that has enough of both.
  • If a niche has too few suppliers, for example only 13 found, go one level up to a broader niche with the same customer. Check how many brands and suppliers that broader category offers before deciding.
  • If a narrow niche has too few suppliers to reach 20, broaden it one category level, for example from stand-up desks to ergonomic furniture. Then combine the suppliers from the product types that fall into the broader category until the total reaches at least 20.
  • Aim for at least 20 suppliers in a niche before building the store. If you only find about eight, broaden the niche by adding related categories until you reach about 20 to 25 suppliers.
  • If a niche yields only about 10 viable brands while all other criteria pass, broaden it one level by combining related categories to reach 20 or more brands rather than starting over. The goal is enough suppliers to build a store with several winning products.
  • If a niche has too few brands and products to reach about 20 brands and 100 products, broaden it to a parent category. If it has thousands of brands and products, narrow it to one specific type.
  • If a broader niche has few brands, count the brands in the next level up before deciding. If only 10 brands make file cabinets, move one level broader to office shelving and count the brands there.
  • If a treadmill niche yields only 10 brands and 50 treadmills, move one level up to treadmills and elliptical trainers.
  • If demand, product count or supplier count is too low in a narrow niche, move one level up in the category, for example from brown leather recliners to leather recliners, then to living room furniture. Check again and stop at the most specific level that still meets all criteria.
  • If a narrow niche (for example luxury family tents) fails the niche tests, step up one level to a broader niche (for example luxury tents) and test again. Typical failure points are fewer than 20 dropship-friendly brands or fewer than 100 products.
  • If a niche has too little search volume or too few suppliers, broaden it one level, for example from a specific product to a wider category, without turning it into a general store that sells everything.
  • If a niche has no or few suppliers in the country, niching up one level (for example from bathtubs to bathtubs plus vanities and mirrors) adds product variety, even though narrow niches normally convert better.
  • Niching up is a fallback when persistent out-of-stock issues remain after contacting all suppliers. It is not the first response to slow supplier replies.
  • If a niche yields few suppliers because suppliers want a longer track record, niche sideways or niche up and widen the product range to find suppliers that will approve.
  • If a product type has low average search volume, for example under 10,000 search results, it is harder to grow into a store. Broaden to a niche level that combines it with a similar product type to get more search volume and traffic, and build the store for the bigger niche first.
  • If one product keyword does not reach the volume target, combine related product types in the same category until the total does. Example: 1,000 plus 5,000 plus 8,000 monthly searches equals 14,000, which is called niching up a level.

Limits on niching up

  • Do not niche up if the niche passes the verification criteria and at least 20 brands and 100 products are available. Keep the niche specific so the store speaks directly to its buyers.
  • Niching up should add only closely related products at a higher price point. Avoid adding loosely related categories, because that turns the store into a general store.
  • Expand into closely related niches to find more brands to contact when outreach to suppliers yields few brands. (Source year 2025.)
  • Niche up only when the real numbers stop matching the research results. A narrower niche usually converts better, provided the research criteria are still met without the extra products.

Niching Sideways

2 min

Definition and purpose

  • Niching sideways means moving into a closely related sub-niche or adjacent product within the same niche when the original version fails the demographics test. It is used to find a price point and buyer group that match the upper middle class.
  • Niching sideways means switching to a different but related product, such as office chairs instead of recliners.
  • If you have exhausted your supplier research and cannot find enough suppliers for your chosen niche, broaden it slightly to find additional suppliers. This strategy, called niching sideways, expands your product category just enough to unlock a larger supplier pool willing to work with dropshipping retailers. (Source year 2025.)
  • Niche sideways when similar products from the original manufacturers sell better than the chosen niche. In the source, the speaker's similar products outsold the original niche, so a new store was built for them. (Source year not stated.)
  • If you cannot find enough suppliers, or if a niche lacks a path to 20 suppliers, work down your brainstorm list, niche sideways into a related category, or niche up one level. Pick the path that gets you to at least 20 suppliers.
  • If your niche has no good options left, you may need to expand into a lateral niche by looking beyond your current niche into the wider market. (Source year not stated.)

Sideways examples

  • If a canoe niche lacks 20 suppliers or 100 products, add related products such as canoe motors, upgraded seats or fishing holders. Stay as close to the core product as possible while reaching the minimum numbers.
  • Pivoting from futons to leather sofas keeps the same use case but raises the price point into a range the upper middle class buys. This lets the niche pass the demographics test.

Procedure for sub-100-product niches

  1. Niche sideways by including all related product types, such as every type of office chair.
  2. If that is still too small, niche up to a broader category, such as office furniture.
  3. Judge the choice by the number of suppliers and products you can actually find.

Niching Down (Narrowing)

3 min

Why narrow

  • Choose the most specific niche possible as long as it passes all validation criteria. If a specific niche fails, broaden it one level at a time, for example from a narrow product type to a broader category. (Source year 2024.)
  • Choose a niche as narrow as possible, provided it still meets the minimum supplier count and product count.
  • Niche down as far as possible while the niche still passes all validation tests. If a narrower niche fails, niche up or niche sideways instead. The depth you can go is decided by the research results (number of suppliers and products). (Source year 2024.)
  • Be as specific as possible within a niche, but only as far as the niche still meets all niche criteria.
  • If all niche criteria hold, narrowing down to a more specific sub-niche usually increases conversion rate. The sub-niche keeps the search demand high enough to remain viable.
  • A narrower niche usually converts better, provided the research criteria are still met without the extra products.
  • Narrow a niche as far as possible, but only while the sub-niche still passes every test: at least 20 suppliers, at least 100 products, evergreen demand and real search demand. Go to the narrowest level that still passes, such as closet organization instead of organization as a whole.
  • Start niche research with the most specific product type you can name, such as ergonomic office chairs rather than a single color or model, and test it against your criteria. If it fails, broaden it one level, for example from ergonomic office chairs to ergonomic office furniture. (Source year 2025.)
  • Choose a niche at the product-type level, such as standing desks, not a narrow variant such as bamboo standing desks.
  • A niche with a huge number of models or types can still work, but limit the store to one narrower product line, such as dirt bike suspension rather than all motorcycle suspension.
  • A deeper niche has a better chance. Broad categories dominated by a few big brands are bad by default, but a narrower subcategory can still be an opportunity, such as wine refrigerators or kegerators compared with general refrigerators.
  • A broad category such as Fitness, Outdoors, Office or Kitchen contains many sub-products. Narrowing to one sub-niche, for example Squat Racks within Fitness or Pizza Ovens within Outdoors, makes the audience and ad targeting more specific.
  • A multi-brand auto parts catalog works better as a sub-niche than as a general car parts store. For example, a store for Jeep Wrangler owners can sell wheels, tires, shock mounts, cages and lights for that vehicle.
  • Furniture is too broad to be a niche. Break it down into specific furniture types and specific audiences who want them, then test each sub-niche against the validation criteria covering product count, supplier count and competition level. (Source year 2024.)
  • Do not sell furniture as a generic furniture store. Within furniture there are thousands of sub-niches that should be researched individually.
  • A broad category can be narrowed into a higher-ticket subtype to target a higher price point, for example generic rugs to Persian rugs or generic sofas to leather sectionals.
  • If a broad niche such as dogs is too generic, brainstorm more specific and more expensive products within it, then run each one through the competition criteria. Only a specific product idea that passes the test should become the starting niche.
  • A niche can be specialized by product type and still work. Among typical niche choices, the price tier is the way to narrow your space, while the brand selection is the way to avoid saturated brands.

Beginner caution

  • Beginners should avoid common niches like pets and beauty, which are flooded with new drop shippers. A sub-niche of a large category with little online discussion is more likely to be underrated. (Source: Source C, year not stated.)

Narrowing mistakes

  • Choosing a niche so narrow that little search volume or brand opportunity exists is a mistake. Broaden the niche one level instead of forcing a narrow one.
  • A niche built around a very narrow buyer group, such as one trade with one product type, often falls below the demand benchmark of about 10,000 average monthly searches for its core product term. If a narrow niche fails this test, widen it to a broader product category rather than forcing the narrow version through.

Combining Sub-Niches

2 min

When combining is allowed

  • Combine related sub-niches only if suppliers sell them together and customers would purchase them in sets, for example chairs, loveseats, sofas and ottomans in matching colors. Do not combine niches purely for SEO reasons.
  • Combine niches into one store only when testing shows a niche has too few suppliers, too little demand or too much competition. Small niches that fail the research criteria are better candidates for combining than strong, large niches.
  • Combining two related product types is a fallback, not the default. Combine only when no single product or broader niche reaches 10,000 search volume in the target country, and combine only similar niches so that the combined volume reaches the minimum. (Source year not stated.)
  • If a niche only has about 10 brands, combine it with a closely related product type and count the suppliers and demand together. Combine them only if the combined niche passes the same criteria.
  • Combine only categories that a customer would compare or buy together, such as stand-up paddle boards and surfboards. Do not combine unrelated categories, because the store then stops reading as one niche store. (Source year 2025.)
  • Combined demand and supplier count can turn a rejected niche into a viable one. Example: a product with 8,000 average monthly searches fell below the demand target, but combining it with a related product reached 10,000.
  • Combining two related product lines into one store is valid when the combined niche meets all the criteria. Example: a sub-niche with about 5,000 average monthly searches and too few brands can fail the demand test, while a related product line with about 8,000 monthly searches and 15 brands can pass. (Source year 2023.)
  • Mixing related categories, such as several home goods types, can reach the target of 20+ brands and 100+ products where local-only suppliers are too few.

Combining in smaller markets

  • Two small connected niches may be combined to reach the 10,000 monthly search minimum, which is common in smaller markets like the UK. The combined niche should still be a clear, specialized store theme rather than a general department store.
  • For smaller markets or countries where drop shipping is less established, combine a few related sub-niches until the niche reaches at least 20 brands. A general store that sells a little of everything is the wrong model in those markets.

One Niche per Store and Mixed Stores

1 min
  • Mixing unrelated products in one store is a last resort, used only when no niche passes the approval criteria. Before accepting mixed products, try to build a loose theme around them, such as a room-style store.
  • If sub-niche stores get little traffic while broader home stores get a lot, study the broader stores that sell your sub-niche plus a bit more to find where the traffic goes.
  • Competitor stores that sell other categories alongside your niche do not require you to sell those categories too. Decide on the niche using its own research, and stay narrow as long as it passes the criteria.
  • Adding categories should never turn the store into a general store that sells everything.
  • Niche expansion techniques are only for owners who have already completed the blueprint's niche and supplier validation and have a working store. They do not replace the blueprint's criteria, so always follow the blueprint first. (Source year not stated.)

Small Markets and Country Limits

1 min
  • For markets with small populations (under 5 million), niche up one level broader than you normally would to reach enough potential customers. This typically lowers conversion rates but increases traffic opportunity.
  • Market size for a small country can be too small for the product criteria. Two options are to niche up a level by adding more products to the range, or to sell in a larger market such as the UK. (Source year not stated.)
  • If you cannot find a supplier that sells to you because a niche is too small in your country, the answer is usually to niche up one level, not to abandon the market. Check whether the larger category gives enough volume, products and suppliers.
  • If a niche cannot reach 20 dropship-friendly suppliers, especially in a smaller country such as Canada, Australia or the UK, move up one category level and test again.
  • Smaller markets can be served by the broader ergonomic furniture level, where combined volume can pass 10,000 when a single product does not.
  • Note the tension: small markets call for broadening, while narrower niches usually convert better when the criteria still pass. Broaden only as far as the criteria require.

Price and Demographic Fit

1 min
  • Do not mark a niche as wealthy or lower-income and drop it right away. First check whether a related sub-niche, such as general chandeliers instead of real crystal chandeliers, fits the upper middle class. (Source year not stated.)
  • Consider pivoting when price points reach the $5,000 to $10,000+ range, or when the product is mostly bought as a first or temporary purchase. Adjust the niche to move it back into upper middle class price territory.
  • A broad niche such as rugs covers an enormous price range, from about $30 to $40 for house rugs up to $500 or more for premium rugs. Broad niches make it hard to tell which demographic they serve, so split them into narrower sub-niches before researching.
  • Narrow niches differ in price: a niche with a $30 to $40 product and a $500 product serve different buyers. Pick the sub-niche that matches the target price tier before you test demand.

Fallback When All Ideas Fail

1 min

Shortlist cycle

  1. Take the next five ideas from the shortlist that have not been researched yet.
  2. Research each of them in full.
  3. Run the niche approval procedure on the five ideas.
  4. Repeat the cycle until at least one niche is green.

Fallback order when all ideas fail

  • Work down your brainstorm list first. If the niche fails the competition test, niche sideways into a related category, or niche up one level, and pick the path that gets you to at least 20 suppliers.
  • If a niche fails, pivot from the closest near-miss niche rather than brainstorming new ones. A niche that failed only one test is a good pivot starting point.
  • Expand into a lateral niche when the current niche has no good options left. Use the same analytics to compare these options.
  • Keep a near-miss niche alive by pivoting it into a related niche that passes, or broaden it one level so the list gets more candidates.
  • Rescue dismissed ideas by combining similar products and retesting the combined version against the criteria.

More rules worth knowing

1 min

  • Niche up (broaden to a higher category) when you cannot find 20 dropship-friendly suppliers in your specific niche but can reach that number at a broader level, such as selling kayaks plus stand-up paddle boards plus surfboards as water sports.

  • When your niche has few suppliers, look at adjacent product categories, such as broader lines of similar products, to reach the minimum number of suppliers.

  • When your niche has few suppliers, look at adjacent product categories, such as broader lines of similar products, to reach the minimum number of suppliers.

  • If a niche like car seats only has about 10 suppliers, broaden to a related category such as child care products or products for new parents. Keep this step-up approach until the store reaches 20 suppliers and 100 products.

  • If a niche has too few suppliers in one product category, consider related product categories instead of broadening the whole store far beyond its focus.
