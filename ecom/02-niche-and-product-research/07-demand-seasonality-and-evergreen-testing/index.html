Playbook › 2. Niche and product research

Demand, seasonality and evergreen testing

Section 2.7 · 7 of 24 in this chapter · 32 min read

This section has 21 parts. Tap one to open it.

Two tests before time is spent on a niche

1 min
  • Validate every niche with at least two tests before investing time: demand (minimum 10,000 average monthly Google searches) and seasonality (evergreen check). Drop niches that fail either test early.
  • The Demand Test checks that people actually want the products in a potential niche. A pass validates real market demand.
  • The Evergreen Test decides whether a niche is seasonal or evergreen, meaning it has year-round demand.
  • Run the evergreen test on each niche to confirm steady demand across the year, not peaks at certain times.
  • Before committing time to a niche, verify that enough buyers exist, using market research.
  • Avoid seasonal niches for the first store. Profit is concentrated in a small time window, which lowers viability.
  • Market research matters most for long-term product survival, so judge a product on sustained demand trends, not on a short spike in ads or searches.

Demand threshold: the 10,000-search minimum

3 min
  • The minimum is 10,000 average monthly Google searches per niche, measured as a consistent monthly average over months.
  • At or above 10,000 in the country where you sell is a green light to continue.
  • Mark any niche below 10,000 in red and deprioritize it.
  • Read the number against the threshold only. Do not compare it with other niches' volumes. A similar monthly range for different niches is expected.
  • The threshold is one criterion among many, not a standalone winner marker. Viability comes from combining all research criteria.
  • In 2024 a core keyword at about 10,000 monthly searches was still the working threshold for a viable niche. It is always judged together with competition, Google Shopping results and other research data. No fixed 500,000-search requirement applies.
  • The demand figure is the average monthly searches for the niche keyword reported by the research tool.
  • The minimum is also reported as "about 10,000 per month" in a 2022 lesson.
  • A 2023 lesson applies the same 10,000 figure to the niche keyword and the average across the niche, and says to use it together with the other research tests, not on its own.
  • A 2018 lesson set the same minimum for the UK and the US, measured as search volume, not clicks.
  • Above 10,000, no specific higher or lower number has proven to predict better store success.
  • Search volume above 10,000 does not reliably indicate higher or lower success or competition. Volume alone is not a ranking signal. Judge success over time, after the store is live, optimized, and has more suppliers and customer data.
  • A niche above 1 million searches has no upper limit. Narrowing it to a sub-niche is optional and only makes sense if it sharpens the store's focus.
  • Average monthly volume reflects general interest in a niche, not the traffic you will get. Actual traffic comes from brand, supplier and SKU keywords, so conversion math based on niche volume is not useful for forecasting.
  • A 2018 lesson also checks that the average product price is $200 or more alongside 10K Google searches and evergreen demand.

Demand test procedure in research tools

2 min

Procedure, the source's own site (demand test for a target country).

  1. Open the keyword research tool at the source's own site
  2. Select the target country.
  3. Search the product keyword, such as "stand-up desk".
  4. Read the search volume shown for that country.
  5. If the volume is 10,000 or more, the niche passes and you can move forward.
  6. If the volume is below 10,000, niche up to a broader category and test again.
  7. If it is still below 10,000, test an alternative niche.
  8. Only if no single niche reaches 10,000, combine two similar niches so their combined search volume reaches 10,000.

Procedure, Source A research tool (niche test).

  1. Copy the niche name from the niche research worksheet.
  2. Open the research tool, set the country dropdown to the selling country (default US), paste the niche and click Search.
  3. Read the monthly search volume. If it is at least 10,000, the demand test passes (green box). If not, the box is red and the text says the niche fails.
  4. Copy the monthly search number into the worksheet's demand column.
  5. Read the evergreen result. A green box with the message that the niche shows little to no seasonality means it passes. A seasonal result means it fails.
  6. Copy the average price from the price analysis at the bottom of the tool into the worksheet's average price column, rounded as shown.
  7. Mark the seasonality column yes or no accordingly.
  8. Refresh the page before testing the next niche.
  • Set the country dropdown to the country where you plan to sell, not where you are located. The default is US.
  • In the research tool, a passing niche shows a green result box with a message that it passes. A failing niche shows a red box, and the text names the failed test, either demand or evergreen (seasonality).
  • The tool analyzes the search-volume graph itself and reports seasonality automatically, so you do not need to read the graph manually to judge seasonality.
  • Example: a wine refrigerator niche showed over 14,800 monthly searches, passed the demand test, got a green evergreen box for little to no seasonality, and passed the price analysis.
  • Example: a Christmas tree stand niche passed demand but failed the evergreen test for strong seasonality. Its average price of $43.15 also failed the price analysis. The tool shows the reason in the text box next to the result.

Keyword tools and their limits

3 min

Country and setup rules

  • Select the target country before running a search. Changing the country after a search still shows the earlier country's data.
  • Select the country first when checking whether a tool returns real results. Zero results in an otherwise working account can signal a glitch worth reporting to support.
  • The same keyword has different volumes in different countries, so select the target country before searching.
  • A tool's keyword data for a brand-new Google Ads account gives very broad search volume ranges. The data becomes more accurate once the account starts spending money.
  • Check whether a keyword has an accurate figure before you rely on it. The rank of a demand-tool result should fall between 1 and 1,000,000. A reading above 2,000,000 is not expected and should be double-checked before use.
  • Demand graphs built on low search volume become spiky and volatile, because they rely on few average searches. Spikes on low-volume keywords carry little signal. Check the search volume figure at the top of the tool before reading the trend line.
  • A flat line at the start of a trend graph usually means search volume was too low to show, not that the niche is seasonal.

Cost-per-click and competition figures

  • Ignore CPC during niche research. Focus on long-tail product-level search volume instead. CPM varies by broader niche category and does not reflect your specific product opportunity.
  • For niche demand research, look at average monthly search volume for broad niche keywords to confirm people search for what you want to sell. Ignore the CPC for those broad terms, because ads run on product-level keywords that cost much less.
  • Ubersuggest can show average monthly searches for broad niche keywords. Use the volume and ignore the CPC shown for those broad terms.
  • CPC and competition figures from Google Keyword Planner or Ubersuggest refer to Google search text ads, not Google Shopping. Do not use them to set Shopping bids.
  • Measure demand by search volume, not impressions. If a product gets many impressions but few clicks, look up its search volume in Google Keyword Planner to see whether people search for it at all.

Keyword selection and search intent

4 min

Finding product keywords (procedure)

Procedure, finding specific product keywords from a broad category (Google).

  1. Go to Google.
  2. Type your broad product category (e.g., "aquarium").
  3. Review the autocomplete suggestions.
  4. Note which suggestions are actual products and which are non-product searches (e.g., "aquariums near me").
  5. Scroll to the bottom of the search results and review "Related searches".
  6. Use the related suggestions Google provides (e.g., "aquarium tanks" instead of "aquarium") to identify specific product keywords that match your target customer's search intent.
  7. Use specific product keywords instead of broad terms for your niche research.
  • To find more rising keywords, use Google Keyword Planner.
    1. Click "discover new keywords".
    2. Search a broad keyword related to your product, such as "dog leash" or "lights".
    3. In the results, click the "three months change" column to see which searches grew over the last three months.
    4. Use the results for product name ideas, SEO keywords and marketing angles.

Procedure, finding more search demand for a product with low volume (2019).

  1. Search the product term on google.com.
  2. Scroll to the footer and note the "searches related to" terms.
  3. Check the search volume of each related term, such as "kids ride-on cars", "push ride-on toys" or "ride-on toys".
  4. If the combined demand is still under $10K, move one level up to a broader category (for example "kids rockers") and combine two niches to reach $10K.

Intent: buying terms, qualifiers and brands

  • When checking keyword demand, pick keywords whose search intent is clearly to buy the product, such as "barbecue grills", not terms used for other purposes, such as "Korean barbecue restaurants". This step needs judgment, so the demand numbers alone are not enough.
  • Example: the US term for a generic sauna showed about 165K monthly searches, but many are for gym or public saunas. Adding "home" dropped it to about 33,000, the number that matches home buyers. Keep or remove an adjective based on whether it changes buyer intent for the product you sell.
  • If a brand name gets more search volume than the niche keyword, the niche keyword probably has too many qualifiers. Remove qualifying words from the niche search and check the results again. Never use a brand name as the name of your store.
  • Very broad keywords rarely convert, because shoppers searching them are often not ready to buy. Target specific buying keywords rather than broad category terms.
  • Buyers in brand-less niches search for product attributes such as a specific size, color and style. Those attribute searches create keywords you can appear for. Selling through a brand site or a comparison shopper still leads buyers to your store.
  • Use 5 to 10 keywords for trend analysis: two or three direct product keywords plus related problem or niche keywords, since people search for the problem before the product. Compare alternative product names and keywords to choose the wording for copy and ad titles.
  • Compare product name variants in Google Trends and in a dropshipping research tool to see which term has more search volume. Then run the research with one chosen term.
  • Broad keywords can inflate traffic estimates, because they mix uses that do not match your product. Searches for "winches" include boat and trailer uses, and keyword lists can include unrelated meanings such as a firearm brand or inch-to-metre conversions. Only count terms that match what you plan to sell.
  • Mark the traffic-volume question yes only if the main keyword meets the minimum for its market.

Source B's keyword procedure (Ubersuggest, 2021-22)

Procedure, Ubersuggest keyword check for a market idea.

  1. Open the Keyword Ideas section.
  2. Set the country dropdown to the market you will sell in, for example United States, United Kingdom, Australia or Canada.
  3. Enter your market idea as a keyword, for example "4x4 accessories", and click Search.
  4. Read the VOL column, which shows average monthly searches for that exact keyword in the selected country.
  5. Review the similar keyword suggestions and add the relevant variations to your count.
  6. If a broad term mixes several product types, search narrower terms such as "winches for trucks" and count those separately.
  7. Ignore keywords with unrelated meanings and add up the relevant ones.
  8. Enter the combined estimate in the market research tracker.

Procedure, Ubersuggest keyword check.

  1. Open a keyword tool such as Ubersuggest in your browser. The lesson says anyone can use it without signing up, though a CAPTCHA check may appear.
  2. Set the country to your market, for example the United States.
  3. Enter the main keyword for your product type and read its monthly search volume.
  4. Check the variations shown, such as "for sale" or "for home" versions, and add them to your total.
  5. Mark traffic volume as yes if the main keyword meets the minimum.
  • Ubersuggest (ubersuggest.com) is a free keyword tool that shows monthly search volume for a term. Set the country before searching.

Combining related products and niching up

1 min
  • If a niche is too narrow to pass the search-volume criteria, combine closely related product types, such as charcoal and gas barbecues, rather than unrelated ones. The charcoal barbecue example had about 6,500 monthly searches.
  • Procedure, reaching 10,000 in the target country (research tool). If the volume is below 10,000, niche up; if still below, test an alternative; only if no single niche reaches 10,000, combine two similar niches.
  • Small-population countries: niche up (broaden the niche to a higher level) until it reaches the minimum.
  • Very small sub-niches can still reach roughly 2 to 3 million people on Facebook, and about half may show buying intent. A small niche is worth testing if the pain point is clear and the ad shows it in the first few seconds.
  • A niche that feels small can still contain millions of people in the target audience, so even a small conversion rate from a narrow ad can indicate a viable market.
  • Niches can take off when one seller tests a product and others follow on TikTok and Facebook. Watch fast-growing sub-niches, such as golf in recent years, and track which products appear in new sub-niches to spot the trend early.
  • Niche accessories that are very expensive, such as premium phone cases, are unlikely to have enough monthly searches to be worth it.
  • If a product term stays near zero, search the broader niche or the problem, feeling or occasion the buyer has (e.g., "kids drawing", "gifts for Valentine's").
  • Demand that follows the product, the problem and the wider market points to different levels of opportunity. Compare them in Google Trends.
  • A gift product can be promoted for two close occasions, such as Valentine's Day and Mother's Day, using the same product. Plan the creator content and ad angles for each occasion separately.

Recurring revenue and traffic volume

2 min

Recurring revenue

  • A market has recurring sales potential if the customer will buy several different product types over time, or if the main product has accessories or add-ons. A coffee machine can be followed by a grinder and mini roaster, and generators by log splitters and snow blowers.
  • Recurring revenue means one customer can buy several different product types from the niche over time. A durable item bought once and lasting for years gives little recurring revenue.
  • Repeat sales to the same customer carry no acquisition cost, so margins on the second and third sale are often higher. A niche that supports several products is worth more than a single high-ticket item.
  • Score every shortlisted market idea Yes or No for recurring revenue. Answer Yes only if the market offers several different products that would appeal to the same customer and could be sold to them over time.
  • Example: a 4x4 accessories niche scores Yes, because one buyer can later purchase rear cargo drawers, a nudge bar, a turbo system, then suspension or wheels, with email used to bring them back. A niche selling only expensive, long-lasting CNC machines scores No, because buyers rarely replace them and rarely buy a second unit of the same product.

Procedure, checking recurring revenue when unsure.

  1. Open the competitor websites you already collected in competition research.
  2. Check whether they sell a range of different products.
  3. Check whether those products would appeal to the same customer.
  4. If both are true, mark recurring revenue as Yes; otherwise mark it No.
  • Recurring sales in other niches: games and entertainment (customers buy more games once bored with the first), subscription models (customers rebuy mop heads and replacement parts), and fashion (seasonal changes quarterly).

Traffic volume

  • Traffic volume is the combined monthly searches across the niche's products. The first example niche had at least 20,000 searches per month, which was far above the traffic targets in every country discussed.
  • Measure traffic volume as the number of people searching each month for the product types you plan to sell on the Google search network. Google is the main channel for a high-ticket dropshipping business, through paid ads or organic search. Bing is much smaller and not a significant part of the marketing effort.
  • Add up the monthly searches for all relevant product keywords as one combined total, not each keyword separately. The total must reach the country threshold to count as enough traffic.
  • Check that the market has enough online search traffic to support a business over the long term. Marketing mostly runs on Google, Google Ads and Facebook, so enough people must be searching for the product at any time.
  • Example, US keyword volumes (2021): about 450,000 monthly searches for "generator", about 74,000 for "generator for sale" and about 49,500 for "generator for home". For log splitters, "log splitter for sale" had about 12,000 monthly searches. Volumes of related products can be added to the niche total to show enough demand.
  • Thresholds for the combined total are shown in the market table above.

Google Trends: reading the five-year view

4 min

Setup

Procedure, checking seasonality in Google Trends (2025).

  1. Open trends.google.com.
  2. Set the region to the country where the business operates.
  3. Change the time range from past day to past 5 years.
  4. Read the chart for yearly peaks and valleys, and compare the busy season with the off season.
  • Check the demand trend over the past five years.
  • Do not use search trend data from around 2004 to judge a niche. Use the last five years, since search behavior has changed a lot.
  • Judge seasonality in Google Trends over five years for the target country, using the 0-100 scale.
  • Procedure, Google Trends check with the Source C method: search the exact product name, then the problem it solves, the solution it offers and several related keywords, and compare their interest over time.
  • Set the time range to the past 12 months and search the exact product keyword first.
  • Use the 12-month and 5-year windows on the product and related keywords.
  • In the Source A tool, change the time period from past day to past five years and set the country to the target market. The graph then shows week over week instead of month over month.
  • Google Trends searched with the product term, the US set as country and five years of interest is the way to check whether people buy the product year-round.
  • Google Trends checks evergreen demand, meaning whether search interest holds over time. It does not show when people open emails or click ads. For ad click timing, use the day and hour breakdown in Google Ads reports.
  • Procedure, checking a candidate's market trend.
    1. Open Google Trends.
    2. Search the main product keyword or, if it returns little data, the niche or a closely related term.
    3. Confirm the term is trending or holding interest before moving the product forward.

Trend checks over time

  • Check Google Trends across years rather than quarters to judge demand stability. Pick products that sell consistently, not trending items. Products that upper-middle-class buyers always need tend to hold up in a recession, while luxury items should be avoided.
  • Demand is judged over a five-year window, not a snapshot. Check that a niche does not collapse.
  • Compare Google Trends and a dropshipping research tool to pick the term with more volume. Run the research with one chosen term.
  • Trending products often return months later after a quiet period, so a product that went viral earlier is worth re-checking before you dismiss it.

Seasonal versus evergreen judgment

4 min

Rules for the first store

  • For a first store, choose evergreen niches with steady sales week over week, month over month and year over year. Seasonal niches produce sales only in a few months and then collapse regardless of how well the store is run.
  • Do not start with seasonal niches as your first store. Slight seasonality (demand that does not drop to zero) is acceptable. Severe peaks and valleys complicate measurement of what actually works.
  • Seasonal products should not be the first store, because a season can pass before you know whether the store works. Test that the product sells year-round with an evergreen check before committing.
  • Seasonal niches with a deep off-season are poor choices for a first store. Demand swings, operations are hard to standardize, and geo-targeting may be needed. Consider them for a second or third store, after a first store is stable year-round.
  • If a niche is seasonal, a first store should avoid it.
  • A niche with a seasonal peak is acceptable only if the valleys between peaks are not too low. Seasonal niches are generally not recommended.
  • For a first store, avoid niches with strong seasonal swings. A niche that never falls close to zero is still seasonal but less risky.
  • Outdoor patio furniture shows a strong seasonal dip, so it is better kept for a second or later store rather than the first.
  • A seasonal store launched during its low season still produces some sales and experience. Finish setup, keep suppliers engaged and prepare for the next peak instead of restarting the niche search. Plan a non-seasonal second store or a product that covers the off months.

Acceptable seasonality

  • Seasonality is acceptable if demand remains consistent year-round with only minor fluctuations. Use Google Trends (five-year view) to verify, watching for drops that do not approach zero.
  • Seasonal dips do not automatically disqualify a niche. A niche that dips in Q4 but grows year over year can beat a steady niche with much lower search volume. Weigh all research factors together.
  • Seasonal niches are acceptable if Google Trends seasonality severity matches the benchmark in the course.
  • Mild seasonal peaks, such as a November rise from gift buying, are not a reason to add other niches. Launch with the niche as it is. Extreme seasonality, where sales nearly vanish for most of the year, is a poor fit.
  • Holiday sales spikes in inherently non-seasonal niches are normal and acceptable. For example, paddleboards sell more in summer. Seasonal products such as holiday decorations are not acceptable in a general niche.
  • A niche is not seasonal if some products sell in fall and winter while others sell in spring and summer. Together they can produce steady revenue year-round.
  • Do not choose niches where all seasonal items dominate unless demand is proven to be high and remains elevated year-round.
  • A bump that only lifts Q4 for a few weeks is holiday demand. A product that sells all year is not seasonal. A product is seasonal only if demand falls to a clear low for most of the year.

Identifying the pattern

  • To check seasonality, compare the average monthly search volume with the trend chart. If most of the yearly search volume falls in November and December, the product is seasonal.
  • Example: a keyword averaging 30,000 searches per month, with about 270,000 of the yearly total in November and December, shows a clear seasonal peak. Such a product is seasonal even if it sells steadily the rest of the year.
  • A niche passes the seasonality test only with 4 out of 4 seasons, meaning it sells month over month and year over year, so it can sell year-round.
  • Check seasonality with a trend graph. Strong peaks and valleys indicate a seasonal product, while a flat line indicates an evergreen product.
  • Use the US Census Bureau's quarterly retail e-commerce sales data at census.gov to check seasonality. Look at the unadjusted series, where Q4 shows the largest peak in most years.
  • Seasonality matters for Google Trends in a 2019 lesson: seasonal dips should be checked against whether the product sells year-round.
  • A seasonal product shows a repeated rise in a regular period such as September to November before winter. When the last year shows a stronger spike than prior years, it suggests higher potential for the coming year.

Using the seasonal result

  • If the busy season is more than double the off season in Google Trends, consider building a second store in a niche with the inverse pattern sooner rather than later.
  • Test products with a gift angle only for a season, since a Christmas-driven product can sell well in one quarter and then fall off. Look for problem-solving products with viral potential for long-term runs.

Evergreen categories and product choice

1 min
  • If starting directly with private labeling without dropshipping proof, choose forever-green niches (selling year-round) rather than trend products that peak and then die within months. This reduces loss risk on inventory investment.
  • Forever-green categories include seasonal fashion (jewelry, apparel renewed each season), home health products (posture correctors, foot correctors, leg supports), home improvement, kitchen utilities and appliances.
  • Prefer evergreen products that sell all year over niches that work only for one to three months. Finding a product that earns 10,000 to 30,000 per month is hard, and replacing products every few months is costly. Look for products that solve everyday problems, since many sell year-round.
  • Prefer products that can run all year rather than seasonal or one-event products, unless the seller deliberately chooses a season.
  • Evergreen products that sell in all four seasons should be sourced first. Seasonal items are added later.
  • Evergreen products that people keep buying over years are preferred, even if demand dips for a while. Luxury items can still be evergreen, and a short-term slowdown in purchasing frequency is not a reason to abandon a niche.
  • Prefer evergreen niches that sold well in the past and will sell in the future over products that are only trending. Long-term demand is what makes a store a lasting asset.
  • Pet toys keep selling consistently and are a reasonable candidate for testing. Evergreen categories like this carry lower trend risk than short-lived viral products.
  • Women's fashion is an evergreen category that performs well across seasons. If a product underperforms in one country, test it in another market before dropping it.
  • Seasonality and fast-growing niches create windows of opportunity. Pet products and winter-season items showed strong momentum in the period described.

Trend timing and short-lived products

1 min
  • A 90-day revenue trend should be checked before committing to a product. A product that peaked and is now slowing needs a different approach or should be skipped. Adjust the plan on the trend, not only on the last 30 days.
  • Product demand can drop after a short seasonal peak. A portable blender that sold well for a period stopped selling. Check the recent sales trend before putting money into ads and prepare a replacement product in advance.
  • Procedure, checking a trend before stocking merchandise.
    1. Search the keyword and view the trend graph, both worldwide and over the past five years.
    2. Judge whether interest is rising, steady or falling, and where you are on the curve.
    3. Estimate how long the trend will last, for example until a season or league ends.
    4. Launch while interest is rising or near its peak, not after it has passed.
    5. Avoid buying large stock at the end of a trend.
  • Buying bulk team merchandise tied to a match or season result can leave unsold stock and chargebacks if the team does poorly. Buyers feel misled when the product no longer fits the outcome they expected.
  • A product that went viral earlier can return, so check it again before dismissing it.
  • In 2020, gym and fitness equipment had stock shortages. Demand spiked when gyms closed and inventory had been planned for normal demand. Check inventory risk in niches with sudden demand spikes.

Seasonal stock, ad spend and budgets

1 min
  • For a seasonal product, keep ad spend low or pause ads in the warm months, from April to October. Resume ads once the cold season returns.
  • Check whether your product sells on a seasonal pattern or year-round before planning budgets and campaigns. Knowing the buying seasons tells you when to increase spend and when to reduce it.
  • Judge the store's margin across the year. If a store can reach a 20% net margin, it is better to have that margin all year than to rely on a seasonal niche with large swings. Seasonal niches are not automatically excluded, but the owner must decide whether the swings are acceptable.
  • A 20% net margin on the peak and low months of a seasonal niche (see the illustration above) shows the cash swing that the owner must accept.

Competitor and marketplace signals

1 min
  • A category leader with a very large review count shows that demand for the product type is already proven. For example, a pet hair brush with about 138,000 Amazon ratings. Review volume on the top sellers is a quick sign of how big a niche is.
  • Evaluate demand by looking at the homepage of competitor stores, since products featured there are usually the best sellers. The Best Sellers tab on those sites shows which brands and suppliers make the top products.
  • There is no fixed number of reviews within the last 30 days that proves a product is selling. Use competitor homepages and best-seller sections as the signal instead.
  • Check inventory risk when a niche spikes suddenly, since stock planned for normal demand can run out.

Order of Research Checks

1 min
  • Older advice. Run the traffic volume check after the recurring revenue check and before the final niche decision.
  • Complete the traffic volume check for all five shortlisted market ideas before making the final choice of niche. Traffic volume is the last research step before deciding which idea to build.

Recurring Revenue as the First Check

1 min
  • Older advice. For each market idea, answer yes or no to recurring revenue potential. Answer yes only if the niche includes more than one product that the same customer could buy. The products must appeal to the same buyer.
  • Example of a yes: in a 4x4 accessories niche, a first sale could be rear cargo drawers, followed by a nudge bar, then a turbo system upgrade, then suspension or wheels, all sold to the same owner at different times. This makes the niche a yes for recurring revenue.
  • Example of a no: a niche selling only CNC machines is a no for recurring revenue. The machines are very expensive, last a long time, and are rarely replaced, so a second or third sale of the same product is unlikely.
  • To check recurring revenue potential for a market idea:
    1. Go back to the competitor sites from the earlier competition research.
    2. Check whether they sell a range of different products, and whether those products would appeal to the same customer.
    3. If they do, the market idea has recurring revenue potential and gets a yes.

Traffic Volume Thresholds

1 min
  • Older advice. Traffic volume is the monthly number of people searching Google for the types of products you want to sell. It is the second research question and is measured after the recurring revenue question is answered.
  • The target is a combined total across all product keywords in the niche:
Market Combined monthly Google searches (target)
United States 50,000+
Australia and any other country outside the US 20,000+
  • Do not judge each product keyword on its own when checking the traffic target. The 50,000 or 20,000 figure is a combined total across all relevant products in the niche, so add up the volumes of every product keyword that fits.
  • For the shortlist decision, a rough estimate of monthly search volume is enough, because the goal is to see whether a market is large or small. Keyword tool volumes are approximations, so exact precision is not needed.
  • Once the summed volume of relevant keywords is clearly above the target, stop counting. Either enter the target figure itself (50,000 for the US, 20,000 for other countries) or enter the exact total, as long as the total is clearly above the target.
  • Example: a US total of roughly 200,000 monthly searches was entered as one number after clearly passing 50,000. Exact summing matters mainly when the total is close to the threshold.

Counting Keyword Volume with Ubersuggest

1 min
  • Older advice. Ubersuggest requires a paid account for full search volume data. Use this procedure for every shortlisted market idea:
    1. Sign up for an Ubersuggest account to access full search volume data.
    2. Open the Keyword Ideas section of Ubersuggest.
    3. Review the market ideas on your research tracker and enter the main keyword for one idea, for example 4x4 accessories.
    4. Set the country in the country dropdown to the market you will sell in, such as English / United States, English / United Kingdom, English / Australia, English / Canada or Japanese / Japan.
    5. Click Search.
    6. Read the VOL column for the main keyword and hover over the volume to see the exact monthly searches.
    7. Go through the suggestion list and keep only keywords that match the products you would sell.
    8. Enter product-specific keywords, such as winches for trucks, to narrow results to the right product type.
    9. Check for regional wording, such as truck accessories in the US, and search those terms too.
    10. Add up the monthly volumes of all relevant keywords for the market idea.
    11. Enter the combined total in the Traffic Volume column of the research tracker.
    12. Repeat steps 3 to 11 for every shortlisted market idea.

Filtering Keywords

1 min
  • Count a related keyword only if you could realistically sell that product. For example, winches for UTV could be counted, while winches for trailers should be excluded when the store sells truck accessories. Narrow broad terms with a qualifier such as for trucks to remove results that do not match your product.
  • Remove keyword variations that do not match what you sell or how you sell, such as local phrases like near me when the store is online. Only count terms that a buyer would type when looking for your products.
  • Broad keywords can show very large volumes but include unrelated uses, so they overstate the market. For example, the broad term winches shows about 60,000 monthly searches but includes trailer and other non-truck winches. Narrow the term to product-specific phrases before counting volume.
  • Leave out keywords that bring in products you would not sell, such as trailer winches. If a keyword is close but off-core, such as winches for UTVs, decide whether you could sell that product. To narrow results, add a product detail such as for trucks and search again.

Google Trends as a Complement

1 min
  • Google Trends can show whether interest in a product is rising and how it compares to other search terms, for example to see whether one color is trending more than another. Use it alongside click data when deciding what to test.

More rules worth knowing

1 min
  • Target a niche with at least 10,000 average monthly searches; a single narrow product keyword with around 600 monthly searches is too low to build a store on.
