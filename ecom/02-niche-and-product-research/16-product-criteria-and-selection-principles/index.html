Product criteria and selection principles

Section 2.16 · 16 of 24 in this chapter · 37 min read

Column letters (A, B, C ...) refer to the research worksheet from section 2.3.

This section has 23 parts. Tap one to open it.

Execution and mindset

2 min
  • Execution creates revenue and results, not knowledge of the framework alone. The goal is to find and launch a winning product, not to complete course modules.
  • Product research is the first and most important priority. Spend most of your early effort on finding the right product rather than building the store or designing pages, because a poorly chosen product guarantees failure regardless of store quality or ad spend.
  • Product selection matters more than platform specialisation in the early stage. A weak product cannot be rescued by a good website, creatives or ad strategy. Master one platform first, since every platform takes a long time to learn well.
  • There is no perfect product or perfect market, so do not search for one. Find products that meet your minimum set of business criteria, pick one and move on.
  • A perfect niche rarely exists. Many good stores were not perfect niches, and the first store is still a learning experience.
  • A niche that fails the verification criteria but is an emerging product can still be tested with a small store. It is a calculated gamble, not a guarantee.
  • Do not give up on a product because your first niche idea was too narrow for big results. A niche that brings in a few thousand dollars of net profit per month with minimal daily work is still a success.
  • Experience comes mainly from testing many products and learning from failed tests. Beginners should test quickly rather than wait for the perfect product.
  • Do not limit yourself to one product type, because a successful product idea is quickly copied by many stores. Apply the selection criteria rather than the example products.
  • Products with no proof of concept are riskier, but being the first to sell an item can be a long-term advantage. Start with a niche you have chosen, then test other products once you have gained experience and sales.
  • Niche selection should be based on the research criteria and your knowledge of the market, not on familiarity alone. Many successful stores were started in niches the owner knew nothing about before starting.
  • Passion for the product is not required. Basic knowledge of the niche is enough, and deeper expertise builds while you research competitors and run the store.
  • Pick the products for the store through market research, and identify which one is likely to be a best seller before building the site around it. Products chosen without research are harder to position and sell.

Written product criteria

5 min
  • Write a product criteria list before you start researching, and use it to remove candidates. A product that meets only a few of your rules (for example 3 or 5 out of 10) should be dropped even if it looks promising.
  • Write down a set of product criteria and filter every candidate through it before launching anything. A list of 50 candidates can narrow to a few strong products, which is better than testing random items one by one.
  • The criteria checklist is a guide, not a strict rulebook. Individual criteria can be weighted differently. The 3x markup criterion is especially important, while for electronics verified product quality matters more than the other points.
  • Real deal breakers are poor quality and insufficient profit margin. Other selection criteria can be less strict if a product passes these two checks.
  • Before choosing a product, confirm that it solves a problem, is marketable, fits the market and ideally has a feature that differs from what competitors offer. That feature can become the angle for a specific audience.

Procedure: product selection criteria

  1. Do you understand or care about this product?
  2. Is the price around $50? Avoid $40 or $60 starting points.
  3. Does it solve a real customer problem?
  4. Does it have visual or functional distinctiveness?
  5. Is it hard for customers to find locally in stores or online? Products that are difficult to source locally have higher profit potential.

Procedure: pre-test checklist

  1. Confirm it solves a clear problem or visually stops people while scrolling the Facebook feed.
  2. Find the same product on Amazon and confirm at least 4.2 stars as a quality signal with low refund rates.
  3. Reject glass products because of breakage and return risk.
  4. Reject heavy products because shipping costs become very high.
  5. Confirm the break-even ROAS is 1.4 to 1.5 or lower.
  6. Check competition and reject products that many advertisers already run with the same creatives.

Procedure: full product criteria checklist

  1. The product can be sold at a minimum of 3x markup over total cost (product plus shipping).
  2. Avoid heavy products, because shipping costs and damage losses become large.
  3. Avoid products tied to health risks, such as supplements or chemicals that touch the skin, unless certificates and proof exist.
  4. Avoid products with poor AliExpress ratings or recurring customer complaints.
  5. Competitors selling the product are a positive sign, since they have already tested demand.
  6. Weak competitor product pages are an opportunity to win by building a better store.
  7. Electronics have higher perceived value and can sell at 4x to 5x or more, but check reviews on AliExpress, Facebook ads and Amazon to confirm the product works, as battery and quality issues drive returns.
  8. Check Facebook ad comments for positive sentiment, realistic names from Western countries and no repeated or copy-paste comments, and click profiles to see their location.
  9. Check Google Trends for the main keyword or niche to confirm it is trending or stable.
  10. Prefer low COGS (product plus shipping), which improves cash flow and lowers break-even ROAS.
  11. Prefer products with only 2 to 3 variants, since many variants add friction and delay the purchase.
  12. Make sure high-quality images and 1 to 2 existing videos (Facebook video ads or YouTube videos) are available for repurposing.
  13. Prefer problem-solving products that give clear pain points and marketing angles for ads and the website.
  14. Prefer a wow factor: something new or innovative, or an upgraded version of something customers already know.
  15. Avoid products that are easily found in retail stores.
  16. Consider deep desires the product can address, such as survival, enjoyment of life or saving money.

Procedure: five-question screen

  1. Does the product solve a problem?
  2. Is it marketable?
  3. Is there a good fit for the product in the market?
  4. Can you segment the audience and angle the product to one specific group?
  5. Is there a specific feature that differs from what else is available and can be used in the marketing?

Procedure: self-test for products found on TikTok or AliExpress

  1. Ask yourself whether you would buy the product.
  2. Ask whether you would use it in your daily life.
  3. Ask whether family members would buy it.

A yes from yourself and from others is a strong sign that other buyers will also purchase it.

Procedure: validate a niche with people around you

  1. List the niches you find interesting and that could make money.
  2. Show candidate products to people you know, such as family or friends, and ask whether they would buy them.
  3. Test several niches in the beginning, keeping the budget small.
  4. Keep focusing on the niche that performs best for you.

Ask people in your circle whether they would buy the product, and judge by market evidence, not by whether you would buy it yourself.

Procedure: pain points and product ideas

  1. Start from a problem you or people around you struggle with.
  2. Ask what you could offer that solves that problem.
  3. Listen to calls with fulfillment partners (for example CJ Dropshipping) to learn which products customers order most.
  4. Check Etsy for products that already sell.
  5. Look at what sells well in one market and check whether it is offered in another market before testing it there.

Procedure: brainstorm worksheet

  1. Open the research worksheet, either in Google Drive or as a downloaded local file.
  2. Brainstorm product ideas and list them in column A.
  3. Look at your surroundings and daily activities and write down any item you could sell.
  4. Only add products you believe sell for $200 or more, regardless of your personal interests.
  5. Aim for at least 10 ideas, and keep adding more when they come to mind.
  6. Limit the brainstorming session to about 15 to 20 minutes.
  7. Once the initial list is complete, mark the lesson as done and continue.

Brainstorm ideas are everyday examples worth testing at a price of $200 or more: a standup desk, an ergonomic office chair, a leather club chair, a rug, a horse saddle, a 3D printer, an espresso machine, a Smith machine or a Pilates reformer.

The first brainstorm list contains hypotheses based on expected selling price, not confirmed winners. Each idea must still be tested for real demand before the store commits to it.

An AI tool can expand an initial product idea list into a longer list of candidates, but the starting list should already contain at least 10 ideas.

The product idea list is an ongoing working document, not a one-time task. Keep adding ideas when they come up in daily life rather than stopping at the first 10 entries.

Step: compare competitor products before choosing how to differentiate

  1. Buy two or more units of the leading competitor products and test them yourself.
  2. Record each weakness you find, such as poor heat retention, awkward handles or excessive size. Each weakness is a possible point of differentiation.

Problem-solving versus wow factor

4 min
  • Shift product research from searching for winning products to searching for winning problems. Identify the problem, issue, state of mind or dream outcome of the customer, then find a product that solves it.
  • Problem-solving products are the preferred type. Wow-factor products are also viable if you add clear lifestyle or experience benefits. Both types should be checked for a real demand signal before testing.
  • Prefer problem-solving products over novelty products that only have a wow effect. Customers need problem-solving products rather than just wanting them, which supports longer-lasting sales.
  • Problem-solving products usually outlast pure wow-factor products because a group of buyers already looks for a solution. The same product can often be pitched through several marketing angles, which can revive an older product.
  • Problem-solving products usually keep selling longer than novelty products, which fade once the wow factor is gone. Novelty items such as a galaxy projector tend to die out faster.
  • Products that are only unique or novel, such as simple toys and gadgets, can produce a short burst of sales. They are hard to scale and keep going long-term because they solve little real need.
  • Consider leaving a product that has a strong wow factor but solves no real problem. Such products often get views without converting. A product with a clear use typically converts better.
  • A product works when the problem is specific and the ad shows the problem and the solution quickly. Choose products that solve a clear pain point for a defined market, and use a strong marketing angle to make a simple product stand out.
  • A winning product should feel new to the viewer, meaning they have not seen it on Amazon, in ads or on YouTube. On Facebook and TikTok the creative must stop the scroll, so the product needs an immediate attention-grabbing hook.
  • On Facebook a product can also work if the audience can easily relate to the problem or concept it represents. Pure wow-factor products are riskier than products that combine attention with a clear use.
  • Products that solve a clear problem and visually stop people while scrolling must make the stop themselves, because the product has to create it.
  • Choose products by the specific problem they solve for a defined customer, not by what the seller personally likes or would buy. A clearly stated problem makes ad copy and the offer easier to write.
  • Find products by identifying a problem people face in their country or season, rather than by chasing trending winning products. A clear problem is easier to communicate in ads, for example a product that keeps people warm in a cold winter.
  • Products that solve a clear problem with very low time delay, effort and sacrifice for the customer are easier to sell, for both the offer and the ads.
  • Products with fast, visible results are easier to sell than products that need long, effortful routines. A spray tan that takes about 10 minutes is an example, compared with hours of sun exposure to get the same look.
  • A product can sell without a wow factor if a strong community or culture surrounds it, such as pet owners or hobby groups. Community strength is a signal worth weighing when choosing a niche.
  • Products with a visible, demonstrable effect are easier to show in a short video. A water bottle that shows its temperature and keeps drinks hot or cold for about 12 hours was praised in TikTok reviews and later sold on Amazon. Confirm the product solves a real everyday need, because a wow factor alone may not sustain sales.
  • Problem-solving products that people need on an ongoing basis are a strong category. A specialised knee brace is an example: it was sold only in the US with credit card and PayPal and reached about EUR 3,000 revenue per day at an early stage.
  • Products with a clear problem solution and a fast, simple buying decision work best. Choose products that buyers can decide on quickly at first sight, since first-time buyers rarely accept a complex or high-priced product. Products that need more explanation or cost more are harder to validate with cold traffic.
  • Products that solve a problem, such as cold-weather leggings in winter, tend to sell well during their season.
  • Problem-solving products sell without a strong wow factor in health, wealth, happiness and relationship niches. During spy-tool research, check which problem a product solves and what wow factor it offers, because the problem size shows how large the market can be and which complementary products fit.
  • Entertainment is also a problem a product can solve, for example through a solid toy. The niche itself matters less than the product, so follow a structured product research process instead of chasing a specific niche.
  • For products that are attractive and have high perceived value, lifestyle pictures and UGC do most of the selling. Investing in these images is worthwhile.
  • Build products and ads around desires people already have, or around rising trends, instead of trying to create demand for an unwanted need. Riding a popular trend is far cheaper than creating a new habit.
  • When researching products, first identify the problem being solved and the benefits or use cases. Upgraded versions of older winning products and products with multiple use cases are strong candidates.
  • Choose products that solve a problem or have a visual element that makes a person stop scrolling, because the product itself has to create the stop.

Conflict: wow factor as a preference

  • Source C's full checklist lists a wow factor (new, innovative or upgraded) as a preference, while the same checklist, and most other sources, put problem-solving first. Use the wow factor as a tie-breaker when the problem is already clear, not as the reason to choose a product.

Upgraded versions and differentiation

1 min
  • An upgraded version of an existing solution, such as a new design or an improved model for the same problem, can sell at a much higher price and scale. In health niches, new variations of older solutions repeatedly succeed because buyers keep looking for better results.
  • In a neck-pain niche, an upgraded version of a supplier product that cost about $50 to $60 on AliExpress or Alibaba was sold at about $244 by another seller. A better design for the same problem can support a much higher price than the basic version.
  • If a product has weak reviews but the same function exists in a better variation, source the better variation from the same supplier and sell that instead. The same function with better quality is a stronger product.
  • When a product in a niche is widely available, aim for quality above the average brand. When a common product has clear weaknesses, such as poor heat retention, bad handles or excessive size, each identified weakness is a possible point of differentiation.
  • Creating a private-label version of a main product without differentiation makes the store just another seller of the same item, which competes on price alone.
  • Customizing a product with your own creatives and messaging is recommended from the start, even though launch takes longer. Copying a product one-to-one launches faster but offers less differentiation.
  • A product that is customizable with your own creatives and messaging gives control over the ads, even if it takes longer to launch. Speed to launch is traded against control and uniqueness.
  • Product research should favour products that can be customised with your own creatives and messaging, even if launch takes about one to two weeks longer before the first product can go live.

Demand signals and proof of concept

4 min
  • Products that already show proof of demand, such as steady sales on another marketplace or a store scaling them, are safer to test than completely unproven items. Taking a proven product into a new market or segment is a common strategy.
  • Prefer products with proof of concept: check whether competitors or other stores are scaling them, and use an ad spy tool to see what is trending before committing.
  • Trend research should look for products that already have an audience and are scaling, and should be combined with checking how saturated the product is before joining the trend.
  • If a product is already scaling on another marketplace or in another store, you can enter that market with an improved version, such as one with better features, provided the market is not overly saturated.
  • Start product research with products that are already selling, because a proven product gives more reliable evidence than an untested idea. The goal is to take a product that already works and improve its offer, store and ads.
  • Look for products that already sell on the platform and in the market where you plan to run ads, because that is the proof of concept. Avoid being the first to test an untapped product, especially as a beginner. You do not need to reinvent the wheel.
  • Pick products that already sell for competitors (market-proven) instead of testing untapped products with a wow factor, because the competitor has already paid for the market test. Untested products are risky because you do not know whether the market responds to them at all.
  • Evaluate a product on three data points together: how fast a competitor is scaling, how new the competitor is in the market, and how many sales the competitor makes right now. A product that passes all three is a strong candidate to test and scale.
  • Recently appearing products usually address a large market, often estimated at 50 to 100 million potential buyers, so a competitor with a few thousand likes has probably reached only a small share of it.
  • A product where a competitor has a fresh page, a scaling ad count and confirmed sales is low risk, because the market has already proven the sales. The seller should still improve the landing page, offer and ads to win against the competitor.
  • Products with no ad proof can still be tested by sourcing them from AliExpress or CJ, asking friends and family whether they would buy, and testing with image ads. This works best for items that need little explanation.
  • An original product idea with no visible sellers is worth examining, but the lack of competitors can also mean weak demand or hard sourcing. Before committing, confirm that buyers actually search for and pay for the product, and that suppliers will sell it to a dropshipper.
  • When a product is brand new with only one or two players in the market, it can sell with minimal marketing and a decent landing page, because novelty gives an advantage. This is luck-dependent and does not replace proper research.
  • New and unique products convert better on landing pages because fewer competitors are selling them and audiences have not yet been saturated with ads. Prioritise products that have been on the market for less than 1 to 2 months.
  • A product that is new to the market is a good candidate for a short test. Use the new-product window when the market is still uncrowded, and treat it as an early signal rather than proof.
  • Both types should be checked for a real demand signal before testing, so a new product still needs the same demand check as an established one.
  • Look for demand in a market already validated for a similar product: a competitor that sells the same product, or a product that succeeds in one country before it is tested in another.
  • Bottom-of-funnel ads work when a product category is universally understood (for example phone case, water bottle, posture belt) regardless of how many competitors exist. A niche with only two sellers can still support profitable bottom-of-funnel ads if the product needs no explanation.
  • Amazon bestsellers and beginners: for beginners, products already proven on Facebook ads are safer than Amazon bestsellers, because a product that sells on search may not sell on a social platform. Once experienced, testing Amazon bestsellers on Facebook can be an advantage.
  • Concerning how many marketing angles a product needs, a product needs at least 5 distinct marketing angles drawn from detailed positive Amazon reviews to be kept, and more angles are better.
  • The first product-level check should be the product's problem, not its popularity: a product that is popular without a clear problem still needs a demand test.

Conflict: new products versus proven products

  • Some sources favour new or unique products because few competitors and fresh audiences help conversion. Others favour products that already sell for competitors because the market has paid for the test. Use proven products as the default for beginners and new products as an early test when the market is still uncrowded.
  • Being first to sell a product can be a long-term advantage, but only after you have gained experience and sales.

Market size and audience

2 min
  • Estimate the total addressable market (TAM) for each product by asking how many people actually suffer from the problem it solves. In the lesson, a market of roughly 50 to 100 million potential people was cited as a large enough example.
  • A product must appeal to a large audience. A broad audience, such as several demographic groups plus seasonal gift buyers, makes a product easier to scale than a tiny niche. Broad appeal also creates more data for testing.
  • Products with a niche angle are preferred over broad products such as general jewelry. Broad products depend more on luck, and whether they work can only be judged by their performance.
  • Products that are not sold in many stores but show strong ad counts and a clear niche can be tested, since many niche products are still under-tested.
  • Personal taste is a poor filter for product choice. Judge by market evidence, not by whether you would buy the product yourself.
  • A generic product, such as a plain sports jersey, has weak demand unless buyers have a clear reason to own it, such as supporting a specific team. Before testing, define who would buy the product and why.
  • Before testing a product, check who would use it repeatedly and whether cheap substitutes already solve the same job, such as jars or simple bag clips. A product that replaces a cheap everyday habit faces strong price resistance.
  • Phone accessories such as cases are a broad category because almost every buyer owns a phone, so a simple single-product store in that category can perform well.
  • Bottom-of-funnel and niche claims are not enough on their own; test the audience with a small budget before scaling.
  • Influencer marketing works best with small, affordable, high-margin products that can be given away free to creators. For products that are large or expensive, paid creators or other channels are more practical.
  • Find new products by looking for B2B-adjacent opportunities, such as office lighting for a home chandelier seller or a commercial-grade version of a home kitchen tool. A separate sister store is usually recommended for B2B sales, but combining B2B and B2C products in one store has also worked well.

Markup, margin and price rules

1 min
  • A product must be sellable at a minimum of 3x markup over total cost (product plus shipping).
  • Electronics can sell at 4x to 5x or more because of their higher perceived value, but verify reviews before testing.
  • Real deal breakers are poor quality and insufficient profit margin.
  • The break-even ROAS for a product should be 1.4 to 1.5 or lower.
  • Prefer low COGS (product plus shipping), which improves cash flow and lowers break-even ROAS.
  • Products that are heavy or bulky are rejected, because shipping costs rise sharply and reduce margin.

Quality, reviews and ratings

2 min
  • Require at least 4.2 stars on Amazon for a product, since this suggests acceptable quality and few refunds.
  • Avoid products whose Amazon reviews average under 4 stars. Poor reviews usually point to products that break after a short time, which damages customer trust and can increase the risk of bans.
  • The band between 4.0 and 4.2 stars is not defined in these sources. The 4.2 figure is the pre-test requirement, and under 4.0 is the avoid threshold.
  • If the identical product on Amazon has many ratings but a low average rating (for example 3.4 stars across 474 ratings), this suggests weak product quality. Rank such products lower on the list.
  • Avoid products with poor AliExpress ratings or recurring customer complaints.
  • Quality matters more than virality, because a viral video for a weak product brings little interest and few sales. Use going viral as a skill to be learned first, then choose only good products once you can reliably generate views.
  • Quality checks matter most for beauty and health products, because a poor-quality product can harm the buyer and damage a store's reputation. Quality problems should be treated as a red flag before any ad budget is spent.
  • When judging a product, check its size, weight and price first, then think about the customer experience. Choose products where customers are lenient about delays and mistakes, because satisfied buyers return and recommend the store.
  • Images and videos matter most in visual niches such as fashion and jewelry, but they matter in all niches. Beginners with small budgets can repurpose existing competitor or YouTube videos instead of paying for new shoots. AI tools such as Canva can also create product images.
  • Make sure high-quality images and 1 to 2 existing videos (Facebook video ads or YouTube videos) are available for repurposing.
  • Check Facebook ad comments for positive sentiment, realistic names from Western countries and no repeated or copy-paste comments, and click profiles to see their location.
  • Check Google Trends for the main keyword or niche to confirm it is trending or stable.
  • A simple product can sell thousands of orders without looking special. A sheet of paper with 100 ideas for a first date sold thousands of orders in a single season, which shows that a product does not need to look special and must be tested to know if it wins.

Case: a niche store reaches its first profit

  • A dropshipper with about six months of experience had started with a done-for-you agency. He had about five years of marketing experience, mostly B2B work with Google, Facebook and LinkedIn ads, and had tested over 20 products without a clear selection method.
  • He pulled a list of 50 product candidates from TikTok, Facebook ads and spy tools, and filtered it with product criteria, which narrowed it to 2 products. He launched one of them, which became his most successful store so far.
  • He first tested Canada, then launched in Australia and New Zealand, where he got his lowest cost per lead and his first profitable sales. Before that he had been breaking even with one to two sales per day, and one Friday he got five sales.

Product types to avoid

4 min

Avoid by material, size or shipping

  • Avoid glass products because of the high risk of breakage and returns.
  • Avoid heavy products, because shipping costs rise sharply and reduce the margin.
  • Avoid bulky products, because shipping costs eat the margin. Prefer small, light products that solve a clear problem.
  • Avoid very large or bulky products when selecting candidates, because the supplier can charge high shipping costs that reduce margin. Products of this size were excluded from the list.
  • Avoid liquids.

Avoid by category or sales channel

  • Avoid electronics, because they break quickly and generate complaints and returns. Home decor is a better choice when it is light enough to ship cheaply.
  • Electronics are an exception to the markup rule only if reviews confirm the product works.
  • Avoid products that can easily be bought at local stores.
  • Avoid products that look like established brands, since they are hard to differentiate.
  • Avoid products with visible Chinese writing or third-party brand logos, such as a Samsung logo, in the ad video or product. Do not test such products even if their ad counts look strong.
  • Avoid products linked to the Kardashians, or products from GoPure, MaryRuth, URO, Lemme, or any book from Global Brothers.
  • Avoid product categories such as shilajit, ashwagandha, perfumes, anything featured on Shark Tank, clothing and baby items when choosing products to test.
  • Avoid products tied to health risks, such as supplements or chemicals that touch the skin, unless certificates and proof exist.
  • Avoid products linked to a single event. Prefer products that can be sold for one to three years or more, can be branded and can reach new audiences. Products tied only to one event, such as Valentine's Day, Father's Day or Mother's Day, have a short sales window and cannot be scaled much after the event.
  • Pump-and-dump products are driven by a trend, season, aesthetic or impulse buying, are easy to copy and are not meant to become long-term brands. They usually have no brand moat, and many sellers can enter quickly.
  • Pump-and-dump products that win are usually simple, common and already sold, so the edge comes from marketing rather than invention. Beginners should not expect the product itself to be unique.
  • Pump-and-dump products fit the emotional vibe of a season through colour, sound and visual style. Warm, soft, cozy and neutral tones suit fall. Products that look like they belong to the season sell by feeling, not by specification.

Avoid by harm, use or sizing

  • Products sold for use on the skin, such as foundation, are not recommended for a dropshipping store.
  • Be careful with items applied to the face or ingested, such as cosmetics, because the buyer's trust and the product's safety are at stake. Only sell products you would use or consume yourself.
  • Reject products where wrong use can harm the buyer, such as non-prescription eyewear sold as corrective glasses. Harm leads to complaints, refunds and legal risk that outweigh the sales potential.
  • Customizable products are not recommended for beginners.
  • Swimwear is not recommended because of sizing issues and ad restrictions.
  • Avoid apparel that often causes sizing problems.
  • For fashion products, prefer items sold in one size where possible, because they avoid most sizing problems and the returns that come with them.
  • Pants and shoes caused fewer sizing problems than jackets and shirts. Products without sizing avoid sizing returns entirely.
  • A generic product, such as a plain sports jersey, has weak demand unless buyers have a clear reason to own it.
  • Products that are only unique or novel, such as simple toys and gadgets, are hard to scale and keep going long-term.
  • Avoid products whose Amazon reviews average under 4 stars.
  • Avoid products with recurring complaints about breakage or battery issues.

Avoid by buyer decision

  • Choose products that buyers can decide on quickly at first sight. First-time buyers rarely accept a complex or high-priced product, and such products are harder to validate with cold traffic.

Conflict: fashion as a niche

  • For a brand, fashion and jewelry are the easiest niches to build around, since almost any product can be branded. Start from knowing the target audience and build the brand around the product.
  • Fashion products were common among the trending items, and fashion was described as a good niche at the beginning of the year. Customizable fashion products are still not recommended for beginners.
  • One operator reported that roughly 80% of his sales came from women's fashion, so this category can be prioritised when shortlisting products. Treat this as a single-source signal and confirm it with your own test results before scaling.
  • Apparel is avoided where sizing causes returns, but one-size fashion items avoid most of that risk.

Product types to favour

1 min
  • Jewelry and bags, such as shoulder or crossbody bags, are suggested for beginners, since they carry less sizing risk and can have strong margins when the perceived value is high. Jewelry sells well on Facebook.
  • Choose products based on data, quality and demand instead of limiting the options by category. A focused niche can be developed later, while a second store can use other niches.
  • Small, light products with a visible problem-solving use, a strong visual design, a fair price, and simple variants such as colour or plug type fit the favoured profile. Use these traits as a filter when shortlisting candidates.
  • Home decor is a better choice when it is light enough to ship cheaply.
  • Vacation-related products can work because demand for travel has grown after the lockdowns.
  • Products that replace a cheap everyday habit face strong price resistance, so favour products that solve a need the buyer does not already cover cheaply.
  • Choose products you can make content about two to three times a day, because a product that is hard to film or present will not be uploaded consistently. Convenience of content creation is a selection criterion.
  • An upgraded version of a winning product, or a product with several use cases, is a strong candidate.
  • Products in categories where the problem is ongoing and recurring, such as the knee brace, are favoured.
  • Look for products where a problem is recognised and the audience already looks for a solution.

Private label and white label

2 min
  • Private label and white label products work well because they carry your own unique brand name and product identifier. Google will not associate your white-labelled products with competing brand names.
  • Start by selling a generic product and watch which products get traffic and sell best and what buyers like or dislike. Then private label a product that is the best of the best in the niche.
  • You do not have to sell every product a brand offers. You can choose which items to list from the catalogue, keeping only the products that fit your niche and margins.
  • When choosing a product to develop as a private-label item, the lesson sorts candidates into four types: accessory, smaller item, lead magnet or large product. Each type can serve as a fit for a private-label launch, so match the product type to your own capacity and goals.
  • Accessories that you already sell, or that you should sell but skipped because the margin looked too thin, are good candidates for private labelling. Review both groups.
  • Neoprene wine bottle holders protect bottles from knocking together during transport and help keep their temperature. Buyers of wine coolers, wine cellar equipment and other wine-industry products are a natural audience.
  • Pellet grill accessories form a large range of white-label and private-label candidates: utensils, pellets, cedar planks, remote thermometers, smoking racks, grill covers, slicing knives, marinades and rubs, meat shredding claws, and cast iron cookware.

Lead magnets and cross-sell

  • A lead magnet is a low-cost product that brings buyers into the store. Check whether its buyers already own or plan to buy a related main product, such as a wine cooler. Then remarket them with the main product or related items.
  • Neoprene wine bottle holders fit a lead-magnet role for buyers of wine coolers, wine cellar equipment or other wine-industry products.
  • Lead-magnet products can be sold on marketplaces such as Amazon or on Etsy, with custom sayings or designs, to reach customers in the same niche before they reach your own store.
  • To find upsell and cross-sell products, check the "frequently bought together" section on Amazon for the main product. Alternatively, open the AliExpress store that sells the original listing, because each listing belongs to a store that usually carries related products.
  • Once a hero product is chosen, upsell and cross-sell products should be small and closely related to it. Complex unique products would need their own landing page, which adds friction.

Case: travelling pellet grill

  • The store owner received white-label offers from a major pellet grill brand's manufacturer and wanted a version with clear differentiation.
  • Repeated customer feedback showed that portability was a real problem for pellet grill buyers. Only one grill on the market addressed it, and it sold strongly.
  • The owner bought two units to test them and found weaknesses: poor heat retention, clunky handles that doubled as legs, and a large size.
  • The proposed improvement was a smaller grill sized for a single brisket, with better heat insulation (for example, a fitted cover) and better handles. It was to be sourced from an American manufacturer and built out through manufacturer outreach, which was expected to be difficult but likely successful.

Air purifiers: a worked criteria set

1 min

Competitor guides weigh tool-free filter changes, ozone emissions, upkeep costs over the unit's life, warranty length, weight and portability, and filter-change monitoring. Use these criteria to judge which brands are worth listing for buyers with allergies, asthma or chemical sensitivities.

  • A unit should exchange the room's air about 5 times per hour, with 2 air exchanges per hour as the minimum, depending on room size. A unit rated for a larger room than needed can run on lower speeds, which makes it quieter.
  • Room size is a major factor when choosing an air purifier, because the unit must exchange the room's air several times per hour.
  • A reliable warranty covers at least 5 years on the main fan and motor parts. The filters are the only part expected to need regular cleaning or replacement.
  • Any ozone emission from an air purifier is undesirable. It matters most for buyers with allergies, asthma or multiple chemical sensitivities, because ozone irritates the respiratory system. Filter out ozone-emitting models when selecting products for these buyers.
  • CADR (Clean Air Delivery Rate), certified by AHAM, shows how much purified air a unit delivers and is used to compare air purifiers. Higher CADR figures indicate higher output for the same type of unit.
  • Combining gas or odour filtration and HEPA filtration in a single cartridge is criticised, because the two filter types degrade at different rates. Separate filters are generally preferred.
  • Products with filters that are easy to change without tools are preferred. The best designs let the user open a door, pull out the filter and insert a new one by hand. Filters that need a screwdriver, the unit turned upside down or are hard to reach are often changed late, past the recommended date, which reduces performance.
  • Upkeep cost is often overlooked by buyers. Some of the most expensive air purifiers cost less to maintain over their lifetime, and units without filters, such as some air sterilisers, have no filter cost at all.

Product idea examples and brainstorm inputs

1 min
  • Product ideas can come from items visible in everyday life. Each brainstorm candidate is a candidate to test for a price of $200 or more.
  • Product ideas on the first brainstorm list are hypotheses, not confirmed winners.
  • Selection should be based on criteria, not on the example products. Apply the selection criteria rather than copying the examples.
  • Passion is not a selection criterion. Product ideas should be selected on the expected selling price.

Conflict: passion, understanding and personal use

  • Passion for a product is not a valid selection criterion, and it is not required.
  • Only sell products you believe in and would use yourself, because selling something you understand makes the marketing and customer answers easier. Spend a couple of weeks researching which brands are actually best before choosing a product.
  • Do not try to sell products you do not understand, because you will struggle to connect authentically with the product, create convincing marketing or answer customer questions.
  • Choosing products only because you like them or would buy them is a mistake. Test for the average customer in your target market. A novelty window-mounted cat bed and phone cases sold little or nothing for one seller, because they had no wow factor and no culture around them.
  • Choosing a product because of personal passion is not a valid selection criterion. Product ideas should be selected based on the expected selling price.
  • Niche selection should be based on research criteria and market knowledge, not on familiarity alone.
  • Products that are not sold in many stores but show strong ad counts and a clear niche can be tested.
  • Only sell products you would use or consume yourself.

Reconciling these: understanding the product and being able to use it is useful; passion and personal taste are not criteria. Judge a product by its problem, its price, its margin and the market's response.

Testing mindset and rules of thumb

1 min
  • Pick a niche that meets the criteria and follow the process, because a perfect niche rarely exists.
  • Product quality matters more than virality.
  • Product selection matters more than platform specialisation.
  • Do not run products with no ad proof without a small test. Use image ads and friend and family checks first.
  • Build the list of product ideas continuously; keep adding ideas from daily life.
  • Brand-new products with few sellers can sell with minimal marketing, but this depends on luck, so keep research as the main method.
  • Before testing a product, confirm it solves a problem and stands out.
  • Prefer products with only 2 to 3 variants.

Problem-Solving Versus Wow-Factor Products

1 min

Two Requirements for a Product Worth Testing

  • A product worth testing needs two things: room to scale, and support for many engaging short videos.
  • A product can fit a popular category and still fail if it is poorly built or poorly structured.

Wow-Factor Products

  • A wow-factor product that is hard to buy locally can go viral quickly, because viewers assume the seller has exclusive access.
  • Once viewers discover that the product does not work as shown, conversion drops sharply and the product is exposed.
  • A wow factor only carries a product if the product actually delivers what the video shows.
  • Before you publish a store for a wow-factor item, check that it works properly.
  • A simple, common item that buyers can easily get themselves is hard to sell at a high price. Example: an ice product listed at about 17 to 18 USD.

Problem-Solving Products

  • Products that solve a problem, especially medical-type or health-related solutions, were described as the best performers.
  • Slight improvements on proven products are also worth testing.

Quick Money-Grab Products

  • Quick money-grab products may not solve a real problem but can generate a few thousand USD.
  • Use them as a reasonable test to practise hooks, angles and video production before testing a product with higher potential.

Quality, Safety and Red Flags

1 min
  • Check product quality with extra care for beauty and health products. A poor product in these categories can harm the customer and creates a red flag.

Product Types to Favour

1 min

Economy-Driven Choices

  • Choose products by the economy. In a cost-of-living crisis, people go out less and spend more at home.
  • In that period, home cooking, home entertainment and kids' board games sold well, and TikTok cooking content performed well.

Household and Seasonal Items

  • Household items were described as strong sellers in general.
