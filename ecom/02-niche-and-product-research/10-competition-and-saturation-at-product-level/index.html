Competition and saturation at product level

Section 2.10 · 10 of 24 in this chapter · 39 min read

Column letters (A, B, C ...) refer to the research worksheet from section 2.3.

This section has 22 parts. Tap one to open it.

Judge competition per product, not per niche

3 min
  • Competition shows up at the product level, so one niche can contain products with very different competitive intensity. Evaluate individual products rather than whole niches.
  • Saturation is determined at product level. A niche can be unsaturated overall while its most popular brands and products are too contested. Run the "How Much Competition Is Too Much Competition" test on individual products.
  • Competition is judged on Google Shopping at product level, not by the number of stores in the niche. A niche is not too crowded just because many stores sell in it. What matters is how many retailers sell the same brand's products.
  • Competition occurs at product level, not niche level. A crowded niche still contains many low-competition products, and those products are the opening for new stores.
  • Products that few competitors bid on are often the easiest way into a crowded niche. Traffic should come from many products, not only the top few Google Shopping results.
  • Look for pockets of opportunity inside a crowded niche, such as products where few competitors run ads.
  • Competition is a per-product question, and new products appear every year. Some top sellers operate in niches that look saturated, so a niche should not be dropped only because it looks crowded.
  • Before dropping a niche that looks saturated, assess competition at product level, because the niche may still have room for individual products.
  • A crowded niche can work if each specific product has only a few sellers, because paid traffic is bought per product.
  • Research of niches, suppliers and products reveals real opportunities even when many new sellers enter the market.
  • Judge competition at supplier and brand level, not only overall niche. A niche with major players can still contain many brands and products that a new store can sell.
  • Check how many stores sell the brands buyers search for, not how many stores exist in a broad niche.
  • Count how many stores advertise each brand and product, rather than counting competitors in the whole niche.
  • Build a master list of competitors for each niche and judge how much competition is too much before committing. A niche with saturated competition is a poor choice for a first store.
  • Saturation of a product market should be checked at product level before committing, using the saturation tests in the training material.
  • Search the supplier's name in Google Shopping to judge how saturated a supplier's brand is. The supplier-name search gives the needed information; product-level searches are optional.
  • Brand-level limits: if one brand's products appear under more than about 25 online stores on Google Shopping, skip that brand and focus on brands that few retailers sell. Carve out space by choosing brands that are not open to everyone.
  • Brands sold in more than 50 stores should not be included in Google Product Listing Ads, because paid clicks would compete with too many retailers. Such brands can still be uploaded to the store for free organic traffic, while ad spend goes to brands with less competition.
  • Before committing to a niche, confirm it has supplier options and enough sellers for the brands. A niche with too many paid advertisers for the same brands is harder to win through ads.
  • A niche can have many products and many competing brands and still work if the brands are not oversold. Even with a few hundred competing products, a niche is worth testing if enough brands exist and demand is confirmed.
  • Sales are usually concentrated in a small share of products, often around 20 percent of them. The number of products alone does not predict revenue; demand and product-level competition decide the outcome.

Count direct competitors, not every seller

4 min

Who counts and who does not

  • Competitors fall into three categories.
Category Examples Counting rule Source
Marketplace sellers Large multi-category platforms such as Amazon, eBay, Walmart Not counted in the total; they market on a different basis and do not target your customer. Note they exist, then exclude.
Own-brand sellers Companies selling only their own brand Count as 0.5 each
Direct competitors Market-specific resellers selling other brands, whether or not they dropship Count as 1 each
  • Only direct competitors count fully in the total.
  • Marketplace examples excluded from the count: in the US, Amazon, eBay, Walmart and Home Depot; in Australia, catch.com.au, Kogan and former chains such as Dick Smith.
  • A seller counts as a direct competitor only if it is specific to your niche. Stores that sell mostly other niches are not counted, for example a Mustang-focused parts store that added truck parts, or a bumper store that added a few extra products.
  • Sellers with multiple unrelated categories, such as snowblower specialists, power equipment or broad auto accessories shops, are generally not counted as direct competitors unless their focus is specific to your niche.
  • If unsure whether a seller is a direct competitor from its name, click through and visit its website. Check whether it focuses on your niche or sells across unrelated categories.
  • Identify dropshipper competitors by generic products without a brand logo, non-custom content and no unique product feature. Dropshipper-only competitors still count toward the competitor total.

Search per product type

  • Search individual product types rather than adding up all competitors across a broad category, because a broad search such as "4x4 accessories" returns too wide a result to analyse.
  • Search the specific product type you plan to sell, for example "truck winches" rather than "4x4 accessories". For a narrow market like CNC machines, search the market term itself.
  • Competition varies by brand and price band within the same product type. Compare sellers across brands and price ranges rather than relying on one product's result.
  • Worked example: for US truck winches, after filtering out marketplaces and non-specific stores, direct competitors reached around 11 for one product and then rose beyond the 15 limit for another brand's list.

Country benchmarks

Market Benchmark (direct competitors per product type or market) Source
United States 15 or fewer
Australia, UK, Europe and other markets outside the US 10 or fewer in the market you plan to sell in
  • Judge competition per market and country, not in absolute terms.
  • Some competition is a good sign because it shows an established group of buyers is already served. The goal is to avoid markets that are either too crowded or empty.
  • The US market is larger, so higher competitor counts are expected there. A smaller country with the same number of competitors as the US signals too much competition for its smaller customer pool.

Per-product range

  • Target 5 to 15 competitors on average for each individual product, counted as the number of sellers Google Shopping shows as "compare prices from N stores". Results showing 10+ are borderline; 20+, 30+ or 50+ signal a market to avoid.
  • Competitor count per individual product should be between 5 and 15. Fewer than 5 usually means a very small market or little buying interest; more than 15 is too crowded for a new store to enter.

Averaging across product types

  • Record competition by averaging across product types: note each product type's competitor count, average them into one figure, and enter that average in the spreadsheet's Competition column for the market idea.
  • Averaging example: winches averaging 15, rear drawers averaging 10 and turbo systems averaging 8 give a broader category average of 13 direct competitors. That is below the US benchmark of 15 and therefore acceptable.

Competitor research procedure (market idea)

  1. Search Google for the specific product type you plan to sell, not the broad market category (e.g. "truck winches" rather than "4x4 accessories"). For a narrow market like CNC machines, search the market term itself.
  2. Open the Google Shopping results (Shopping tab) and scroll to the individual seller list below the horizontal sponsored results.
  3. Look for the "Compare prices from X stores" line on each product listing; the number tells you how many sellers offer that product.
  4. Click into listings with a high store count to open the full seller list.
  5. Sort sellers into marketplaces (exclude), own-brand sellers (count 0.5) and market-specific resellers (count 1), checking unclear sellers by visiting their websites.
  6. Total the direct competitors for that product type.
  7. Repeat for each product type within the market idea (e.g. winches, rear drawers, turbo systems, nudge bars, bull bars).
  8. Average the counts across product types to get the market's competitor figure for the spreadsheet's Competition column, and compare it against the benchmark for your country.
  9. Repeat for each shortlisted idea (five in the course example).

Competition as an elimination criterion

  • Competition is the biggest research criterion that can eliminate a product. If a product shows poor competition results, do not proceed with it regardless of the other criteria.

Google Shopping: how to count and read the results

3 min

Basic reading

  • The "compare prices from X stores" line on a grouped listing is a practical count of competing sellers for that product.
  • A search on Google for the product type, with Google Shopping results, shows which sellers pay for product ads in the category. Sellers appearing there signal paid competition, and the results can be checked for specialist versus general sellers.
  • Sellers in Google Shopping are already paying for ads. This matters because Google Shopping is one of the primary marketing channels for launching a dropshipping business.
  • Google's "compare at prices" listing on a shopping search reflects the same competitor signal as a count of five or more stores selling the product.
  • A listing showing "compare prices from 2 stores" or "compare prices from 5+ stores" is normal in the US and other markets.
  • Google Shopping results vary by user location and search history. If your results look different, interpret them with common sense rather than assuming an error.

Count distinct brands and listings

  • Count distinct brands and listings, not just total products, because one supplier can have many products but few brands.
  • Keep big box retailers in the results, since they still count in the price averages.
  • If two or three brands dominate Google Shopping results, the niche may be locked up, but a few prominent listings alone do not prove that. Before dropping the niche, check the future competitor stores and count their suppliers.
  • A quick way to count sellers for a brand is to search each brand or product on Google Shopping and read how many stores are listed. There is no shortcut that avoids checking brands one by one.

Seller quality and filters

  • Check seller ratings and positive feedback percentages on Google Shopping's seller list to gauge how established a competitor is. Examples: 65% positive feedback on 646 reviews; 98% on 1,323. Also check the price range filter to compare brands within the same price band.
  • Price bands for a US search (compare competitor counts only within the band that matches your planned selling price):
Price band Source
Up to $1,000
$1,000-$7,000
$7,000-$20,000
Over $20,000
  • The same product type can show very different seller numbers across bands.
  • Other Google Shopping filters in the left column: Show only (Buy on Google, On sale), Condition (New items, Used items), Features (for example For ATV, For Towing), Shipping (for example Free shipping), Product rating (for example 4 and up) and Seller. Use Condition and Product rating to see whether competitors sell new goods and how well they are rated.

Procedures

Competition check in Google Shopping (2021)

  1. Search the main niche keyword, not a supplier or brand name.
  2. Review the first page of results.
  3. Count the distinct brands and listings, not just the total products, because one supplier can have many products but few brands.
  4. Keep big box retailers in the results, since they still count in the price averages.

Check competition for a product on Google Shopping (2023)

  1. Search the brand or product name on Google Shopping, not with a keyword search on your own niche.
  2. Count how many stores list that product on the first results page.
  3. Keep the product if more than half of the first page shows products sold by fewer than 10 stores. Treat products with many listings as too competitive for paid traffic.

Procedure for checking how many stores advertise the same product (2024)

  1. Search for the product on Google Shopping.
  2. Ignore the Sponsored carousel at the top, because it shows one store per ad rather than the full competitor count.
  3. Scroll to the All products section and gather data from the listings there.
  4. If a product shows only one store, it has one seller. If it shows an "and more" link, click it to see the full list of advertising stores.
  5. Count Amazon, Walmart, Home Depot and Wayfair listings as competitors, since they advertise the same product.

Check on Google Shopping for each product

  • Check competition on the first page of Google Shopping results for each product before committing to it.

First-page share tests

  • On the first page of Google Shopping organic results, if more than half of the products come from stores with 10 or more sellers, the niche is too crowded. In that case, move up at least one niche level.
  • On the compare-price listings, more than half of the results should come from 10 or fewer stores for a good niche. Count only compare-price listings, not sponsored results.
  • A product is a red flag if the competition test shows high competition right away and you cannot change the keyword or niche to reduce it.
  • During the competition test, the suppliers and brands on the first page of results signal how crowded a product search is. If heavy competition from these brands shows right away and you cannot narrow the keyword or niche, treat it as a warning sign.
  • Two statements from 2023 conflict. One says to keep the product if more than half of the first page shows products sold by fewer than 10 stores. The other says the benchmark is under half of results from ten or fewer stores. The two cannot both be read literally. Confirm against the source before applying either; the majority reading in other units favours "more than half from 10 or fewer stores".

How to read the thresholds together

  • Sources give different values because they count different things. Source B's figures count direct competitors and exclude marketplaces, with 15 for the US and 10 elsewhere. Source A's figures count listings and stores on the Google Shopping results page.
  • When to use the direct-competitor count: for a market-level go or no-go decision by country.
  • When to use the first-page store count: for checking whether a specific product's paid traffic is viable.

Big-box retailers and marketplaces

4 min

Big-box presence

  • Big box presence (Walmart, Home Depot, Amazon) selling a category does not eliminate opportunity. Multiple suppliers and variations exist within categories sold by big-box retailers. Example: chandeliers appear at Home Depot and Lowe's, yet hundreds of online chandelier suppliers are profitable.
  • Chandeliers are sold in big box chains that carry about five brands, while the market has roughly 500 to 600 brands with many variants. The opportunity is in selling the brands and variants those chains do not stock.
  • Treat a brand sold in physical big-box stores as a competition warning, but brands sold only by online retailers are acceptable. A niche can still have room when big-box stores stock only a handful of brands while hundreds exist in that category.
  • Compare the number of brands sold by big-box retailers with the number sold by online-only sellers. If big-box stores carry only about five brands with limited stock while online sellers offer hundreds of companies and thousands of products, the niche has room for an online store despite local competition.
  • A product type sold by big home-improvement chains can still have hundreds of suppliers with different products suitable for online sale. A few brands that also run showrooms do not rule the niche out.
  • A niche that Walmart or Home Depot also sells is not disqualifying if the niche is broad, with thousands of products from hundreds of companies. Big retailers carry only a small slice, so shoppers cannot find the exact product locally.
  • Big box retailers on the first results pages are not a problem if the niche contains many brands and products they do not carry. They are a concern only when a few big players dominate the niche.
  • Use local availability as a filter: if buyers can easily find the same product, with the size, colour or pattern they want, at nearby home improvement or big-box stores, online dropshipping has less advantage. Products with many sizes, colours and patterns that local stores do not fully stock are stronger candidates.

Large niches with many brands

  • Large niches like furniture can support multiple successful stores because they have thousands of brands, tens of thousands of products, and new ones introduced yearly. High competition does not eliminate opportunity if supplier count is sufficient.
  • Furniture is a large viable niche with thousands of brands manufacturing tens of thousands of different products, with new styles introduced yearly. This scale creates room for multiple new stores without market saturation.

Marketplaces as competitors

  • Large online marketplaces that host many third-party sellers and are not niche-specific count as weak competitors. If they are the main results in Google Shopping for a niche, that is usually a sign of opportunity.
  • Products that appear on eBay, Walmart Marketplace or Overstock at lower prices are not a threat in most niches. Walmart Marketplace listings are often sold by third-party sellers rather than Walmart itself.
  • Walmart's ads mostly promote third-party marketplace sellers rather than products Walmart stocks itself. Because that marketplace is not drop-shipping, a niche specialist store with a focused buyer does not need to treat Walmart as direct competition.
  • Many sellers in a niche, including large marketplaces such as Amazon and eBay, are not a problem. You can stand out through usability, service and offers rather than trying to match their scale.
  • Large marketplaces such as Wayfair selling the same products are not a reason to drop a niche. Being the focused expert in one niche is the advantage against general shopping sites.
  • Marketplaces such as Amazon, eBay, Etsy and Target count as sellers in the 10-plus store test. The test measures how many sellers carry the product, so the presence of marketplaces alone does not decide whether a niche qualifies.
  • Sources differ on whether marketplaces count. Excluded from the direct-competitor total; included in the store-count tests as sellers. Use the exclusion for the market benchmark and the inclusion for the first-page store test.
  • A niche where most sales go through eBay is a warning sign, because the main brands would compete directly with eBay. Look for suppliers whose products are not sold on eBay. If the top brands are only there, consider building a store anyway, since Google Shopping competition may be lower.
  • Phone accessories such as screen protectors and cases are heavily sold on Amazon, which makes organic TikTok sales difficult for them. Dropshipping is usually not much affected by Amazon, but in these categories Amazon can dominate quickly.
  • Avoid products that are easy to find on Amazon with simple keywords and have sellers with 5,000+ reviews. Buyers who see your ad will search Amazon and find a cheaper or more trusted listing. The product should be hard to find elsewhere, so customers trust your store as the owner.

Ad-library and platform saturation signals

4 min

Reading ad counts

  • Do not judge product saturation on ad count alone. Test ads first; only after running campaigns can you accurately assess market oversaturation.
  • Finding the same product across multiple competing stores running ads indicates market saturation, but this is not conclusive. Run actual ad campaigns before excluding a product.
  • Do not stop a product for being saturated if competitors' ads are actively scaling. A product is worth testing if competitor ads show it running at scale. If competitors are not scaling, look for a new competitor or a new product instead.
  • Checking competitor ad activity in the target market helps judge a product, because a product easily scaled in one market is often being scaled by competitors there too.
  • A product is not saturated until its market and targeting are saturated. The same product can keep selling by changing demographic, platform, country or marketing angle.
  • The Ad Library is a search tool for count and duration. A product that many accounts already promote with the same video style is considered saturated.

Costs and timing

  • When many advertisers run the same product, CPMs and CPAs rise over time as copies appear. A product that was unsaturated at launch can become expensive after 1 to 1.5 years.
  • Check how long a product has been circulating, using the earliest video dates on TikTok or ads. Products that have been around for years may already be saturated; newer products may still have room to grow.
  • A product can stop performing when many competitors begin selling the same item, so check how many stores advertise it before testing. A product's early success does not guarantee long-term demand.
  • Gap between competitive and uncompetitive products: a well-tested product might bring 20 to 50 sales per day at 5x to 10x ROAS, while a crowded product brings 1 to 2 sales per day.
  • Your pixel versus competitors' pixels: a product a competitor is scaling may not work for you because their pixel already has data on their buyers, while a new pixel has none. Early entry into a product with few competitors can give that seller a data advantage.

Sales concentration and sellers

  • A high concentration rate in Kalodata, such as 91.6% on a 24.99 USD study guide, means most sales come from a few sellers. Treat it as a warning and check how many creators drive the revenue before entering the niche.
  • Products with thousands of sales on AliExpress often already have a dedicated supplier and brand competing for the same buyers. Strong sales volume can signal a crowded market, not an easy opening.
  • Visible sales by other sellers show demand. Competition is healthy in a market only if it is not overcrowded. Sellers can check which stores make the most sales in a niche and compare them.
  • Before creating an audience, check whether a niche has enough demand, which can be seen from other sellers doing well. Competition is good as long as it is not too heavy, since many sellers suggest demand exists.

Procedures

Competitor check before testing a product

  1. Find competitors running ads for the same product, for example in the Meta Ad Library.
  2. Check whether those ads are running at scale and for how long.
  3. Review their creatives and landing pages.
  4. If the competitor ads are scaling, test the product. If not, look for a new competitor or a new product.

Checking whether a product is unique or saturated for a country

  1. Search the product on Amazon; more than 20 listings suggests the product has been on the market for a long time.
  2. Search the product name in the Facebook Ad Library or a spy tool, filtered by the target country.
  3. If multiple stores run ads for the same product in that country, and some ads have run for about a year, the product is already established.
  4. Use these checks together to measure how many competitors and ads exist and how long they have been running.

Rip-check a product before testing it

  1. Search the product by name or image in Magic AI, or open it from the TikTok Shop product list.
  2. Review which stores and advertisers already sell or run the same or a similar product.
  3. If few or no results appear, the product is not saturated and can be tested.
  4. If many sellers already run it, skip the product.
  • Skipping a product that is already heavily ripped saves roughly $20 to $100 in ad spend per test. The lesson presents this as the main financial reason to run the check before every test.

Competition check before launch

  • Search the Facebook Ad Library and the TikTok ads library (TikTok Ads, abbreviated as "pp ads" in the lesson) to find big players selling the same product. See how long and how heavily they run it.
  • Check how many competitors run the product in the target country before entering it.

AdSpy-style competitor check

  • Search the Meta Ad Library with product keywords and count the number of advertisers, or use an ads spy tool, to gauge how much competition a product has.

Keyword count for competitor search

  • Keep 10 to 15 keywords per product for the competitor search: product name, name variations, name subsets, niche keywords, product-related keywords and ad copy keywords from the ad spy research.

TikTok

  • On TikTok, a product with 50,000 or more ad impressions shows active competition. Products with 1 million or more impressions in a single ad indicate strong winning creatives worth studying.

Creative lessons from saturated products

  • A product that has already gone viral also shows which video styles work, which gives a roadmap for your own videos. Check saturation before copying a viral one, because a saturated product can still sell with a clear angle, but a product with a bad reputation or heavy overexposure becomes a weak bet.
  • A face-care ice-cube product was filled with water and frozen, and was described as a six-figure product. A small test earlier had made a few thousand dollars, the margin was high at the time, and the product became saturated as many viewers recognised it. Viewers also said it was just an ice cube from the refrigerator, so the product must show a clear problem and solution to sell.

Saturation is not a blocker

3 min

Principles

  • Saturation is not a blocker. Competitors do not eliminate opportunity because new brands continuously appear, existing brands launch new product lines, and superior website optimisation and customer acquisition outperform lazy early entrants.
  • Competitors in a niche validate the market rather than eliminate opportunity. You can profit by bringing your product to a new market segment within an existing category.
  • Saturation is not a reason to reject a product, because many competing ads show that money is being made in that product. What decides success is your own creative, your angle and your execution.
  • A product with high potential has a large addressable audience. A cozy fall sock example was estimated at over 100 million potential buyers. The number of people who could buy is what matters, not whether a single competitor covers part of the market.
  • A product that has only recently appeared often sits in a large niche market, where competitors have reached only a small share so far. Niche markets often span 10 to 100 million potential people, so new entrants still have room to grow.
  • Some competition is a good sign that a niche has demand. Do not drop a niche just because many sellers are already in it.
  • Forty dropshipping competitors in a niche is a good sign of market viability. Many competing stores show that people are buying.
  • Generic-looking product names from suppliers are not a problem by themselves. What matters is that the niche passes the tests. Products that are not unique can still sell when many brands in the niche draw buyers.
  • Boring niches can sell well. Success often comes from finding an opportunity within brands and products in an apparently saturated industry.
  • Niches with dominant brands can still be entered when the margins work. Look for weaknesses that competitors do not address and do those things better.
  • A crowded niche with many products and a few hundred competing products can still work if brands are not oversold.
  • Low competitor counts for a single product are not the only sign of saturation. With tens of thousands of niches available, a new store does not need to avoid every niche that others also sell in. It must never copy a competitor's site.
  • Competition is healthy in a market only if it is not overcrowded.
  • A viable market needs some existing competition, because it proves people already buy that product online. A market with zero competitors usually has no online buyers and is not viable.
  • Competition in this model happens at product level, so a strong niche can still contain products with room.
  • A product's saturation can be worked around by a beginner or an experienced advertiser. A beginner should look for a proven product with active ads and then find a new angle or an audience competitors have not reached yet.

Proven demand with unproven competition

  • Proven demand combined with unproven competition is the opportunity to look for. A product or language market with little competition can beat a larger, saturated market.

Products with no competition

  • A niche with no direct competition is acceptable only if the product type is actively sold elsewhere online. If the products are not sold by anyone on the internet, the market likely does not exist for good reason and should be avoided.
  • A brand's own pages: before investing in branded social pages for a planned store, check whether the niche is already crowded by similar businesses. A planned homebrewing store was dropped because it was judged too competitive, so the competitor check should come before the brand is built.

Saturation is a blocker

2 min

Warnings

  • Competition is the biggest research criterion that can eliminate a product.
  • Avoid markets with too much competition, since that is one of the main reasons a niche fails. The goal is a middle ground with some competitors but not a saturated market.
  • Trending products are a signal to avoid rather than enter. Electric bikes passed the price and local-brand checks but were too competitive because they were a popular trending product.
  • Products that appear on public trend lists draw many store owners at once, so competition rises quickly. Choose a related product that is not on those lists instead.
  • A niche with too many paid advertisers for the same brands is harder to win through ads.
  • A niche with saturated competition is a poor choice for a first store.
  • Before launching, check whether the people who need the product are already served by a strongly established rival and what it would cost to win them. Products in fields dominated by entrenched brands require a clear, convincing advantage to succeed.
  • Heavily tested categories such as books have very high competition. Copying the same creatives as many sellers makes it hard to stand out. Differentiate through the message, the avatar or the angle.
  • Two or three established brands already scaling a product on Facebook: avoid it.
  • Visible high-ticket dropshipping stores signal crowded markets. Markets where high-ticket dropshipping stores are already visible usually have suppliers who approve dropshippers, but they also tend to be more competitive. Using visible dropshipping as the only filter means choosing the most crowded markets.
  • Products with many paid advertisers and the same brands are harder to win through ads.

Blockers without differentiation

  • Competition against an established brand can still work if you use a clearly different marketing angle. The risk is high when the angle is the same as theirs.
  • A product that a competitor is scaling may not work for you because that competitor's pixel already has data on their buyers.
  • A product that is saturated in one market can work in a different market or segment. Rethink the target audience and angle rather than dropping the product entirely.

Differentiation for crowded products

5 min

Choose the lever

When a product is old or already strongly competed, differentiate through one of three factors: a different market or demographic, a different platform, or a different marketing angle or audience. Do not switch the product itself.

Market and audience

  • A product that is saturated in one market can work in a different market or segment, such as a common product sold to a new niche. Rethink the target audience and angle rather than dropping the product entirely.
  • Product selection filters such as high order counts, low negative reviews and Amazon competition can be relaxed when the segment is different. A few negative reviews are acceptable if the rest is strong.
  • A saturated product can still work when you find a buyer group with a related concern that competitors ignore. Example: a relaxation-focused head massager could also speak to men worried about hair loss. A claimed link, such as scalp tension and hair loss, should be checked against studies before it is used in ads.
  • A commodity product can stand out in a crowded category by narrowing to one specific audience first, then narrowing again by ingredients or features that matter to that audience. Example: targeting men only, then using natural ingredients and strong masculine scents.
  • A competitive product can be made to work by narrowing it to one specific buyer and their problem, or by serving it to a different segment, such as dog owners instead of cat owners. A product that is generic in one niche can perform well when repositioned into a niche with a specific problem.
  • Changing the audience means selling the same product to a different group of people, such as cats instead of dogs for a harness.
  • Changing the marketing angle means keeping the audience but changing the main benefit. For a head massager, one seller focuses on migraine relief, while another can focus on productivity or sleep quality.
  • Differentiating by marketing angle or audience: keep the same product but target a different use case or customer group. Well-known example: a dog shower spray sold for car washing from 2017 to 2020; a store reframed it as a pet-washing product, opened a new audience and reportedly did 3,000 to 10,000 dollars per day.
  • For health and wellness products, most sellers target gym-goers. Differentiate by targeting people who have no time for exercise, such as desk workers, since time-saving is a usual selling point.
  • Build on the audience. Where the product is the same, pick the people who are not served: a common product sold to a new niche wins if its angle fits that niche.

Platform

  • Differentiating by platform: a product that has peaked and collapsed on one platform can still be unique on another. Check the Facebook Ad Library for scaling products, and test platforms such as Pinterest or Snapchat for products that are saturated on Facebook.
  • Bodysuit trend on TikTok: many dropshippers ran the same product in March and April 2023, then demand collapsed and few sellers kept selling it on TikTok. A brand still scaled it on Facebook, showing that a platform switch can open a new opportunity.

Angle and creative

  • Competition can be reduced in a crowded category such as blenders by showing one visible feature the top sellers lack. Show that difference in the first seconds of the video so viewers see why this product is different.
  • A product is not saturated until its market and targeting are saturated.
  • Your own creative, angle and execution decide success even when competitors run the same product.
  • Differentiate through the message, the avatar or the angle when many sellers run the same creatives.

Product-level differentiators

  • Narrow within the niche: competition occurs at product level, and crowded niches still contain products with low competition. Products that few competitors bid on are the easiest way in.
  • Brands and variants: the opportunity is in selling the brands and variants that big-box chains do not stock.
  • Hundreds of suppliers with different products suitable for online sale.
  • Market or niche with many products and brands: a broad niche with thousands of products from hundreds of companies leaves room.
  • Entering a dominant-brand niche: find weaknesses competitors do not address and do those things better.
  • Ideas at the product level for a new entrant: look at the product gap, not the niche gap.

Unique mechanism

  • A unique mechanism is a real attribute of the product, such as a proprietary technology, a special material or a distinctive design feature, that competing products do not have. A product with nothing unique is a commodity and is much harder to market.
  • If you make the product yourself, you usually already know its difference from competitors because of your research, planning and production process. Resellers need to dig into documentation and competitor products to find it.
  • A product can have one, two or three unique mechanisms, and one is enough. The mechanism you lead with should enable the product to deliver the primary promise, at least in part, so the copy hangs together.

Generic products and brands

  • Generic-looking product names from suppliers are not a problem by themselves; what matters is that the niche passes the tests.
  • Differentiation can come from brand presence: a new brand entering with a clear angle can take share from crowded products.

Searching for new products

  • A product with high saturation can still be tested when you change the product type. Do not switch the product itself when the lever is market or angle.

Pre-launch decision points

1 min

When saturation is not a blocker

  • The product still sells when the market or targeting changes.
  • The product has a large audience even if a few competitors cover part of it.
  • Competitors show money is being made.
  • A new product in a large niche has room.
  • Competitors are small, with 1k to 2k likes.

When saturation is a blocker

  • Competitor ads are not scaling and no new angle, audience or platform is available.
  • Many established brands already scale the product.
  • A product has many competitors with heavy engagement.
  • The first-page store test fails and the keyword or niche cannot be narrowed.

Ecommerce research practice: rule summaries for product-level judgement

1 min
  • Competition is judged per product on Google Shopping and in ad libraries.
  • Per-product counts are set against a country benchmark and a first-page store test.
  • Marketplaces are excluded from the direct-competitor total and included in the first-page store test.
  • A brand that many retailers sell is skipped; brands with few retailers are chosen.
  • The product is kept only if the ad and Google Shopping tests show room.
  • Saturation alone is never the decision; creative, angle and audience decide.

Judging Saturation per Product, Not per Niche

1 min
  • Competition for a dropshipping business should be measured at the product level, not the niche level. What matters is whether the specific products you plan to sell can attract profitable traffic against existing sellers. A niche with many stores can still be workable if its individual products have little competition.
  • High-ticket products sell into a very large online market, so a store can capture only a small fraction of demand and still build a very profitable business. Do not reject a niche just because many stores exist in it.
  • A competition test is only useful when it runs across the whole master supplier list, because a decision needs data for every brand. Product-level counts for all suppliers together show whether the niche is viable, and these results are later used to decide whether to proceed with the niche.
  • Judging competition from the total number of stores in a niche, or from sponsored ads, is misleading for this method. Checking only one product or skipping the store-count click gives an unreliable average. Both lead to a wrong go or no-go decision.

Saturation Signals from Ad Libraries

1 min
  • Saturation is judged by how many competitors run ads for the product.
  • A product that is saturated but has a weak offer can still be kept if you change the offer slightly before giving up.
  • Products from a shared research list that many people have already picked up can become saturated quickly. Re-check their active ads in Meta Ad Library right before testing, and skip the product if the ad count has grown fast or the ads have stopped.
  • Avoid products where thousands of advertisers already run the same top creative. Pick a product that few advertisers run, but that has proof of demand and a rising trend.
  • A product is worth a closer look when it is new (not seen in the last six months) and another seller has run ads for it in the last seven days with good engagement. Ads running this way show that the product sells even with competition.
  • Products that have not been seen for about six months are considered fresh enough to test.

Store Count on Google Shopping

3 min

Setting

  • In Google Shopping, the free product listings sit below the sponsored carousel at the top of the Shopping results. A single seller shows as [Store name] seller, and several sellers show as [Store name] and more. Clicking "and more" opens a right-hand sidebar that lists every store selling that product.
  • Only the first page of Google Shopping results is used for the competition test. Sponsored ads are excluded from the count. Column G is the only column to fill in at this stage; the other columns are completed later in the research process.

Procedure

  1. Open the master supplier list or supplier tracking sheet and copy one supplier name.
  2. Search for that supplier name on google.com and add the product category, for example "supplier name lighting".
  3. Click the Shopping tab in the search results.
  4. Skip the sponsored carousel at the top, since it is not used for this test.
  5. Scroll down below the sponsored carousel to the free Google product listings.
  6. For each product, read the seller indicator: a single store appears as [Store name] seller, and several stores appear as [Store name] and more.
  7. For [Store name] and more, click "and more" to open the sidebar on the right with the full store list, then count the stores.
  8. Count each store name only once per product, even if it appears more than once.
  9. Check the products in the first row and continue through the results on the first page of Google Shopping, then calculate the average number of stores per product.
  10. Enter that average in column G of the supplier tracking sheet, under the notes section.
  11. Repeat the whole process for every supplier on the list.

Counting Rules

  • After clicking "and more" on a Google Shopping product, the right-hand sidebar lists the first group of sellers and may include a "more stores" option. Scroll the sidebar and click "more stores" until the list ends, then count every distinct store before calculating the product's number.
  • The "and more" label can appear even when only one store is in the sidebar, so the label alone does not give the store count. Always open the sidebar and count the stores listed there.
  • If a store name appears more than once in the list for a product, count it only once, because repeated listings from the same retailer represent one competitor. This matters for large retailers that show up in several places on the same results page.

Worked Examples

  • Lighting supplier, example A: the first product had 15 stores listed, the second had 4, and the third had 1, so the average after the first row was about 5 stores. Continuing down the results, the next rows showed 1, 3, 3 and 1 stores, and the average settled at about 3 stores, which was entered in column G. The product-level result was very low competition for that supplier.
  • Lighting supplier, example B: the first product listed 7 stores, the second listed 7, and the third listed 2, so the running average fell to about 5. Further products showed 4, 3, 3, 2 and 1 stores, and one product had 10 stores. The final average was rounded to 5 stores and entered in column G.

Reading the Result

  • Competition benchmark: if the average across a brand's products is about 10 different stores or fewer, that is a green light to keep the brand in the niche. If almost all brands on the list average 20 or more stores, the niche has too much competition for this method. The source gives no rule for brands whose average falls between these two values.

Competition in Mainstream Retail and Marketplaces

1 min
  • A product sold in mainstream retail such as Walmart or shopping malls, or easy to find on many online sellers, is usually saturated for dropshipping. Saturation makes it hard to win buyers on price and content alike.

Crowded Niches and Themed Markets

1 min
  • Trendy themed niches attract many competing stores selling the same products. A store in such a niche has to look polished and distinct, because a basic site that looks newly created loses against established ones.
  • Before picking a product, check for products in the same niche that many competitors have sold, and avoid those with saturated offers. Products whose audience is already heavily targeted by large players give less room for a new store.
  • A market that looks uncharted often already has established sellers who simply use different terminology. Ask suppliers about direct-to-consumer services rather than dropshipping, and look for existing competition as a sign of demand before concluding a niche is empty.
  • Trending product lists do not show how saturated a product is on organic TikTok, so saturation is hard to judge from a list alone.

Differentiation for Crowded Products

1 min
  • Change the offer slightly before giving up on a saturated product with a weak offer.
  • Build a polished, distinct store in a themed niche, since a basic site that looks newly created loses against established competitors.
  • On organic TikTok, a crowded product is not a reason to skip it when most competitors post inconsistently. Steady posting and better videos can outperform them.
  • Use supplier terminology such as direct-to-consumer services to find sellers who already serve the market under different names.

When Saturation Is a Blocker

1 min
  • A product sold in mainstream retail or easy to find on many online sellers is usually saturated for dropshipping.
  • A product whose top creative is run by thousands of advertisers should be avoided.
  • A product from a shared research list whose ad count has grown fast, or whose ads have stopped, should be skipped at the re-check before testing.
  • A saturated offer aimed at an audience already heavily targeted by large players leaves little room for a new store.
  • If almost all brands on the supplier list average 20 or more stores on Google Shopping, the niche has too much competition for this method.

When Saturation Is Not a Blocker

1 min
  • Competition is not a reason to drop a product if an ad for it was launched within the last 7 days and is getting good engagement, because the product is working for someone else.
  • A crowded product is not a reason to skip it when most competitors post inconsistently on organic TikTok and can be outperformed with steady posting and better videos.
  • A store can capture only a small fraction of a large high-ticket market and still be very profitable, so a crowded niche is not an automatic rejection.
  • A niche with many stores can still be workable if its individual products have little competition.
  • Existing competition is a sign of demand, so a market with established sellers is not automatically empty or unworkable.
  • An average of about 10 stores or fewer per brand is a green light to keep the brand in the niche.

More rules worth knowing

1 min
  • Analyze competition by counting reviews on competing products, not by counting total sellers. Products with 1,000+ reviews are practically untouchable due to accumulated momentum and ranking advantage. Target products with fewer than 300 reviews where you can realistically compete.
