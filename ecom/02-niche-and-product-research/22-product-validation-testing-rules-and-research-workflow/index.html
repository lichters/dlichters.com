Playbook › 2. Niche and product research

Product validation, testing rules and research workflow

Section 2.22 · 22 of 24 in this chapter · 40 min read

In short

  • Research at least 10 products, record all of them in your sheet, and check each one before you move on.
  • Keep 5 to 10 checked products ready at all times, because most products fail.
  • Do not pick products by gut feeling. Pick only products that other sellers are running ads for right now.
  • Expect many failures. Plan for about 10 to 20 tests before you find a winner.
  • Spend small amounts first. Scale a product only after sales keep coming in.
More key points
  • Set a stop point before each test. For ads, stop at about $300 of lost ad money.
  • Check the click rate (CTR) first. Good CTR means keep the ad. Bad CTR means try new ads, then move to the next product.
  • Stop a product that gets clicks but no sales after about $300 is spent across offers, ads and audiences.
  • Grow spending (scale) only after sales prove the product sells again and again.
  • Do your own product research first, before you give it to anyone else.

Research Volume and Shortlist Size

2 min
  • Research at least 10 products using the course framework (the course's step-by-step checklist). Enter all of them into the Product Research Sheet (your product spreadsheet). Check each one before you move on. Moving on is blocked until all three are done: 10 researched, 10 entered, and 10 checked.
  • Build a research list of at least 10 product ideas, not just one.
  • Research at least 15 product ideas before you submit anything for approval. A shortlist (a short list of your best picks) of 10 to 15 ideas is the minimum before you narrow down.
  • Aim for 10 to 12 candidate products (possible products to sell) per research session. Each one needs its active ad count (how many ads are running now), the countries it targets, and its store data.
  • One research session can find about 15 to 20 products. The research itself should take about three to five days.
  • Use one or two research tools that together give at least 10 to 15 candidate products. If a tool lacks the filters (settings that narrow results) you need, switch to a free method. Examples: the burner account method (using a spare, throwaway account to see ads) or a Chrome extension method for Facebook (a small add-on for the Chrome web browser).
  • Research 10 to 15 potential products across different niches (groups of related products or buyers). Do not commit to one niche at the start. For each product, record its margin (profit left after costs), whether it is new, whether it solves a problem, the value it gives the customer, and how many marketing angles (different reasons to buy) it offers.
  • Build a sheet of 10 to 20 candidate products before you commit any budget.
  • Build a Facebook product research list of at least 10, ideally 10 to 15, products so you can rank them later. Add a column for the Facebook page creation date (when the seller's page was made) next to the ad library link (Facebook's public list of all running ads).
  • Keep 5 to 10 solid, checked products ready at all times. Products fail often, so the next one must already be prepared. Product research takes time, so start early.
  • Before competitor analysis (studying rival sellers), build a shortlist of roughly 10 to 15 product candidates that already meet basic criteria. For example: the product is unique and has not been seen before in the market.

Shortlists and Scoring

2 min
  • Refresh the product research list regularly, for example every two weeks. Active ad counts and trends change quickly. A product that was strong last month may lose its active ads and need to be dropped.
  • If a rival's store page or ad link does not load, do not add the product to the test list yet. Put it on a watch list (a list to check later). Recheck it once the page works.
  • Score each viral product candidate (one that is spreading fast) from 1 to 10 on separate criteria, such as comment demand, product appeal and store quality. Do not rely on one overall impression. A candidate with buyer comments scored 10 for demand can still be worth a test, even if the product itself scores around 7. A candidate scoring 4 or 5 overall should be dropped.
  • Rate each competition criterion (how hard it is to beat rivals) on a number scale in the Facebook competitor scoring sheet. Mark 7 or higher as a red flag (a warning sign). Then add the ratings into a total score to rank products. This is a custom sheet made for this method, not a standard tool.
  • Rank each product by how many validation criteria (checks it must pass) it meets. Products that meet more criteria rank higher. Drop products that fail critical checks, or move them to the back of the line.
  • Rank final product candidates by how many marketing angles they have, how well they fit your criteria, their quality, and how little competition they face. The top-ranked product is the best candidate to launch.
  • Use the Weighting column (a score that ranks products against each other) after the four phases (see the Four-Phase section) are complete. Products with better engagement (likes, comments, shares), lower competition, and a workable break-even ROAS (see below) should get higher weights. ROAS means return on ad spend: money earned back for each dollar spent on ads. Break-even ROAS is the ROAS where ads cover their costs and you neither make nor lose money.
  • Score the product list in a spreadsheet with a checkbox (a tick box) for each criterion. Products that miss criteria drop to the bottom with a rank of 1 out of 5. Products that meet them rise to a higher rank.
  • Use a 1 to 10 opportunity scale when comparing niches. Low scores (around 1 to 5) rarely become profitable, no matter how much effort you put in. Pursue niches scoring around 8 to 10.
  • Rank new brands and products using the same analytics (measurement data) you use for your current products. Search volume (how often people search for a term) is generally a good metric for this.
  • Use one consistent product name or search term for all research steps. Switching terms makes the data impossible to compare. Related terms can be used later in ads.
  • Check the research tools for product ranking and sales data using the same method every time, so results stay comparable. Reliable niche decisions depend on consistent measurement, not on a single data point. **

Screening Criteria and Product Filters

3 min
  • Define validation criteria (the tests a product must pass) before choosing a product. The main beginner mistake is spending money on untested products until they give up. Use the results of each tested product to decide the next one.
  • Do not select products by personal intuition (gut feeling) or by random browsing on AliExpress (an online marketplace where products come from suppliers). Beginners cannot afford such guesses. Back each research step with data showing that the product is selling for others right now.
  • Only promote products you have tested yourself and that actually do what they claim. Genuine belief in the product makes it easier to create convincing videos and sell it to customers.
  • Before choosing products, check the price point (the selling price), the number of available suppliers, and the competition on the first page of Google Shopping results (Google's product search results). **
  • Check the product, its type, and its seasonality (how sales rise and fall across seasons) with this sequence:
    1. Find candidate products in a product research tool (Peak was used).
    2. Check the product is legitimate (real, not a scam) and does what it claims, e.g. by reading the reviews on Amazon.
    3. Check the product type: entertainment products that attract parents, such as activity products for children's summer holidays, suit seasonal selling.
    4. Consider seasonality: pick products that fit the upcoming season, e.g. summer products such as water dispensers for offices and barbecues, or Apple Watch straps, which sell well in summer.
    5. Check platform fit: decide whether the product works on Facebook and TikTok.
    6. Ask family, friends or partners whether they would buy the product, instead of relying on personal taste.
    7. Test with a budget of about 200 dollars and see how it performs.
  • Product selection order: first check whether the product has a lot of competition and whether it can be sold profitably, e.g. at a 3x markup (selling for three times its cost) on a cheap item. Then check that real demand exists. A very small niche makes scaling (spending more on what works) difficult, compared with a large market like pets.
  • Before testing a candidate, define a customer avatar (a made-up profile of your ideal buyer). List several marketing angles for it. Estimate the total addressable market (TAM: the total number of buyers who could want the product). Products with a clear audience, several angles and a large market are worth testing.
  • Judge the market before entering. If the product is new and ads run by others are working, check the problem size, the number of use cases (ways people use it), the store (your online shop) and the margin (profit per sale). These checks decide whether to proceed.
  • Complete customer research, product research and primary text research (the main words in your ad) before testing. Then the product page and ad copy are tuned well. Testing without them makes it much harder to know why an ad works.
  • Check TikTok Shop traction (sales activity on TikTok's built-in store) before building a product page. Look at three things:
    • A strong creator conversion ratio (the share of creators whose videos lead to sales).
    • Steady revenue across weeks.
    • A concentration rate (how evenly sales spread across creators). Sales should spread over several creators, not one. Compare these numbers with the price and the commission rate (the cut paid to creators) to see whether the product is worth testing.
  • Keep a fixed set of criteria for every product. For example, a product criteria checklist of six to seven points that defines the kind of product you want.
  • Set clear criteria for the team. For example: upsell potential (a chance to sell an extra item after the first purchase) and a maximum supplier cost of 30 USD on AliExpress. Clear criteria let the assistant screen products before the owner picks one.
  • Products that sold well organically (without paid ads) on TikTok do not automatically work with paid ads. Once a proof of concept exists (a small test showing the idea can sell), test horizontally (try other products in the same niche). Find one that converts with ads, meaning it turns viewers into buyers.
  • Keep the full research order in mind. It runs: product criteria checklist, TikTok ad spy search (a tool that shows competitor ads), AliExpress supplier quality, competition and sales check, competitor store verification, and product and store quality check.

Four-Phase Research Process and Sheet

1 min
  • Thorough product research happens in four phases. Each phase removes weak candidates before the next one begins. Use this order. Each stage filters out products before the more time-consuming checks begin.
  • Work through product research in four phases, in this order:
    1. Phase 1, Initial research (criteria based): enter the product name, the ad link, the competitor store link and the AliExpress supplier link ("Ali").
    2. Phase 2, Competitor analysis: record the ad engagement (likes) and the competition level for the product.
    3. Phase 3, Market validation: record the market and mark whether the product should be tested ("TEST?").
    4. Phase 4, Decide: enter COGS (cost of goods sold: what the product costs you to buy), selling price, competitor price, break-even ROAS (BE ROAS), mentor notes, your own notes and the weighting score.
  • Work through the product testing sheet in phase order. First research the product and review competitor ads. Then fill in the competitor analysis (phase 2) with engagement (high or low) and competition (low, medium or high), plus your findings. Next, set the market size as small, medium or large (phase 3). Finally, record the yes or no decision on testing the product (phase 4).
  • A mentor review (a second person checking your choices) of a shared product list lets two people compare candidates and pick the test product together. Leave a comment for each product.

Demand Validation: Signals and Their Limits

2 min
  • Beginners often overthink validation (checking whether a product is worth the risk). They ask whether a product is guaranteed to win. There is no guarantee, so testing is required.
  • To validate a product before testing, look for active ads, recent creatives (the ad videos or images), and multiple advertisers (several sellers running ads). These signals are enough to justify a test.
  • Look only at products already advertised on the ad platform, not untested items.
  • Confirm product demand from several sources. Examples: supplier sales counts, the follower count and views of organic accounts (accounts that grow without paid ads), and ad activity. Do not base a decision on a single signal.
  • Validate a product with data, such as sales and engagement signals. Do not choose it by guessing.
  • Only scale a product after validation shows it sells consistently. One lucky sale is not validation.
  • Validate niche ideas with actual numbers and real market data gathered free online. When time is limited, choose the niche with more opportunity over the weaker one.
  • Do not judge a product by one viral video or one spike in sales. Check that revenue holds across the Last 30 Days window (the past 30 days of data). Also check that the product has a rising trend before you commit.
  • A product page with only about 10 reviews after a large viral spike is weak evidence of quality. Such reviews may be fake. Do not judge demand from review count alone. Combine it with real order checks.
  • A common guideline is a minimum of 300 orders before a product is considered proven. Treat this as a guideline, not an absolute rule. Some products with strong potential sell below this level.
  • Low AliExpress order counts are not a reason to drop a product if the ads show strong engagement, such as several thousand likes on one ad. Check the CJ listing (the same product on the CJ Dropshipping supplier platform) and test the product anyway.
  • Test product variants separately instead of assuming a whole category works. Example: one version of a door-lock style product sold while another version of the same product did not. Track each variant's sales on its own.
  • Research the product category before launching videos for it. Without category research, the first days of filming are slow, because the creator has to learn what to show and how.

Demand Validation: Saturation and Trend Checks

2 min
  • Before testing, check when the first ad for the product started running. Look for viral ads already run for the product. If there are many, the market may be saturated (too many sellers already selling it).
  • Search TikTok for old trending videos. If trends from several years ago still circulate, the trend has likely passed.
  • Check the product in ad-spy apps (tools that show competitor ads) for old ads.
  • Mark which candidates look saturated or heavily competed. Get an experienced outside opinion on saturation for the marked candidates. Then test the ones that remain.
  • If you are unsure whether a product is saturated, test it with a new TikTok account and no store. Building a store takes significant time, so skipping it removes much of the cost of a failed test.
  • Before investing in a product from scratch, validate demand with a market test on Facebook, TikTok and Instagram. A small group of friends or testers cannot show whether a product can reach millions of buyers. Test with an audience of one thousand to tens of thousands of people on these platforms. Do this before committing money to inventory (stock you hold) or custom product development (making your own version). A small group of friends or testers cannot show whether a product will work at scale.
  • Pushing a product that the market does not respond to leads to poor ad performance on social platforms. Founders should not rely on intuition alone. They must let the market react.
  • Test demand on Facebook and TikTok first, not Google. Product testing is about spending time and money efficiently.
  • Check the trend with Google Trends (a free tool showing search interest over time) and with news. Collect marketing angles from Amazon reviews.
  • For a product too new to check with Amazon reviews, the goal is proof of concept and demand, not quality certainty. Start with a small test spend. If the first two or three sales come in, stop or scale based on results. The cost of failure stays small.
  • Validate a product with a small number of sales on your store before spending a large budget. The goal is proof of concept (a small test showing the idea can work), not a large test.
  • Before buying a large quantity of a custom-brand product, get approval of the concept by testing it. Scaling before the concept is validated increases chargeback risk (customers reversing their payments) and cash-flow risk (running short of money to pay bills).
  • Dummy test order refunds: competitors rarely accept refund requests for test orders. A dummy order is a fake order placed to check a rival store. A cancellation request is often refused, with a claim that the order already shipped. Plan for losing the order cost.
  • Do not rush into buying a product or spending on it all at once after a single positive check. Test in small steps and compare multiple reference points. A store's age and account history can change how reliable dummy orders are.
  • Check the supplier or CJ shipping cost and the sourcing cost (what it costs to get the product) before testing.

Demand Validation: Conversion Depends on the Product

1 min
  • Conversion (the share of viewers who buy) from views to sales depends heavily on the product. A good, unique product that solves a clear problem sold 5 units from under 50,000 views. Some other products got one sale or none from a million views.
  • When a video or account does not convert, the cause could be the audience, the product, the videos, or a mix of all three. Find out which one is responsible before changing everything.
  • A result of 500 to 600 views per post over three weeks, at two to four posts per day, across several accounts and devices, suggested the product or the video angle was the cause. The account itself was not.
  • Bottom-of-funnel ads (ads aimed at buyers ready to buy) work for market-aware products. These are universally understood items (phone cases, water bottles), or products already validated and run by competitors. For niche products that need explanation (specialized supplements), first warm up audiences with video ads. Warm audiences are people who already know you. Revisit bottom-of-funnel ads once you have repeat customers or brand awareness.
  • Attention rate (how many people keep watching) and click-through rate (the share of people who click) can be lower for a relevant audience that buys. So a low soft metric (an indirect number, not sales) can still come with an ad that makes sales, and that ad can be profitable. Retargeting (showing ads to people who already visited) the people who clicked can build a useful audience later.

Demand Validation: Research Cadence and Sources

1 min
  • Start product research while still going through the training, not after it. Also check TikTok Shop, Kalodata (a TikTok sales data tool) and competitor stores for demand signals.
  • The research approach: launch small tests, look for a little demand and some sales, and only then go deeper. Deeper research uses sources such as Reddit, Amazon and emotion valence exports (data files with emotion scores). These are fed into an AI tool like Claude for analysis.
  • The Market Research habit is a daily 30-minute block. Its purpose is to understand the target audience better through a structured research process. Doubling or more of that time is encouraged when possible.

Testing Approaches: Choosing an Approach

1 min
  • Product testing falls into three approaches:
    • Spam testing: many products tested quickly with little thought.
    • Continuous testing: a steady flow of products checked against fixed rules.
    • Branded testing: building a brand around one product, with custom content and quality control.
    • Spam testing is not recommended. It depends on luck and needs a large budget to test 50 to 60 products.
  • Spam testing means testing roughly 3 to 5 products per day, or 10 to 15 per week. The hit rate (the share of tests that win) is about 2 to 3 winners out of 10 tested products.
  • Continuous testing with rule sets means testing about 1 to 2 products per week against defined criteria. You are not tied to a single product or niche.
  • The recommended approach combines rule-based continuous testing with a light branded approach. Add brand elements without investing heavily upfront.
  • Product tests should be good enough, not perfect. They should not consume much time or money. A test can fail, and the time spent should still produce learning.
  • Early on, test products aggressively, since you cannot yet tell which products are good. Once you have a viral win (a video that spreads fast) and know how to create virality, shift to strict product selection.
  • Early tests on weak products help build the skill of going viral. Before investing heavily in a product, confirm it can produce sales.
  • Product research has no shortcut. A product's potential is only known after spending money on it. Learn audience research as the core skill, rather than relying on hiring freelancers.
  • No single product research method is perfect. Adapt a method that works for you rather than following one rigidly. Keep a method only when it has produced winners for you.

  • Creative tests (tests of the ad itself, a video or image) should use at least 12 videos taken from the last 30 days. The range for a test is 12 to 24 videos.

Stop Rules

  • If an ad has a good CTR but no sales after spending a lot (about $300 across several offers, creatives and audiences), move on to the next product, since the data is no longer good. Continue only when the data is good.
  • When a store has a good CTR but poor sales, test a different offer (what you sell and on what terms, such as price or bonus) before moving to another product. A good CTR means the ad is doing its job, and the offer is the likely problem.
  • A loss of around $300 with sales still coming in can justify stopping. A good CTR with few offers tested means more offers should be tested first.
  • If the first two or three sales come in, stop or scale (grow spending and sales) based on the results.
  • If a product makes some sales but cannot be scaled, stop it rather than forcing it. Move the budget to the next product test. Scaling potential matters as much as early sales when choosing which product to keep.
  • Use a rule set for every product: if the CTR is good, continue; if it is bad, try new creatives; if it is still bad after new creatives, move to the next product.
  • Switch immediately to the next product when the CTR is bad.
  • Products that miss criteria (the standards a product must meet) are dropped, not tested further.

Procedures With Stop Rules

  • Saturation (the market is full of the same product) and trend (a product many people talk about right now) procedure for a product:
    1. Check when the first ad for the product started running.
    2. Look for viral ads already run for the product. If there are many, the market may be saturated.
    3. Search TikTok for old trending videos. If trends from several years ago are still circulating, the trend has likely passed.
    4. Check the product in ad-spy apps (tools that show other sellers' ads) for old ads.
    5. Test it with ads and watch the CTR: if the CTR is good, continue; if it is bad, try new creatives; if it is still bad after new creatives, move to the next product.
  • Women's product test:
    1. Pick a niche from Jungle Scout (a product research tool) or similar tools.
    2. Search women's (or target-audience, the group of people a product is aimed at) products on AliExpress (an online marketplace of suppliers).
    3. Sort the results by likes or orders.
    4. Choose the designs you like most.
    5. Run carousel ads (ads with several images to swipe through) for them.
    6. Check the CTR: if it is good, continue with the product; if it is bad, switch immediately to the next product.

Sales Proof

  • A common product research guideline is a minimum of 300 orders (purchases) before a product is considered proven. Treat this as a guideline, not an absolute rule, since some products with strong potential sell below this level.
  • The 300-order guideline is for judging a product as proven. Early stages need only a few sales to justify continuing, as set out in the stop rules above.

Diagnosing a Test: What to Change

2 min

Diagnosing means finding the cause of a result.

  • Check a strong CTR before changing anything else. If a store has a good CTR and poor sales, test a different offer before moving on, because the ad is doing its job.
  • Diagnose the audience (the people the ad is shown to), product, videos or a mix before changing everything.
  • Test many products rather than a few, but change one variable at a time (one thing) when diagnosing a test, so you know what caused the result.
  • Keep one fixed core audience, such as women over 40, and test every product against it. This keeps the ad data comparable from product to product. Consistent audience data makes it faster to tell which products work.
  • Test with ripped creatives (fast, simple ads, not polished). The test measures the product and the offer, not the quality of the creative. Creative quality is improved only later.
  • A creative test uses at least 12 videos from the last 30 days, with a range of 12 to 24 videos.
  • For a collection of many similar products, test sending ads to the collection page (one page listing a group of products) or to a landing page (a page built for one offer) made from it, not only to individual product pages. A collection landing page can add a hero section (a large top banner), testimonials (customer reviews) and a product list.
  • Test several designs or products, and send traffic (visitors) both to collection pages and to individual hero products (the main featured products).
  • Give a product new ad data (the numbers collected from its ads) before changing it. A product that does not perform after three to four days is replaced with a new product page.

Google Shopping Diagnosis

Google Shopping is Google's product search results, with pictures and prices.

  • Diagnosing high impressions (how often your listing is shown) with low clicks on Google Shopping: **
    1. Check which search terms and keywords (words people type) trigger your ads, in Google Ads.
    2. If the keywords are very broad (they match many unrelated searches), accept a low click-through rate and add negative keywords (words that block your ad from showing) to cut irrelevant searches.
    3. Compare your price, promotions (such as free shipping), product images and ad position (where your ad appears) with the competitors showing for the same keywords.
    4. Search the keyword yourself as a customer and fix what makes your listing less clickable.
  • Diagnosing a 50% impression rate with only a few clicks per day: **
    1. Open Google Keyword Planner (a free Google tool showing how often words are searched) and check the monthly search volume (how many people search the keyword each month) of your keyword.
    2. If search volume is low, people are not searching for the product, so reconsider the keyword or product.
    3. If search volume is high, search the keyword on Google as a customer and compare your listing with the others.
    4. Check whether your price, your image or the listing itself makes people skip your product.
  • Google tests are for established products (products already proven to sell). New product tests run on Facebook and TikTok.

Scaling, Stock and Commitment

3 min

Stock means products you have on hand to sell. Commitment means spending money on a large order.

  • Track clicks and early traction (early signs of demand) on a product to decide whether to scale it. Check profitability (making money) alongside demand. A trending product (popular right now) is only useful if the margin (money left after costs) still works at higher volume (more units sold).
  • A trending product list is used this way:
    1. Research which products are trending in the niche.
    2. List products that are not your own, to test whether they get clicks.
    3. If the products get clicks and traction, adjust the pricing.
    4. Check that the product can be profitable at scale. For example, lower cost through bulk shipping (sending large batches) or a faster shipping method.
    5. Go back to the manufacturers (the factories that make the products), replicate the winning product, and expand into its category.
  • Once sales or sign-ups are coming in, you have proof the product sells. This justifies the supplier order.
  • For a new product, run ads and collect sales or pre-order sign-ups (people who register or pay before the product is in stock).
  • If the product does not ship on time, email the buyers, explain the situation, and either refund them or give them the expected delivery window (the time they should expect to wait) if they are willing to wait.
  • Stock procedure for confirmed winners (products proven to sell):
    1. Test several designs or products and send traffic both to collection pages and to individual hero products.
    2. Check at least two weeks of sales data to find the top sellers and the best-selling variant (one version, such as a size or color) of each.
    3. Confirm that the best seller keeps selling consistently for about three weeks.
    4. Order stock for about one month of sales for each confirmed top product.
    5. During the stock order, keep ad parameters (the ad settings) and performance unchanged, and watch sales for the next month.
    6. If performance stays consistent or improves, scale the ad budget and order stock in proportion to the increase.
  • Scaling potential matters as much as early sales when choosing which product to keep.
  • Only scale after validation (proof) shows the product sells consistently.
  • Spot-check your top 10 to 20 selling products regularly. For each product, check the Google Ads results, the Bing Ads results, and the top organic search results (free results, not ads). **
  • Optimize the top products (improve their pages and ads) rather than the whole catalog (the full product list). A supplier catalog with about 1,000 SKUs (product codes, one per product) is updated first on the top 20%. The 80-20 rule (the idea that about 20% of items bring most results) helps identify them. **
  • In a catalog of about 2,000 products, typically 200 to 300 products are the best sellers. So use a 200-product group as the starting point for paid campaigns (paid ad runs). **

Catalog Optimization Procedure

  • Upload the supplier's full product catalog (e.g., 1,000 SKUs) with only SEO modifications (changes for search engine optimization, making pages easier to find on Google): product name and meta descriptions (the short text shown under a page's title in Google). Do not rewrite product descriptions. **
  • Monitor Google Analytics (a free tool that tracks website visits) and Google Ads data for 3+ months to identify which products gain traction.
  • Apply the 80-20 rule: identify the top 20% of products (200 from 1,000) generating impressions and clicks.
  • Further optimize only those top 200 by improving descriptions, enhancing pages, and increasing ad spend. Leave the others as they are.
  • Continue until performance plateaus (stops improving), then repeat for the next product tier (group).

Relaunching and Revisiting Products

2 min
  • Do not abandon a niche that already shows sales and buyer trust, such as about 11,000 USD in two months. First optimize (improve) the existing ads and products within it. **
  • Expect the plan and the niche to change as sales data comes in. Adjust rather than hold to the first idea. Revisit projected sales (sales forecasts) regularly to see which direction works. **
  • If a niche was chosen using the blueprint criteria (the standard checklist for choosing a niche) and is not profitable, do not switch niches right away. Troubleshoot (find and fix problems in) the supplier, product pages, traffic and conversion elements (parts of a page that turn visitors into buyers) first. A new niche will often show the same problems. **
  • After passing the brand and product count test, continue with the evergreen demand test (demand that stays steady all year) and price point tests (checks of whether customers accept a price level). A niche that passes the count test still needs demand over time and workable price points. **
  • Systematic process for finding more products after a winner:
    1. After every test, review each funnel stage (each step from ad to purchase): the ad, the store and the product. Find the weak point.
    2. Use a strong click-through rate as proof that an ad angle (the main idea or hook of the ad) works. Replicate that angle with other products.
    3. Research products in the same niche continuously.
    4. Study competitors: what they sell and which marketing angles they use.
    5. Look for angles others are not using, for example in Amazon reviews.
    6. Revive older products with new marketing angles.
    7. Use customer reviews and feedback to improve the product, working with agents and manufacturers if needed. This improvement took about one to two months.
  • Revisit a product once a new marketing angle or a new product page is ready. A product that failed a test can be retried with a new page, as set out in the three-to-four-day rule.
  • What you learn from each failed product test should carry into the next test. Product page, copy and creatives improve from test to test instead of starting from zero each time. Product selection should become stricter as the operator (the person running the store) gains experience.
  • If a product that does not perform is revived, keep the same core audience so the data stays comparable.

Delegating Product Research

2 min

Delegating means handing a task to someone else.

  • Do product research yourself across several products, niches and countries before handing it to a team. Without knowing which niche works for you, a team returns a huge generic product list that is hard to use.
  • Delegating product research (short version):
    1. Give the team member access to your product research software.
    2. Show them exactly which product criteria and customer problems you are looking for.
    3. Have them send you a list of products they believe will work at the end of each week.
    4. Scan the list, choose the products you like, and start testing them.
  • Delegating product research (full version):
    1. Develop your own product research skill first. Test multiple products, niches and target countries.
    2. Define the target audience and the problems you are looking for, based on what has worked in your past stores.
    3. Teach the team member your exact research methods and give them access to your research tools, for example Peeksta (a product research tool).
    4. Show them exactly which problems and needs to look for.
    5. Ask for a list of products at the end of each week.
    6. Scan the list, choose the products you like and start testing them.
  • Delegating without a defined target audience leads to a huge, general list of products that may or may not work. Team members do not know what to look for unless the owner has already identified the best niche and audience.
  • Give product research criteria to the team, for example upsell potential (the chance to sell an extra or upgraded item) and a maximum supplier cost of 30 USD on AliExpress. Clear criteria let the assistant screen products before the owner picks one.
  • A team member with a personal interest in a niche suggested it for a second store. That store then converted better (more visitors became buyers) than the first store. Letting people who know a category choose products can beat the owner's own guesses. **
  • Every research step can be split into smaller tasks. Each task with clear instructions can be taught to a person or an AI agent (a computer helper that does tasks). The quality of the result depends on how well the instructions are written and which best practices (proven good methods) are applied. **

End-to-End Research Process

6 min

A phase is one stage of a plan.

Phase Timeline

  • Phase 2, Product Research and Validation (Days 15 to 30): identify a proven product worth building a real brand around, not just testing for quick sales.
  • Research and tests begin early, run in parallel with training (at the same time as learning), and keep running after the first winner.

Niche Stage

  • Niche selection and market research, in order: (Source A; the two sources give the same five steps, with slightly different wording for step 2.)
    1. Review the niche selection material.
    2. Brainstorm at least 50 niche ideas and list them in a niche worksheet. Add 50 or more niche ideas to the niche worksheet.
    3. Begin the market research tests. Start the research tests from the market research material.
    4. Complete all market research tests.
    5. Choose the niche for your first store.
  • You do not need to research all 50 niches. If 2 to 3 feel strong and meet criteria, research those. Most lists contain multiple winners, and thorough research is only needed on the top candidates.
  • Research every niche only until a winner is found. It is not necessary to search suppliers for every niche on a list of 50. **
  • Niche checks procedure:
    1. Confirm the niche has at least 20 brands that sell the products through online stores.
    2. Confirm there are at least 100 different products available across those brands.
    3. Check that demand and interest in the niche exist.
    4. Run the seasonality test (checking whether sales rise and fall with the seasons) and avoid niches that collapse for part of the year.
    5. Search Google Shopping and review the first page of results to see the average selling price (the usual price).

Product Stage

  • Product research procedure, in this order: **
    1. Look only at products already advertised on the ad platform, not untested items.
    2. Check proof of concept (proof the idea works) on competitor ads: likes, comments and especially shares.
    3. Find early winners: ads launched in the last 1 to 2 weeks, with about one month as the widest window.
    4. Check the Facebook page creation date of the competitor.
    5. Confirm scaling: count active ads and new ads per day on the competitor's page.
    6. Verify sales with at least two tools.
    7. Check product criteria: at least 3x markup (selling price at least three times the cost), longevity (lasting demand), quality rating, and a problem-solving or wow factor (it solves a problem or amazes people).
  • Product research process:
    1. Use spy tools (tools showing other sellers' ads) to find products.
    2. Compare 10 to 15 candidate products (possible choices).
    3. Check the pros and cons of each niche and product.
    4. Look at the value the product gives, including whether it solves a problem.
    5. Find marketing angles competitors are missing.
    6. Pick the products that meet the most criteria, with a clear competitor gap (a space competitors leave open) and room for better creatives and websites.
  • Product selection approach with the Peak tool: see the seven-step procedure in the screening section above.
  • Product research routine for a batch of 8 to 10 products:
    1. In AfterLib (a product research tool), find trending products and sort them by percentage change in ad count, viewing up to 80 ads per page.
    2. Open each product's store and note its price, shipping offer and target countries.
    3. Copy the store link into the research sheet.
    4. Open the Facebook Ad Library (Facebook's public list of ads), and check the number of active ads, the ad copies (the ad text) and the age of the Facebook page.
    5. Remove products whose ads are inactive, because nobody is selling them right now.
    6. Check the supplier or CJ shipping cost (CJ is a supplier and shipping service) and the sourcing cost (the price to get the product) before testing.
    7. Keep the products that pass and test them one at a time.
  • Product research order:
    1. Go through a product criteria checklist of six to seven points to define the kind of product you want.
    2. Search TikTok ads in a spy tool (PeakStar or PP Ads) with the filters (sorting settings) for that tool.
    3. Check supplier quality on AliExpress by reading ratings and reviews of multiple listings.
    4. Check the competition and whether a product is already heavily sold.
    5. Verify sales of competing stores with a store-tracking tool (a tool that estimates a store's sales).
    6. Check product quality, the size of the problem it solves, the number of use cases (ways people use it), how the store looks, and whether the margin is sufficient.
  • Product selection approach: see the seven-step procedure in the screening section above.
  • Competitor analysis: check how many competitors run the same or a related product, and how strong they are.
  • Product quality check: review ratings on AliExpress and Amazon and, if possible, order a sample (a single test copy).
  • Product spy research: find products that are currently selling, using filtered spy tools.
  • Five success factors for profitable product research, in order:
    1. Use a solid product criteria list of validation points (checks) to judge potential winners. Adapt it to your own process, since no single process fits everyone.
    2. Learn to be a differentiator (stand out): find how you can stand out from competitors.
    3. Understand why a customer buys from you and not from a competitor, Amazon, or another store. Do this by critically evaluating your own landing page and branding (your name, look and message), ideally with outside feedback asking for negative points as well.
    4. Understand your exact audience by stepping into their shoes.
    5. Combine points 2, 3 and 4 to turn a regular product into a stronger potential winner.
  • Women's products and other audience-led categories, tested through the Jungle Scout route: see the women's product test procedure in the stop rules above.
  • Fashion dropshipping research workflow (dropshipping means selling products you do not stock, while the supplier ships them to the buyer):
    1. Create a free AdSpy account (a spy tool for ads). If the free page limit (the free view allowance) is used up, create a new account with a different email address.
    2. Set the gender filter to female, the age filter to 40 to 65+, and the country filter to United States.
    3. Type a product keyword into the ad text filter, for example shoe, sneakers, boot or slippers.
    4. Scroll through the results and note ads with many likes, comments and shares, and check the ad creation date.
    5. Open the AliExpress app on your phone, use the camera search (search by photo) on a photo of the product, and open the matching listing.
    6. If the AliExpress order count is low, check whether the seller fulfills (ships) through CJ and look at the CJ listing.
    7. Watch how the ad uses copy, video type and images, and use that as inspiration for your own product page.
    8. Test the product in a market where it is not yet saturated, such as Australia, New Zealand, Germany, Switzerland or Austria.
  • Use the 4-stage sequence in order, because each stage filters out products before the more time-consuming checks begin.

Daily and Weekly Cadence

A cadence is a rhythm, a regular schedule.

  • Keep product research running in parallel with testing (at the same time), and check the list every week.
  • Test the sales hypothesis (your guess about sales) against the rule set each week, rather than on a fixed guess.

Ads, Pages and Variant Tests

  • Test a few products in one segment (one group) rather than all products at once, so each product gets enough attention.
  • For a product test, use the full research order, then ad testing, then store and offer checks.
