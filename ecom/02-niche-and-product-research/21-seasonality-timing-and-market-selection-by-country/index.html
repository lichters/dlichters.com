Seasonality, timing and market selection by country

Section 2.21 · 21 of 24 in this chapter · 27 min read

In short

  • Pick a product that fits the season in the country you sell to.
  • Launch well before the season or holiday. For Christmas, aim for late October.
  • If a holiday is only 2 to 3 weeks away, it is usually too late. Pick the next one.
  • Choose the country first, then judge the product.
  • Test where the product is not yet crowded, because competition and ad costs are lower there.
More key points
  • Use a VPN (a tool that makes your internet look like it comes from another country) set to the target country. Then search results show what local buyers see.
  • Check demand, competition and shipping before you spend money on ads or stock.
  • Test a proven product in a new language or country before you build a full store.
  • Avoid products with waits of about 10 weeks or more, and products sold out for months.

Research steps for a new country

2 min
  1. Set a VPN (a tool that makes your internet look like it comes from another country) to the target country. Then search the supplier name or product on Google Shopping (Google's product search) to see local results.
  2. Use the Source A research tool (a tool that shows how often people search for a product) with the target country selected. Check whether people search for the product there.
  3. Search results depend on location. When you research a market in a country where you do not live, use a VPN and set the location to the country you plan to sell in. Otherwise, results may come from your home country or other countries.
  4. Use a VPN set to the target country so Google returns local results. Without it, search results from another country distort findings about competitors (sellers who sell the same kind of thing) and niches (small groups of buyers with one shared interest).
  5. Market size check using the Facebook Ad Library (Meta's public list of all ads running on Facebook and Instagram):
    1. Search for the outlook for growth or market valuation (the estimated total money value of the market) of the industry (for example the pet industry).
    2. Check the ad library for each target country (for example Germany) to see how many sellers run ads there.
    3. Countries with lower saturation (how crowded a market is with sellers) are better test markets (countries where you try a product on a small budget first).
  6. Facebook Ad Library country check:
    1. Search a product keyword in the Facebook Ad Library. Use the country filter (a setting that shows one country only) to limit results to one market, such as the United States.
    2. Compare it with a test market such as Germany. Low scaling (spending more on ads that already sell) in the test market suggests room for a new seller.
  7. Check whether a product is popular in each target country before testing. A product that sells well in one country may not sell in another. For example, air fryers are less common in Switzerland than in the US.
  8. Compare the new country's shipping systems (the roads, carriers and warehouses that move parcels) and supplier responsiveness (how fast suppliers answer) with a market you already know. Check how quickly suppliers reply and how reliably parcels arrive before you commit money to ads or inventory (the goods you hold in stock).

Language, translation and language arbitrage

3 min
  • Test products in English-speaking markets before translating the store into other languages. Do this even if competitors are not marketing there. Translation needs two things: professional translation services, and a customer service virtual assistant (a remote helper who answers customers) who speaks that language fluently. Together, these add significant cost and complexity.
  • Start product research with English-language ads. The US English market has a larger total addressable market (TAM) (all the buyers you could reach) than smaller language markets. Study which creatives (ad images and videos) and messages already run there before you look at underserved markets (markets few sellers serve).
  • Language arbitrage (profiting from a language gap): take an ad that already converts (turns viewers into buyers) in one language and run it in a language market that has not seen it yet. The Spanish-speaking market in the US has about 60 million people. Its TAM is smaller than the English market, but it has far less competition.
  • The Spanish-language market in the US is largely untapped (few sellers serve it yet). Its TAM is smaller than the white and Black American markets. But ad auctions (the bidding for ad space) are less crowded, so demand is proven while competition is not.
  • Products whose ads are mostly in Spanish can be run. But only run them if the landing pages (the page buyers arrive on after clicking) are customized and everything is translated into Spanish. This is harder for a beginner, so beginners should skip Spanish-language ad products at the start.
  • Taobao videos (videos from Taobao, a large Chinese shopping site) mostly feature Asian presenters and may underperform in the US market. Recast them (replace the people on screen) with white or Black American talent when you produce custom ads for the US.
  • When the market is English-speaking, a product that worked for a long time there can be tested in other languages such as German or French. Native-language markets (countries where that language is the everyday language) often have less competition.
  • Validate a product that sells well in one country. Run the same product with ads in the language of another target market. If the new market also shows a strong hook rate (the share of viewers who keep watching past the first seconds) and CTR (click-through rate: the share of viewers who click), the product is worth more store work.
  • When testing an ebook (a digital book) or another digital product in a new country, translate the mockup (a rough preview of the product) and the ad copy (the written words in the ad) into that country's language before you run ads. The example built a German mockup for a German market test. This makes it possible to judge whether the offer itself is attractive to local buyers.
  • Entering an untapped market needs only minimal effort beyond the product. Native translations and local payment methods are required. Stripe (a payment provider) lets you enable the most used payment methods for each country. Custom branding (your own logo and look) or UGC (user-generated content: real customer videos and photos) is not needed from the start.
  • Study what works in other markets to find message patterns (wording that sells again and again). But do not copy a competitor's exact wording. Adapt the underlying structure to your own market.
  • Pricing differs by market. Australian dollars look cheap to US buyers, so check pricing and currency per country.
  • When pricing in countries where the local currency is much smaller than USD (US dollars), such as Malaysia at 4 MYR (Malaysian ringgit) to 1 USD, do not automatically adjust price points by converting wholesale cost (what you pay the supplier). Validate your intended prices against what other online stores in that country sell for. Use market-based pricing (prices set by what the local market pays).
  • For an Italian-market test in Q4 (October to December), classic, mid-priced jewelry was suggested. The reason given was that Italian buyers were said to prefer classic styles over expensive fashion. Treat this as a hypothesis (a guess to test, not a proven fact). Validate it with a small test budget before scaling.

Testing a proven product in a new market

2 min
  • Do not reinvent the wheel. Take a product that sells well in one market. Test it in a market where nobody is selling it in volume (in large amounts). Check the product in the new market before scaling.
  • Start by testing products that already show scaling ads in the USA or Australia. A product that works there may also work in other countries. Adapt the store to the new market, including language and payment methods.
  • Differentiating by market: launch a product proven in the US in English-speaking countries such as Australia, New Zealand, the UK and Canada. Or launch it in European markets such as Germany, France, the Netherlands and Switzerland. Check in the Facebook Ad Library whether the product is scaling in the new market. Low competition there is the differentiator (the thing that sets you apart).
  • Comfy case: a sleeved blanket (a blanket with sleeves), first sold in the US market, was adapted with minor product and design changes. It was launched in Australia and New Zealand and reached over 300 million dollars in sales. The same concept was later launched in Switzerland under a different brand name by another seller. That seller knew the market well and sold well there.
  • Question for research into a new product market: if a product is not selling in a new country, check three things. Do competitors exist there? Are their store and ads good? Does the market accept this product type? A lack of sales usually comes from missing trust, missing native-language content, or the market not yet knowing the product. The last one requires educating customers (teaching buyers what the product is).
  • Competitor products proven in one market can be tested in another. Copy the product and store and change the store currency (for example to euros or Australian dollars) and the language. Stores in weak condition with many ads running still prove the product sells, so the offer and store can be improved.
  • Example Swiss test: an early dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships to the buyer, so you hold no stock) test in Switzerland spent about 200 to 300 USD on ads. It produced roughly 3,000 CHF (Swiss francs) in sales. That low-spend result was the signal to test the product further.
  • Test the product's appeal on a shared identity (a feeling of belonging to the same group) rather than on local details. A product tied to a shared identity, such as an African heritage map shape, can reach a broad audience across many countries. Buyers respond to the same feeling of connection.
  • Validate a product idea by testing it in the new market before scaling. Let the new market's hook rate and CTR decide whether it earns more store work.

Supply chain, lead times and delivery

1 min
  • A supplier with inventory in the country where you sell is necessary. Cross-continent shipping (shipping between continents, for example Australia to Europe) makes costs too high. With shipping included, it roughly doubles product prices.
  • Products that are backordered (sold out, waiting for new stock) for months should not get paid traffic (visitors from paid ads) until stock returns. Genuinely custom made-to-order products (made only after an order comes in), with a known build time (for example custom pool tables taking about two months), can still be advertised.
  • Products with production or delivery times of about 10 weeks or more should get very limited ad spend (money put into ads). Customers rarely buy them without knowing the lead time (the wait from order to delivery). So the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) tends to be very low.
  • Fitness products suffered the most from port backlogs (ships waiting to unload at ports) and supplier shortages after 2020. In the 2021 situation, the fitness industry was the niche to avoid for the time being. Stock was thin, and a store could not reliably fill orders.
  • A weak supply chain (the chain of suppliers and shipping that gets products to buyers) can make an otherwise promising niche unworkable. Check supplier responsiveness and parcel reliability before committing money.
  • Seasonal products (sold only at certain times of year) may be copied quickly. Shipping times from suppliers can delay launches. Plan the next seasonal product early.

  • A product that worked in 2018 in the kids niche was picked up for 2020. It generated about 10k in first sales after its competitor data was used to build a better offer.
  • Products that take a practical approach, such as a tool-type home product, can be tested in these markets as shown in the earlier sections. Treat them as examples, not guarantees.

Holiday and event procedures

1 min

Valentine's Day campaign (procedure, as written)

  1. Build a separate store for the Valentine's Day campaign about one month before the holiday.
  2. Prepare around five products with everything needed: creatives, offers and videos. Then ads or organic posts (free posts, not paid ads) can start immediately.
  3. Target men and parents as well as women, since many buyers are not the female partner.
  4. Consider products for couples, such as matching bracelets where each partner wears one part.
  5. Check shipping times. Start ads early enough that the product arrives before the date.
  6. Find the winning product (the product that sells best) at least about 15 days before the holiday.

Q4 product research procedure (as written)

  1. Find products that are trending (becoming popular right now) in Q4 (October to December). Examples: gifts, winter clothing, personalized jewelry.
  2. Look for similar designs on AliExpress (a large online marketplace for products from Chinese suppliers).
  3. Choose designs that look decent and test them in ads.
  4. Repeat in Q1 (January to March) with fitness products.
