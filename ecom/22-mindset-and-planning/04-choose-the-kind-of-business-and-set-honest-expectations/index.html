Playbook › 22. Mindset and planning

Choose the kind of business and set honest expectations

Section 22.4 · 4 of 26 in this chapter · 15 min read

In short

  • Decide early what kind of business you want: a sale in 2 to 3 years, a freedom business with a small team, or a company of 15 to 30+ people.
  • Start with fast-selling pump-and-dump products, not supplements. Use the cash and lessons to build your own brand later.
  • Build one store that makes a profit before you open a second one.
  • Expect months or years of steady work. Nobody can promise a fixed income, so judge results by your own numbers.
This section has 12 parts. Tap one to open it.

Decide the kind of business before anything else

1 min
  • Scaling needs a new mindset. Stop asking whether the business works. Start deciding how big you want it to become.
  • Decide early what kind of business you want. There are three options:
    • One you run for 2 to 3 years, then sell.
    • One that gives you freedom with a small team.
    • One that grows to 15 to 30+ people with several stores.
  • This choice shapes every hiring and process decision. Every team member must understand the company's direction.
  • Brand building (creating your own brand instead of selling generic products) is a long-term commitment. It does not suit everyone, because it needs steady content and patience instead of quick sales. Decide whether you are willing to invest that time before you start.
  • Brand building takes several years, not a short project. Plan for patience, because partnerships and supplier improvements take time.
  • Dropshipping means you sell a product, and a supplier (the company that makes or stocks it) ships it to the buyer. You never hold the stock yourself. High-ticket dropshipping means selling expensive products, often $1,000+ each. It suits people who want to leave a job, gain control over their time and money, and who work long hours with no way out.

Choose your entry path

2 min
  • Revenue means the money you take in from sales, before costs. Beginners should start with pump-and-dump products instead of supplements (vitamins and similar health products). Supplements compete against established brands that earn very large monthly revenues. A pump-and-dump is a product that sells fast for a short time. It teaches selling, testing and fast action with less risk.
  • A pump-and-dump is a stepping stone, not a destination. Follow these steps to use it to build a long-term business:
    1. Enter a pump-and-dump product to earn quick cash.
    2. Use the sales period to learn how ads work and how to test creatives (ad images and videos you try to see which ones sell).
    3. Reinvest the earned cash into a long-term brand.
    4. Level up by building the new brand with the skills and cash from the earlier product.
  • Treating a pump-and-dump as a forever brand leads to getting stuck. Its job is to make cash, teach advertising and fund a later brand.
  • A niche is a small market segment, such as one type of customer or product group. Once a niche has produced a clear winner with strong revenue, you can grow it into a brand with related products, such as adjacent fashion items (clothing next to the winning item). Expand carefully, because the niche may fade (lose buyers over time).
  • A Swiss brand for a wearable blanket started as dropshipping and moved to stock (products you buy and keep in your own warehouse) once early tests justified it. The sequence was:
    • Early tests with about 200 to 300 USD in ads produced about 3,000 CHF (Swiss francs) in sales in Switzerland. That justified the move to stock.
    • In April 25, designs were bought. A photo shoot was held in mid-May and June. About 500 units (individual items) were ordered for the season, at roughly 10,000 USD in product cost.
    • Offers were set at 30 off, free local shipping and 2 to 3 day delivery, a buy-two saving of 14, and a code (a coupon the buyer types in) for 5 off a single item.
    • Ads cost about 5,000 USD in winter, for about 18,000 in November sales. That is roughly break-even overall, meaning no profit and no loss.
    • The first stock sold out by the end of December, with 410 orders and no refunds (money given back to buyers). After new stock arrived, customers kept ordering without ads, bringing about 1,000 USD in revenue.
    • Next steps were custom partner designs, such as a football club logo for a one-time fee of about 10,000 USD for one year. Also Galaxus, and Germany and Austria.
  • Use the opportunity of a physical retail location (a real shop you rent) to get access to brands and to learn which products customers return. Sell online only the brands whose return rates (how often buyers send items back) and manufacturer policies (the brand's own rules, for example on returns) make online sales workable. **

Treat it as a business, not a side hustle

1 min
  • Treat dropshipping as a real business that needs to be run, not as a side hustle (a small extra job on the side).
  • Building a business takes months, not days. Be patient with the process and plan the timeline accordingly.
  • Each product test is part of running a real company with a revenue target in the six or seven figure range (100,000 dollars or more a year). It is not a quick product pump-and-dump. A disciplined process with patience is what separates a business from one-off wins.
  • Building a store is a long process that often takes weeks or months, not a few clicks. Consistent work over time is what produces results. **
  • Dropshipping is widely promoted as a fast-money method. So many newcomers quit once they see the real work involved. Building a real business with a professional approach is what lasts. **
  • Build for the long term rather than hoping for quick launches. A store can be built on the first launch. Results compound (grow on top of earlier results) over the next decade. **
  • Many online shop owners become so absorbed in the business process or in finding a product that they neglect the basics (the fundamentals). Without knowing where the business and its products stand in the market, and which customers to attract, the rest of the work is built on an unclear base. **

Build the first store before you expand

2 min
  • Advice on growing the business is best given after the first store works. A new owner has no baseline (a starting point to compare results against) to judge results. Start small, measure, and only then add stores, staff or agencies. Agencies are service businesses that sell marketing or other help to others. **
  • Before opening another store, make a store with no sales profitable:
    1. Ask friends and family to browse the store as a potential customer and give honest feedback on trust and what stands out.
    2. Make improvements based on that feedback.
    3. Focus on optimizing (making it work better) the ads and generating profitable sales.
    4. Open a new store only after this one is profitable.
  • Starting a high-ticket dropshipping business from zero is a split effort, meaning the work is different at each stage. Beginners set up and launch their first store. People who already run one focus on growing it. Anyone starting from scratch should work through setup and launch before moving to growth and marketing systems (repeatable ways to find customers). **
  • Typical early challenges for new dropshippers are setting up the online store, finding reliable suppliers, and running profitable ads. Plan time and learning for each of these from the start. (Older advice, 2019-22 source; no exact date.)
  • Dropshipping requires several skills at once: computer work, customer service and a business mindset (thinking like a business owner). So it covers more than a typical degree. Learning does not end, but the challenges change as the business grows.
  • Dropshipping requires several skills, such as product finding, copywriting (writing sales text), video editing and Facebook ads (paid posts shown to chosen people). Results move up and down over time. Expect setbacks and plan for them rather than assuming the business will be easy.
  • Dropshipping is simple but not easy at first. Each process becomes second nature (automatic) with repetition. Early overwhelm is normal.
  • The first goal for beginners is experience and learning how to produce good creative content (ads, images and videos that get attention), not making money. Sales follow once the skills are developed.
  • When starting out, focus on gaining experience in posting and making content rather than on immediate profit or finding a winning product. Learning to produce good content is the prerequisite (the thing you need first) for later profits.
  • A new agency cannot reach 10 clients within two weeks. Early growth depends on building confidence and a portfolio (a collection of your past work) first. Often this comes through free work for friends and family.

Plan the exit deliberately

1 min
  • Build to sell. Store multiples (how many times a store's yearly profit its sale price is) are currently at their highest levels in 12+ years. Selling a profitable store generates capital (money to invest) for scaling or starting new ventures. **
  • Start preparing for a sale early. The sale process needs time, whether or not a broker (a person paid to find buyers and handle the deal) is used. Telling buyers that a trusted person runs the business and will stay is very valuable.
  • Expect buyers to push back (argue for a lower price) on expenses. Do not take this personally, because the store is a business deal, not a personal project. Pushback on expenses is very common in negotiations.
  • Selling during a steep growth phase (sales rising fast) can feel like selling the farm (selling your most valuable thing too early). Some sellers regret it when the business doubles soon after. Choose the sale timing deliberately, based on the trend, rather than on emotion.
  • Choose the sale date by the revenue trend, not by emotion. If growth is still steep, a sale can leave money on the table (lose money you could have earned). Waiting until the curve flattens (growth slows) may produce a better price.
  • A website is the output of a repeatable skill set. That skill set is the real asset the operator (the person who runs the business) keeps after a sale. Selling a single site while keeping the process lets the operator build the next one.
  • Expect a business to change through growth stages. The main decisions at each stage are the ad budget (how much you spend on ads), the number of products, and the use of the existing audience (the people who already buy from you). **

Read the economic and market conditions

2 min

Recession and downturns

  • High-ticket dropshipping (products priced $1,000+) remains viable (workable and profitable) even during economic downturns and recessions. Wealthy consumers continue buying premium goods. Niches with lower competition but higher margins (more profit kept per sale) can grow rapidly. **
  • In the 2007-2008 economic collapse, the author built a high-ticket e-commerce business to multiple millions in revenue per year. Economic downturns create opportunity for those with online infrastructure (online sales systems and tools) while local competitors shut down. **

Inflation and costs

  • Inflation (prices rising over time) and rising costs are a permanent reality. Expect products to become more expensive, and adapt your pricing. Launch now rather than waiting. 13 years of business proves successful adaptation is possible regardless of economic conditions. **
  • Plan for rising costs. Inflation is expected to raise wholesale prices (what suppliers charge sellers) and retail prices (what buyers pay). So keep a broad set of suppliers and monitor your margins regularly. **

Supply chain

  • The supply chain (the whole path from supplier to buyer) has real challenges, but they affect different niches to different degrees. If you work with 10+ suppliers, enough alternatives exist to stay profitable. Keep contacting suppliers while sales go through existing partners. **
  • A store owner used a supplier list of 20 to 25 approved brands. But a niche with only five suppliers was not enough. Broadening the niche one level (choosing a wider market group) brought the count to 20 or more. That allowed the store to move forward with a workable supplier base.

Post-pandemic demand

  • After the COVID-19 pandemic, e-commerce adoption (how many people shop online) returned to its historical 20-year growth rate after the lockdown spike (the sudden jump in online buying during lockdowns). Shopify (a platform for building online stores) hired for the spike rate, adding 1,000 people. Then it laid off 1,000 people (10% of its workforce) on July 26, 2022, when growth normalized. This does not signal e-commerce decline. Growth continues above pre-pandemic levels. **
  • Government cash payments to consumers, such as the 2020 US stimulus checks, can produce sales spikes for stores selling higher-priced products. Buyers spend money they did not expect to have. Such payments are not guaranteed and have no set date, so a store plan should not depend on them.

Competition and market size

  • Competition in dropshipping is rising slowly, not sharply, and many niches still have room. Businesses come and go. So a list of about 20 market ideas gives enough options to find a market with a suitable level of competition. **
  • New drop shippers entering a niche rarely harm a well-run store. Many of them rely on weak methods, such as cold social ads (ads shown to people who did not ask for them) or supplier directories (lists of suppliers). Judge your store by its own metrics (measured numbers such as sales and profit), not by how many new competitors appear.
  • A single profitable store is a tiny share of total online buying, so one store does not put other businesses out of business. The US alone has about 330 million people, most of them online shoppers. **

Effort and expectations

2 min
  • Your timeline will differ from others, because your store, products, website and marketing style will be unique. The time needed to reach a goal is not a measure of success. What matters is whether you actually reach it. **
  • Compare only your own progress, because build time varies by person. **
  • Do not expect quick results. Dropshipping requires sustained effort (steady work) over months and years before significant profits appear.
  • Dropshipping and other e-commerce businesses usually take months of consistent work before results appear. Expecting quick riches is a common reason people quit. So plan for sustained effort over a long period, not fast returns.
  • Building an e-commerce business requires long-term commitment. Even in the worst case, five years to $300,000 to $500,000 in profit is valuable and life-changing.
  • High-ticket dropshipping is simple as a model but not easy to execute. The first one to two years usually demand long hours and many failed experiments. Expecting quick results without this workload leads to discouragement. **
  • High-ticket dropshipping is not a quick fix or get-rich-quick plan. It requires sustained hard work, time and consistency. It is not a fit for anyone who expects results in two weeks. **
  • There are no get-rich-quick shortcuts, and business requires consistent work. Success depends mainly on following a process consistently until it works. Quitting early is the most common reason for failure. **
  • There is no secret formula for dropshipping. Success comes from building basic skills, testing several products and offers (deals on products), and scaling the one that wins.
  • Expect the first real results from e-commerce to take around six to twelve months, with a plateau in between. The plateau is usually the hardest part. Continued consistent work is what gets a store past it.
  • Low start-up cost does not mean easy. The low cost is balanced by the work you need to put in. Business is not easy. The people who succeed are the driven, focused, committed and patient ones. **
  • Dropshipping is simple but not easy at first. Early overwhelm is normal.

Never promise income

1 min
  • Do not promise or expect a fixed income from the business model. Results vary widely based on niche, optimization, traffic (visitors to your store) and effort. So set short-term goals, such as a net profit (profit after all costs) target over the next 90 days. **
  • Income from this model varies widely, from zero to millions. It depends on how you run the system, how fast you grow and how much you spend on ads as you scale. Do not plan around a fixed profit figure. **
  • There is no reliable average for first-year sales or profit in dropshipping. Results vary widely depending on the work and optimization done. Plan with your own test numbers rather than industry averages. **
  • Avoid promising revenue amounts by a fixed date. Launch timing depends on many variables, such as account flags (warnings or blocks placed on your account by the platform) and optimization delays. Most stores need several weeks of setup and data before results can be predicted.
  • Revenue goals such as $1M are possible but never guaranteed. Income claims should not be made to customers or prospects (people who may buy). Set goals based on your own tracking, not promises.
  • A six-figure sales month (100,000 dollars or more in sales) is gross sales, not net profit. A two-person family store launched in 2023 reported its first six-figure sales month in May 2024. Six-figure months followed in June and July 2024. The owners had a steep learning curve before reaching them.

Be skeptical of online claims

1 min
  • Be skeptical (doubtful until proven) of people claiming success online. Verify that they can actually prove their business makes money, because many large creators promote products they don't actually use or believe in.
  • Do not trust online claims that promise a fixed income within a fixed time. Profit timing varies with the product, the market and the store's own setup, so such promises are usually unreliable. **
  • Be skeptical about claims made online that suppliers refuse new retailers. Such claims often describe a specific period or industry, such as fitness products during the pandemic. That does not apply broadly. **
  • Most people who start an online store never get to the stage of having traffic (visitors). The odds of success improve a lot once a store gets its first real sales. Opening an empty Shopify store is a very small commitment. So an alleged 98% failure rate is not a reliable measure. **

Use AI as leverage, not as passive income

1 min
  • Traditional dropshipping relied on guesswork and hustle (hard, fast work). AI (artificial intelligence, software that can write, sort and create things) dropshipping relies on systems and leverage (getting more results from the same effort). The seller becomes an operator who uses technology to outpace competitors, rather than only a seller.
  • AI amplifies entrepreneurs (boosts business owners) rather than replacing them. Sellers who use AI correctly are expected to outcompete those who do not.
  • This is not passive income. Passive income means money from investments, such as rental property or dividends (shares of profit paid out), that needs no daily work. A dropshipping business does not provide that on its own. It demands a lot of work early on. **
