Playbook › 22. Mindset and planning

Work with partners, family, team and delegation

Section 22.23 · 23 of 26 in this chapter · 14 min read

In short

  • Agree with your partner on money, goals and roles before you start.
  • Learn each task yourself before you hand it to someone else.
  • Set up your system and check your team's work before you hire more people.
  • Hire for daily running tasks first, and make sure the business can handle more sales before you push for them.
  • Treat your partner, staff and customers with honesty and respect.
This section has 15 parts. Tap one to open it.

Partners and spouses: agree before you start

1 min
  • Build an emergency fund before you start a business with a spouse. An emergency fund is money saved for hard times. It helps the household handle losses or slower income.
  • Discuss how much financial risk each partner accepts before the business starts. Financial risk means the chance of losing money.
  • Agree on finances and family responsibilities before launch. Launch means the day the business opens. Money is named as the top reason for divorce. Treat the business as one part of the family plan. Do not let it take from the family plan.
  • Both partners must agree on the vision, which is the long-term goal, and the big picture before committing.
  • Share a long-term vision. It should cover income goals, the number of stores and family plans.
  • Confirm that both partners are on board with that vision.
  • A mismatch on children or career can break the partnership later.
  • Rent a separate office as early as possible. This keeps work and home life apart.

Partners and spouses: roles and fit

1 min
  • Each partner should understand their own personality type and the other's before dividing responsibilities.
  • Each partner must know their own strengths and weaknesses. Then tasks go to the person best suited for them.
  • Keep your ego under control. Ego means pride that gets in the way.
  • Listen to the input of others. Input means ideas and advice. Others include partners, advisers and peers. Peers are people at the same level as you.

Partners and spouses: communication and feedback

1 min
  • Communicate openly and often with your partner. Talk about progress, problems and expectations.
  • When giving feedback to a business partner, balance praise with constructive criticism. Constructive criticism means useful comments on what to improve.
  • Without this balance, the working relationship suffers.
  • Notice and compliment each other's contributions regularly. This keeps motivation, meaning drive to keep going, high.
  • Celebrate every goal and success when it is reached. Do not wait for the end. This keeps the team motivated.
  • Keep a sense of humor in the partnership, especially in stressful times.
  • Humor reduces pressure between partners.

Partners and spouses: boundaries, mood and rest

1 min
  • Keep separate hobbies and personal time outside of work.
  • Constant togetherness at home and at work causes friction, meaning tension and arguments.
  • Respect that each partner has interests of their own.
  • Protect work-life balance on purpose. Work-life balance means having time for work and time for life.
  • Otherwise the business can consume the whole relationship.
  • Combine intense work periods with deliberate rest and fun.
  • This keeps effort sustainable, meaning you can keep it up over time.

Founder first: learn before you delegate

1 min
  • As a beginner, do not outsource right away. Outsourcing means paying others to do work.
  • Learn product research, which means finding products that could sell. Learn store building, which means setting up your online shop. Learn ad launching, which means starting paid ads. Do this yourself first. Then begin delegating, which means handing tasks to someone else.
  • Do the full email-finding process yourself at least once.
  • Then write clear instructions for a VA. A VA is a virtual assistant, a remote helper.
  • Almost every step can be delegated. Only choosing the topics and deciding which targets to pursue stays with you. Targets are the people or companies you want to reach.
  • Build a system. A system is a set way of doing work.
  • Know every part of the business before delegating it.
  • Only then can you judge whether a hire is doing the work correctly. A hire is a new team member.
  • Build a team early when you lack time.
  • Give each assistant the tasks you understand yourself first. That way you can judge their work.
  • Running ads yourself gives full control over the data. Data means the numbers.
  • Fulfillment, customer support, creatives and store building can be done by freelancers. Fulfillment means packing and shipping orders. Creatives means ad images and videos. Freelancers are independent workers.
  • Ad management stays in-house, meaning your own team handles it.
  • Use the time freed up by delegation for higher-value work.
  • Examples are product research, ad creative and offer development. An offer is the deal you make to customers.
  • Do not use that time for routine tasks.
  • The Four-Hour Workweek by Timothy Ferris is a useful source for delegation ideas and the mindset of handing off work.

Outsourcing timing: when each rule applies

1 min

Sources give different starting points. Use the one that matches your situation.

New store, more time than money Invest your own hours early in PPC (pay-per-click ads, where you pay each time someone clicks) to learn the setup and campaign mechanics, meaning how campaigns work. Then outsource
More money than time Pay a specialist to run the ads
You have time Learn traffic generation (getting visitors to your store) yourself. This will be the most valuable skill for the business. Allocate about one hour a day to it
Lack of time, team forming Build a team early, but give each assistant the tasks you understand yourself
Still doing everything alone after about six months This signals you are not willing to hand tasks to someone better placed. Some solo hustle (working alone hard) in the first six to twelve months is normal
  • Doing everything yourself is the biggest confidence killer. A confidence killer destroys your belief in yourself.
  • Pay-per-click work becomes more time-intensive as spend grows. Spend means the money you put into ads.
  • A new store with more time than money should invest its own hours early. This is to learn the setup and understand the campaign mechanics. Do this before outsourcing.
  • Do not take on challenges by making excuses.
  • Do not take on challenges by delegating them away.
  • Growth comes from doing the work, not from waiting for perfect conditions.

Outsourcing decisions and emotional cost

1 min
  • Consider emotional cost, meaning how much a task drains you mentally, alongside financial cost.
  • If you hate managing ads enough to stop doing it, outsourcing is worth the cost.
  • This holds even when strict hourly ROI math shows it is marginal. ROI means return on investment, the profit made compared with the money spent. Marginal means small or barely worth it.
  • Preventing burnout, meaning total exhaustion, and regaining mental energy have business value.
  • Delegating work significantly reduces the owner's stress.
  • This improves business longevity, meaning how long the business lasts.
  • It lowers the risk that the owner burns out. Burnout can lead the owner to close, sell or exit (leave) a business that could have been kept.
  • Outsourcing complements time management and productivity methods. Time management means planning your hours. Productivity methods are ways of getting more done.
  • An owner who does every task will eventually face more work than time allows. This is true however well they organize themselves.
  • Trying to handle everything personally leads to overload, meaning too much to handle.
  • This includes pure distractions. Overload happens however good the owner's self-organization is.
  • Delegate tasks that do not need the owner.
  • Done-for-you services offered by the course team are optional, and most cost extra. Done-for-you services means someone else does the whole job.
  • The course content alone contains everything needed.
  • Paid help can speed up progress. It can also cover tasks you dislike, such as website design, product uploads or market validation. Market validation means checking that people want the product.

Systems before people

1 min
  • Get organized before hiring anyone.
  • Have a system with a clear task overview, which is a list of all the work to do.
  • Include progress tracking, which means checking how far each job has gone.
  • Include an approachable channel for questions, meaning an easy way to ask.
  • Begin each day by checking the task board. A task board is a shared list of jobs, often shown as cards.
  • Check your own tasks and your team's tasks.
  • The goal is that every card is done within its deadline and the store is running.
  • Aim for a system that can be pressed to run.
  • A human checks the nuanced parts. Nuanced parts are details that need judgment.
  • Give AI, meaning artificial intelligence software, the right training and a proven methodology, which is a tested way of working.
  • AI can then run a repeatable process with only the necessary oversight.

The owner ladder

2 min

Source B's owner ladder is older advice (2021-22). Its structure still applies. The owner ladder is a set of steps, from doing all the work to only collecting profit.

  • Growing sales without growing operations and finances at the same pace usually leads to burnout or business failure. Operations means the daily running of the business.
  • Growth holds only when the back end, meaning the behind-the-scenes work, can carry the extra volume.
  • An owner who still answers customer questions cannot scale.
  • An owner who still fixes checkout or product pages cannot scale. Checkout is the page where customers pay.
  • An owner who still handles stock-outs or updates prices cannot scale. Stock-outs are times when products run out.
  • Leaving daily operations is the precondition for growth. A precondition is something that must come first.
  • The owner ladder has five levels:
    1. Doing all the work yourself.
    2. Paying others to do it.
    3. Managing those who do the work.
    4. Managing those who work on the business.
    5. At the top, only collecting profit distributions. Distributions are payouts of profit to owners.
  • This advice sets profit distributions at about once per quarter. A quarter is three months.
  • Working in the business means performing tasks. These are customer service, website and product work at the base.
  • At the higher level, it means marketing such as SEO (search engine optimization, getting free visitors from search), PPC and email.
  • Working on the business means building a machine that runs without you.
  • This includes hiring, creating systems and managing finances.
  • One person cannot run marketing, operations and finance at scale. Scale means large size.
  • So outsourcing work such as SEO and PPC is required.
  • An HR manager (the person for hiring and staff matters) can handle hiring.
  • An operations manager can build processes.
  • A CFO (chief financial officer, the top money leader) can manage finances if the owner is weak in those areas.
  • Owners may keep tasks they enjoy and do well, such as customer calls or running PPC.
  • This is fine as long as the choice is deliberate, meaning chosen on purpose.
  • The owner can sit anywhere between doing basic tasks and doing none.
  • But not because they believe they cannot delegate.
  • The owner stays ultimately responsible for the business.
  • They should understand what each person does.
  • Strong hires, meaning good team members, should then be trusted to do their work without constant checking.
  • Over-supervising holds them back. It means checking every step too closely.

Hiring order and roles

1 min
  • Map all roles first. Mapping means drawing out each job.
  • A solo owner fills every box of the company's role chart at once. A role chart is a diagram of all the jobs in a company.
  • A solo owner also switches roles all day.
  • Mapping the roles shows which boxes the first hires should fill.
  • Do not look for a mini-version of yourself to hire.
  • Give each hire one main role with at most one or two side roles.
  • This way, no one is stretched too thin, meaning given too many jobs.
  • Hire maintenance roles before growth roles. Maintenance roles keep the business running, such as customer service and order handling.
  • Growth roles bring more sales, such as marketing.
  • Growing sales into an unready operation multiplies problems. 10 times the sales means 10 times the problems.
  • Scale hiring one role at a time.
  • Build product listers first. These are people who add products to the store.
  • Then add customer service, then operations.
  • This prevents chaos. It lets you perfect each function before adding the next layer.

Capacity planning and staff budget

1 min

Capacity planning means working out how much work your team can handle.

  • Once a business is in the growth phase, operating capacity is the main limit on growth. Operating capacity means how much work the team and systems can handle.
  • Ask how many orders and sales the team and systems can absorb before tasks slip. Slip means get delayed or forgotten.
  • Examples of slipping include supplier orders that are forgotten, or customers chasing late orders.
  • Aim for about 80% capacity utilization, not 100%. Utilization means how much of the capacity is used.
  • Full capacity burns out staff, meaning it exhausts them. It also leaves no time for improvement.
  • Running at the limit throttles growth, meaning it slows growth down.
  • In seasonal niches, which are markets that sell mainly in certain seasons, save surplus, meaning extra money, in the staff account during the peak season, the busiest time.
  • Use that money to pay for the slow season.
  • Avoid laying off people in slow months. Laying off means letting staff go.
  • Give them other useful work instead.
  • Hire before you need the person.
  • Recruiting, meaning finding people, and training take longer than expected.
  • Allocate more to staff than the current need requires. This gives a buffer, meaning a safety margin.
  • New hires need time before they add results.
  • Budget committed to staff should be spent on hires.
  • Money left idle while the plan says it is for staff means the plan is not being followed.
  • Hire into the staff allocation on a schedule, not on impulse. Impulse means a sudden decision.
  • Check the budget against real revenue, meaning actual money coming in, and remaining staff funds. Do this before adding any new role.
  • Plan each hire as an ongoing role that may last for years.
  • Do not plan it as a short placement.
  • Repeatedly rehiring costs time and money. It is one of the worst outcomes in team management.

Leadership, performance and trust

1 min
  • Evaluate leadership on two dimensions, meaning two sides to judge.
  • The two sides are Performance and Trust.
  • Performance means skills and results on the job.
  • Trust means integrity, which is honesty, plus reliability and character.
  • High performers with low trust are toxic. Toxic means harmful to the team.
  • They damage culture long-term. Culture is how people behave together.
  • Medium performers with high trust are more valuable than high performers with low trust.
  • Organizations over-measure performance, using thousands of metrics. Metrics are numbers you track.
  • Organizations under-measure trust, using zero metrics.
  • This causes them to promote toxic people.
  • Reverse this. Make trust equally important.
  • Allow creative team members to be creative.
  • Do not micromanage them. Micromanaging means checking every small detail.
  • Micromanaging prevents you from getting their best work.
  • It also contradicts why you hired them.
  • If you want to go fast, go alone.
  • If you want to go further, go together.
  • Lean on your team's perspectives.
  • Include diverse viewpoints, meaning different opinions, when developing brand strategy. Brand strategy is the plan for how your brand looks and talks.

Treating staff with respect

2 min
  • Even without a formal employment contract, which is a written work agreement, once someone works for you, you are their employer.
  • They experience the relationship that way.
  • Treat outsourced staff as you would a local employee. Outsourced staff are people hired from outside, often remotely.
  • Give them support and development, meaning help to grow.
  • Many owners fail at outsourcing because they value a remote assistant less.
  • This happens when pay is low compared with local wages in their own country.
  • That attitude leads to weak effort, poor results and little success with outsourced staff.
  • Greet your team at the start of each day.
  • Do this even when you have no task to discuss.
  • Ignoring staff creates the same poor impression as a boss who walks past the team without acknowledging anyone.
  • Hold performance reviews, which are formal talks about work results.
  • Make them positive and focused on development.
  • Name what the person does well.
  • Then identify weaknesses together. Ask how the work could be improved and what they need.
  • Harsh threats do not produce improvement.
  • An example of a harsh threat is telling someone to fix their act or be fired tomorrow.
  • The person never gets a real chance to explain what they find difficult.
  • Give them a chance.
  • Work with them using empathy. Empathy means understanding how they feel.
  • Apply empathy in every interaction with staff.
  • Imagine being in their position.
  • Ask whether you would be happy with how you treat them. If the answer is no, change your approach.
  • As soon as you hire staff, you are a leader.
  • Your example sets the tone for the team.
  • A poor manager cannot expect good work from the team.
  • Treat people with the respect you would want yourself.
  • Treat other members, meaning people in the community, with respect.
  • Avoid bickering, which is small, pointless arguing. Conflict does not help results.
  • Address problems that disrupt the community directly.
  • Keep the focus on positivity, helping others and results.
  • Success is a team effort.
  • No single person is responsible for it, so every contribution matters.
  • Do not look down on others.
  • Everyone is valued equally, regardless of their stage. Stage means how far along they are in their journey.

Honesty and values in the business

1 min
  • Do not misrepresent your experience, skills or how long you have been doing business.
  • This applies to customers, suppliers and community members alike.
  • Lying about your skills, experience or track record is a serious mistake. Track record means your past results.
  • Lying is a mistake when done to sell products or to create extra income in a community.
  • Such misrepresentation tends to be discovered.
  • Those involved can be removed from the community.
  • Handle every business issue with honesty and transparency, which means openness.
  • Do this with colleagues and with customers.
  • Ethical integrity, meaning doing what is right, is the highest standard for long-term success.
  • Stay humble and keep learning.
  • There is always a way to improve, regardless of revenue, meaning money coming in.
  • Track changes in marketing continuously.
  • Share what works.

Family and life outside the business

1 min
  • Schedule regular calls with family back home, including grandparents.
  • This keeps children connected and relatives included.
  • Plan activities together to make the time away worthwhile.
  • Keeping store phone duty, meaning answering the store's phone, while living in a different time zone meant waking at 4:30 a.m. every day. A time zone is the hour difference between places.
  • Hand over phone handling to a trained assistant before a long trip abroad.
