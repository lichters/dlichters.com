Manage risk and fallback plans

Section 22.21 · 21 of 26 in this chapter · 6 min read

In short

  • Measure a risk before you take it. Know the good result you hope for, the bad result, and your backup steps before you spend money or time.
  • Never put everything on one result. Keep a plan B (a backup plan) and know how long it would take to recover if it fails.
  • Staying in a job is also a risk. Your pay often rises more slowly than prices.
  • Have a second place to live and a second way to sell, so one failure does not stop everything.
  • Stop a bet that keeps losing money early. Do not keep paying for it out of hope.

Assess the Risk Before You Commit

Calculated Risk Versus Reckless Risk

  • Business requires taking calculated risk, which means a risk you measure before you take it.
  • Avoid reckless risk, which means a risk taken without thought.
  • Some risk is necessary to succeed and to serve customers.
  • A calculated risk is taken only after you define the concrete goal and the benefit it brings.
  • Do not take a calculated risk for the thrill of the risk itself.
  • Each bet needs three things before any money or time is spent: a stated upside (the good result you hope for), a stated downside (the bad result), and a mitigation plan (steps that reduce the damage).
  • Putting everything on a single outcome, such as going all-in on one venture or one market, is gambling, not managed risk-taking.
  • A managed risk taker limits how much of the total is exposed to any one result.
  • Your risk tolerance (how much risk you can handle) should match your life situation.
  • Avoid risks that threaten a mortgage (a home loan) or dependents (people who rely on you).
  • Only take large risks you can afford to lose.
  • Build safety bars (firm limits that protect you) before spending heavily.
  • Start with a limited budget, test products, and move on if one fails.
  • Spending a large budget on one product early leaves no money to test a second product.
  • That also makes mistakes more painful.
  • Credit cards (borrowed money you pay back later) only make sense for an established, profitable business.
  • That business may need cash flow (money coming in and going out) while payments clear.
  • They give room to leverage (using borrowed money to grow) only when income is already coming in.

Steps Before a Major Risk

Work through these steps in order before taking a major risk:

  1. Define the actual goal and what achieving it would give you.
  2. Estimate the potential reward.
  3. Estimate the potential risk, including the worst realistic outcome (the worst result that could really happen).
  4. Decide how to mitigate that risk.
  5. Write a plan B (a backup plan) for the case where everything goes wrong.
  6. Estimate how long it would take to return to your current situation if the plan fails.

Test a goal with a worst-case analysis (a deliberate check of the worst result) in this order:

  1. Define the upside if the goal is reached.
  2. Define the downside if it is not pursued, such as illness, job loss, or a falling value of an asset like a house.
  3. Define the worst-case scenario and decide which concrete action would prevent or reduce that downside.

Compare the Status Quo

The status quo means the way things are now.

  • Staying in a stable job is also a risk.
  • Raises are often small, about 3-8% per year, while the cost of living rises by a similar amount.
  • Compare the long-term path of the status quo against the proposed change.
  • Do not compare only the risk of the change.
  • Treating a fully packaged, zero-effort option (a ready-made deal that needs no work from you) as risk-free ignores its costs.
  • Heavily guided arrangements (programs that guide every step) can cost several times more.
  • They also remove flexibility, which is a loss of its own.

Plan B and Fallbacks

Plan B Recovery Estimate

  • A plan B should answer one question: how long would it take to get back to your current life if everything fails?
  • Typical recovery steps include finding another job, rebuilding savings (saving money again), and replacing essentials (things you must have to live).
  • The answer is often much less severe than people fear.
  • Making this estimate turns a vague fear into a measurable risk.

Concrete Fallback Example

A realistic fallback (a backup you could actually live with) for a failed life change can include:

  • Moving back in with family
  • Taking a lower-paid job
  • Buying a cheaper car
  • Owning fewer clothes

Listing these concrete downgrades (cutbacks) before committing shows whether the worst case is actually survivable.

Continuity Plan

  • Always have a continuity plan (a plan that keeps you going) with a second destination.
  • Use the second destination in case the first one becomes unlivable.
  • Without it, a sudden problem forces an unplanned move.
  • Moving to one destination with no backup plan led to a rushed relocation when the air quality became unlivable.
  • Plan the fallback before leaving, not on the day it is needed.

Backup Sales Channels

  • Account suspensions (a platform blocking your account) and lost sales can be demoralizing (discouraging) for a one-person business.
  • Plan backup sales channels (other places to sell) in advance.
  • Then one blocked account does not stop revenue (the money coming in).

Time-Boxing and Exit Rules

Time-Boxing Uncertain Bets

  • Uncertain bets, such as launching a new content channel, should be time-boxed (given a fixed time limit) with a known fallback.
  • For example, committing two months of full-time effort to a channel with no proven audience is acceptable.
  • It is acceptable only if the outcome of failure is survivable and understood in advance.
  • Do not commit too heavily to one campaign type (one kind of Google ad setup).
  • Google can phase it out, which means stop offering it over time.
  • Keep your work flexible enough to move to another campaign type when one disappears.

Cutting Losses

  • If a venture keeps losing money despite major investment, exit it early (stop and leave) and take the lesson.
  • Do not keep funding it out of hope.
  • Cutting losses sooner usually protects the rest of your cash and your core business (the main business you run).

Declining Niches

  • Set aside profits as they come in instead of reinvesting everything.
  • This matters most for products in a niche (a small, focused market) that may fade.
  • Avoid heavy stock purchases (buying a lot of inventory) for a niche that is showing signs of decline.
  • Track the active ad count (how many ads run for a niche) over time.
  • Use it to see if buyers still care.
  • Emotional or problem-driven products can hold demand.
  • People's interest in a problem can shift quickly.

Liability and Legal Risk

Liability Brainstorm

Liability means legal responsibility when something goes wrong.

  • Hold liability brainstorm sessions (short sessions to list ideas freely).
  • List 100 potential ways your business could be liable.
  • Then solve the ones that can be fixed with one email.
  • This principle draws on the idea that only the paranoid survive.

Litigation Pattern Check

Litigation means lawsuits.

  • Use ChatGPT (an AI chat tool) to identify the most common litigation patterns (types of lawsuits that keep happening) and risks in your industry.
  • Common litigation fixes can prevent major legal issues before they occur.

Quitting a Job and Leaving Security

  • Before quitting a full-time job, check that the online business can be sustained as a side business.
  • Only then resign.
  • Company perks such as a car, pension (retirement money paid by the employer), and high salary can make staying feel easier than leaving.
  • Plan the exit deliberately.

Stretching the Comfort Zone

  • Keep stretching your comfort zone (the area where things feel safe) after the early hurdles are mastered.
  • Early hurdles include calling suppliers (companies that sell you products).
  • Operators (people who run the business) who stop pushing themselves tend to stall in growth.
