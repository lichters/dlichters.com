Playbook › 22. Mindset and planning

Build the goal ladder and financial targets

Section 22.9 · 9 of 26 in this chapter · 11 min read

In short

  • Pick your goal first. Then work out how much time and money it needs.
  • Plan backwards: a 10-year picture, then a 3-year goal, then a 1-year goal. Each smaller goal must move toward the bigger one.
  • Make each goal measurable: a date, a profit number, and small daily or weekly steps you can count.
  • Check your goals often and raise them when you reach them. Never treat a goal as fixed.
This section has 10 parts. Tap one to open it.

Define your goals before you expect results

1 min
  • Define your own goal before you expect results. The time you need depends on the goal.
  • A side income that supplements (adds to) a main income is a much smaller target than building a large business.
    • Revenue means all the money your sales bring in, before any costs are taken out.
    • A large business can have millions in annual revenue per year.
  • Set two goals:
    1. Set a financial goal for the business.
    2. Set a personal goal for life outside the business.
    3. Use both goals to guide decisions about the store and how much time to invest.
  • Start thinking about next year's goals before the year begins. Set the yearly plan in advance by breaking long-term goals into steps.

Write a vision and build the goal ladder backwards

2 min
  • Start business planning with a written vision document. This is a written picture of the business in 10 years.
    • Derive the 3-year and 1-year goals from it.
    • Record them in a tracking document.
  • Check the 3-year and 1-year goals against the 10-year vision. They can change as you make progress.
  • A 10-year vision is a destination. Planning every step to it in detail far in advance is unrealistic, because the online business space changes quickly.
    • Break the path into 3-year and 1-year milestones (checkpoints).
    • Smaller plans are easier to make, so strategy and tactics are easier to plan.
  • Build the roadmap (a plan of steps over time) with three horizons (time ranges):
    • a 10-year vision;
    • a 3-year goal;
    • a 1-year goal.
    • Plan the 3-year and 1-year goals in less detail than the 10-year vision.
  • For each horizon (10 years, 3 years, 1 year), define four main elements:
    • a revenue goal;
    • a profit goal, stated either as a dollar amount or as a margin percentage.
      • Profit is what is left after all costs.
      • Margin is profit as a percent of revenue.
    • The business measurables (numbers you track to see progress, also called KPIs, or key performance indicators) needed to reach those numbers;
    • a short, one-paragraph description of what the business looks like at that point.
  • Plan backwards from the 10-year vision:
    1. Decide the 3-year revenue goal, the profit goal, the measurables and a short business description, based on where you think you should be. It is acceptable to be experimental.
    2. Split the 10-year revenue target into roughly equal steps. For example, 10 million dollars in 10 years can be reached through 3 million dollars in 3 years if growth is linear (adds the same amount each step).
    3. Repeat the same questions for the 1-year goal, working back from the 3-year goal. Growth does not need to be linear, but the steps help set a realistic path.
  • If you fill in the 10-year vision, 3-year goal and 1-year goal without working backwards from the 10-year target, the numbers may be unrelated. Each shorter-term goal should clearly move toward the longer-term one.
  • Source A sets business goals on four horizons: 10-year, 3-year, 12-month and one-month. His personal life planning runs on a parallel track of 10-year, 3-year and 12-year plans.
    • The source reads "12-year" for the personal track. Given the 12-month horizon above, this is most likely a typo for 12-month.
  • Source D uses a 90-day horizon for the main goal and a 12-month horizon for the long-term vision when setting personal targets for an ecommerce business (selling products online).
  • The sources differ on horizon length. Use the 10/3/1-year ladder for long-range business planning. Use the 90-day main goal with a 12-month vision for near-term personal targets.

Set revenue and profit targets

1 min
  • Revenue and profit targets are a starting point. Expect to revise them, because the link between revenue and profit is not known at the beginning. Set them early anyway, since they give direction to work backwards from.
  • Define financial targets for the business:
    1. Add to your written vision a specific revenue target and a specific profit target for the store.
    2. If you plan to exit (sell) the business, write down a specific target exit payout (the money you receive when you sell). For example, 50,000, 100,000, 500,000, 1,000,000 or 5,000,000 dollars.
    3. Research the typical sale multiples (how many times a yearly figure a business sells for) that dropshipping businesses are currently sold for. Dropshipping means you sell products you do not hold in stock; the supplier ships them to the customer. Use other websites or community groups for this.
    4. Work backwards from the target exit payout and the multiple to a required revenue or profit goal.
    5. Estimate how long reaching these goals would take, and write down the ideal outcome.
  • A six-figure sum is not necessarily permanent life-changing money. It may not earn enough interest to live from. Set targets high enough to allow lasting financial independence.
  • Source D describes the first milestone as a benchmark (a standard to compare with). It is the difference between students who waste six months and students who reach their first 10,000 dollars per month in revenue.
  • Before you call the first roadmap complete, check these end results:
    1. A fully launched branded dropshipping store.
    2. A validated product or system (proven to work) generating consistent sales.
    3. The knowledge to scale beyond 10,000 dollars per month toward six figures.

Make goals specific and measurable

2 min
  • Write goals with a specific target date and a specific profit figure. Vague aims, such as having a successful store or making a sale, cannot be measured and are easy to abandon.
  • Write a concrete income target and a 12-month vision. Vague goals, such as wanting more money, are hard to track and easy to abandon.
  • Turn a goal into an executable plan (a plan you can start today) in this order:
    1. Set a specific, realistic target date. For a beginner, about two months ahead.
    2. Set a concrete profit target. For a beginner, 3,000 USD profit.
    3. Write the specific actions needed to reach that target, not only the wish.
    4. Break each action into a countable number with a deadline. For example, 5 supplier approvals obtained by calling 10 suppliers.
    5. Set daily or weekly activity targets for each action. For example, calling suppliers every day for two weeks.
    6. Track each count. Add accountability (someone else who checks your progress), such as sharing progress with someone, so that progress stays visible.
  • Specific steps combined with accountability make a goal more likely to be achieved. The more concrete the actions and the more they are committed to, the higher the chance of success.
  • Set a few measurable business goals at the start of the year. Examples: a fixed percentage increase in net profit (profit after all costs), or a monthly profit figure.
    • Write them down with a check-in date (a planned review day).
    • At each check-in, record which changes moved the number and which did not. The next decision is then based on results, not repetition.
  • Detailed, specific goals are more likely to be reached than vague ones. They should be visible to both the owner and the team.
    • A short-term target, such as 10 sales in 5 days, can be reached by the end of a month.
  • Track these metrics and turn them into measurable targets with rewards:
    • Conversion rate means the share of website visitors who buy.
    • Organic search traffic means visitors who arrive from search results without paying for ads.
    • Phone call close ratio means the share of calls that end in a sale.
    • Supplier approval means a supplier agrees to work with you.
Metric Example target
Daily sales 3 sales per day
Monthly sales $10,000 in one month
Conversion rate 2 buyers per 100 website visitors (2%)
Organic search traffic A set number of visitors from search
Phone call close ratio 5 sales from 10 calls (50%)
Supplier approvals Reaching a gold-level supplier status
  • Define success for each store upfront, such as a monthly net profit goal over a set timeframe. A store that earns a smaller but steady net profit with minimal daily work can still be a success.

Sequence growth and expansion

1 min
  • Growth should come from keeping the store focused and expanding in steps. Do not launch many products or stores at once. Check each step with traffic (visitors) and sales before you move on.
  • Speed and impatience are different things. Move fast on doing the work. Plan long-term goals in a big-picture view. A goal set for one to three years can be broken into a three-month plan.
  • When results are steady, growth requires taking larger risks. A break is fine, but after it, set a bigger goal.
  • Set a clear next goal before starting a new project, such as reaching 5,000 USD profit per month or selling the existing store. This prevents coasting after early success.

Plan monthly and daily actions

1 min
  • Break a yearly revenue goal into monthly goals. Track the current month's goal on a checklist, such as a Trello board (an online task board with columns). Review progress monthly so you can spot where you are stuck early.
  • Set an internal deadline a few days before your real goal date. Then delays do not push the real goal back.
  • Use the goal tracking horizons below. Source A's tracking structure uses shorter horizons than his business ladder.
    • The dreamline is a list of your big life dreams.

Use the goal tracker

2 min
  • Fill in the goal tracker in this order:
    1. Write the vision document describing the business in 10 years.
    2. Enter the 10-year picture in the tracker: date, revenue, profit, measurables and a brief overview taken from the vision document.
    3. Enter the 3-year picture, working backwards from the 10-year figures: date, revenue, profit, measurables and a brief overview.
    4. Enter the 1-year plan, working backwards from the 3-year figures: date, revenue, profit, the same measurables and a brief overview.
    5. Review the three horizons together. Check that each shorter-term figure clearly moves toward the longer-term one.
  • Fill in the 10-year picture of the tracker:
    1. Enter the date that is 10 years from today.
    2. Enter the revenue target in dollars, for example 10 million dollars per year.
    3. Enter the profit goal either as a dollar amount or as a margin percentage.
    4. Enter the business measurables you expect at that point. Examples: revenue, profit, customer numbers, cost per acquisition (money spent to win one customer), repeat business rate (share of customers who buy again), average order value (average amount per order) and number of products sold.
    5. Paste in the overview of the business from the first paragraph of your vision document. Keep it brief, not the full plan.
  • Fill in the 3-year picture of the tracker:
    1. Enter the date that is 3 years from today.
    2. Enter the revenue target, for example 3 million dollars.
    3. Enter the profit goal as a dollar amount or margin percentage.
    4. Define the measurables you need to track. Make each one specific and clearly defined.
    5. Write a brief overview of what the business looks like at that point. Cover the highlights, such as team size. Example: a team of 20 people in one year could mean a team of 10 staff within a few years.
  • Fill in the 1-year plan of the tracker:
    1. Enter the date that is one year from today.
    2. Enter the revenue target for the year, for example 1 million dollars.
    3. Enter the profit goal for the year, for example 300,000 dollars, or a margin percentage.
    4. Use the same measurables as in the 3-year picture, since this is generally easiest. Set the figures for the one-year horizon, for example 1,000 customers per year.
    5. Write a brief overview of what the business should look like one year from now.

Set personal life goals

1 min
  • Track progress against lifestyle outcomes, such as reaching a home, a trip or a car, rather than only against profit. Concrete targets motivate far more than a monthly profit figure. Without a known amount and purpose, results tend to be random.
  • The sources frame profit differently. Source A's personal track uses lifestyle targets. The store-level figures above are profit targets. Use both:
    • The store goal governs business decisions;
    • The lifestyle goal governs how much time you invest.
  • Stretch goals (goals a bit bigger than what you do now) gradually. Aim somewhat beyond what you currently believe is possible, but not so far that it sets you up for failure. Expand your comfort zone one step at a time, so larger goals become reachable over time.
  • Set goals slightly bigger than what seems possible, but realistic enough to be reached. Define the final destination before planning the path toward it.

Review, share and raise goals

1 min
  • At the end of each month, sit down with the team and follow these steps:
    1. Review financial goals.
    2. Review operational goals, including return rates and the number of customer complaints.
    3. Set specific, detailed targets for the next period, including small short-term ones.
  • Share the goals tracker with the team as the business grows. Employees who understand and buy into the vision tend to work toward it more effectively. If the revenue and goal figures feel uncomfortable to share, that is usually a sign the goals are ambitious enough.
  • Goals are living documents. Review and update them as the business grows. If you reach a 3-year goal early, raise it, and revise the 10-year vision upward as well.
  • Missing a goal is acceptable when goals are continually raised, as long as the chase keeps pushing the business forward. Goals do not have to stay fixed once set.
  • Success is not final. After reaching a goal, reevaluate it, set new long-term targets and keep improving your procedures. Failure is not permanent either. Treat it as a lesson to apply the next time.
