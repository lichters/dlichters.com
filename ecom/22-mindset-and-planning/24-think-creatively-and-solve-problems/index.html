Playbook › 22. Mindset and planning

Think creatively and solve problems

Section 22.24 · 24 of 26 in this chapter · 18 min read

In short

  • Find the real cause of a problem before you fix anything else.
  • Change one thing at a time, so you know what actually worked.
  • Ask AI for help first, then the community or a coach.
  • Research deeply before you write any ad.
  • Test every idea with small spending before you spend big.
More key points
  • Come up with ideas by hand first. Use the computer later, when the thinking is mostly done.
  • Keep two places: one with no electronics, and one with your computer.
  • When stuck, leave the computer and do something else for a while.
  • Keep a daily routine. It matters more than finding long free blocks of time.

Diagnosing a problem before fixing it

2 min

  • Find the root cause first: the real reason a problem happens.

  • Do this before you work on side issues, such as page layout or copy (the written sales text on your pages).

  • If the root cause stays unsolved, changes to other parts of the business bring little benefit.

  • Treat a business problem like a blocked stream: something is stuck in the flow.

  • Find what blocks the flow, remove that block, and the rest of the business starts working.

  • Decide which other changes are worth making only after that.

  • Changing five or more things at once hides which change fixed the problem.

  • It often leaves the real cause in place.

  • Test the most likely root cause first.

  • When a plan does not work, adjust it.

  • Address the problem directly and push through.

  • Do not hold on to the original plan.

  • When a store is struggling, take responsibility for the cause, such as weak ads or too few suppliers (the companies that provide your products).

  • Do not blame outside factors.

  • Fixing the causes you can control is the main lever (the strongest way to change results).

  • Successful entrepreneurs do not panic when a problem appears.

  • They stay calm, break the problem into steps and solve them one by one.

  • Long hours do not automatically produce results.

  • What counts is working on the right tasks.

  • If effort brings no progress, check whether the problem lies in product choice, ad angle (the main idea your ad uses to sell) or offer (what you give the buyer, such as a price or deal), not in how hard you work.

  • Limit the time you spend on a single problem.

  • Keep working on the business at the same time.

  • Spending hours each day on a payment dispute (an argument over a payment) reduced the time and focus left for the product and the company.

  • A common beginner mistake is panicking when something breaks.

  • Then you send many messages to coaches (paid teachers) or the community (the online group of members) and wait for replies while doing nothing.

  • Asking AI (artificial intelligence, a computer helper that answers questions) first and trying to solve the problem yourself avoids this loss of time.

Handling a problem in your business:

  1. Describe the problem to AI. Include the platform (the software your store runs on), the theme (the design template of your store), the exact symptom (what you see) and any numbers.
  2. Try to understand the answer. Apply the suggested fix.
  3. If you are still stuck, post your question in the community or ask a coach. Say what you already tried.

This order builds independence and speeds up your growth.

Example: AI gave a reset plan for a stalled store (a store that has stopped selling). It covers 3 days:

  • Launch 1 new product per day.
  • Test at least 3-5 creatives per product. Creatives are the ad images and videos you show.
  • Spend small amounts only to collect data (facts about what buyers do).

Stop, or "kill," products quickly when they show no traction (no real sign that buyers want them).

Using AI as a thinking partner

1 min

  • Talking through a problem in writing with AI forces you to organise your thoughts.

  • It reveals the bigger picture.

  • Use AI to clarify decisions before you act on them.

  • AI can help with personal problems too, such as low energy or demotivation (losing drive) at work.

  • Describing the situation to it gives you an outside view you may not see yourself.

  • Use AI tools only at named steps where they measurably shorten the task.

  • For each step, state when AI should be used.

  • Using AI on every step without that decision adds effort but does not save time.

  • Long AI chats drift (slowly change direction) and forget earlier brand instructions.

  • Keep one restartable thread that holds the primer (the starting instructions) and the prompts (the requests you send).

  • Re-prime it (send the starting instructions again) when the output stops matching the brand.

  • Use Google Gemini instead of ChatGPT (another AI chatbot) to review policy pages for Google Ads (Google's paid ad system) and Merchant Center (Google's tool for product listings) compliance (following the rules).

  • Gemini is Google's own AI model.

  • It is the better fit for checking content against Google's own policy rules.

Challenging AI recommendations

1 min

  • Challenge AI-generated SEO (search engine optimization, the work of getting found on Google) and strategy recommendations before you act on them.

  • This matters most for big decisions about content direction.

  • Ask for the strongest argument for and against each suggestion. Expect to drop some items from the list.

  • This matters most for premium brands (high-price, high-quality brands), where AI-written content can drift toward a cheap tone.

  • Use this prompt to test a recommendation. A steelman argument means the strongest honest version of an argument.

Before we act on this recommendation, give me the strongest steelman argument for doing it and the strongest steelman argument against it.
Then give an honest verdict: is this necessary for our goal right now, and which items from the list would you cut?
Flag anything that does not fit our brand positioning, price level or target customer.
Keep the verdict short and tie every action to either a new visitor or a new customer.
  • The same AI audit (a review of your store) can give a different priority order each time you run it.
  • Treat its ranking as a starting point, not a fixed plan.
  • Ask whether each suggestion will actually move traffic (visitors) or revenue (money from sales) in the next weeks.

Approaching every new problem with fresh thinking

1 min

  • Every new product, market and advertisement is a new problem.

  • Solutions that worked before cannot be reused without copying.

  • Success should push you to search again, not to reopen old files.

  • Analysis is a method of asking the right questions.

  • It means making rough guesses from past successes, setting checkpoints (review points) and letting the problem dictate the answer.

  • It can be learned the way you learn grammar.

  • Repeat it for each new product and market, because fixed rules go stale quickly.

  • The right approach is found through study and testing, and sometimes by accident.

  • Expect to try many versions before one works.

  • Keep learning from the results.

  • You do not need to be a creative genius to start.

  • What you need is to recognize what already works and copy its model.

Research before writing

2 min

  • Good advertising results from deep research.

  • Expert writers (copywriters) often spend weeks reading and testing before they write a single ad.

  • Hard work and preparation separate successful ads from guesses.

  • Motivation research helps the copywriter discover hidden desires.

  • It reveals the taboos (things people will not say openly) that limit how desires can be expressed.

  • It shows the splits within a market (groups that want different things).

  • It shows early reactions to the wording of a headline (the big title line).

  • A research finding is a direction, not a headline.

  • It tells you where not to go and the general area of the solution.

  • Before you script any ad (write its words and scenes), the seller must know the product inside out.

  • That includes its features (what it is and does), the benefits they create, and which goal or pain (problem that hurts) each benefit addresses.

  • Without that product knowledge, do not write a script.

  • Before scripting, the seller must also understand the target audience's wants, needs and pain points.

  • Also understand where they want to go and what they want to escape from.

  • The ad must make the viewer feel the seller understands them and is on their side.

  • The method described works for problem-solving products, which is most products.

  • With these products, the buyer is trying to get from point A to point B, reach a goal or escape pain.

  • Customers come to an e-commerce store (an online shop) because they want to solve a problem.

  • That problem is the real reason they buy.

  • Copy should start from the buyer's problem, not from the product's features.

  • Procedure for testing a concept before scaling (spending more):

  1. Run a deep-dive research (a very detailed study) on Amazon reviews and Reddit threads (online discussions) for your niche (a small, focused market). Fill in the research form: pain points, exact customer language, USP (unique selling proposition, what makes your product different) and ICP (ideal customer profile, a description of your best buyer).
  2. Write one clear main message per concept (idea), built from what real users said.
  3. Build one or two concepts with 3-5 creatives each. Use statics (still images) if you have not yet reached the proof-of-concept gate (a checkpoint that proves an idea works). Use VSL (video sales letter, a long sales video) once you hit $75-$100 per day.
  4. Launch in a testing CBO (CBO means campaign budget optimization, one budget for the whole campaign that the ad platform shares out by itself). Run it for 3 days. Then read website purchases and ROAS (return on ad spend, the money earned for each dollar spent on ads).
  5. Fold the winners into your main CBO.
  • In e-commerce, a store does not need the best copy in the world to win.
  • Beating most competitors' copy is enough to gain an edge, since many online stores have weak copy.

Planning an ad before writing

3 min

  • Planning an ad can take weeks or months.

  • The result may be only five or ten words.

  • Those words carry most of the ad's value. If they are right, the ad can work. If they are wrong, nothing written after them will save it.

  • Spend the time on the idea before you write the body copy (the main text).

  • The unique idea for a product lies in the one element that makes its product and market different.

  • Find that element through research into the market and study of the product.

  • Do not borrow a formula.

  • Advertising is not about entertaining or pleasing the creator.

  • It is about the buyer's interests.

  • Always write from the viewpoint of the typical buyer.

  • Think of one real person who is likely to buy, not the mass audience.

  • Advertisers who sell to the buyer's interests, not the seller's, succeed far more often.

  • The seller's view tends to focus on the product rather than the buyer's needs.

  • Imagine the buyer's face-to-face reaction to every line.

  • Give each letter (sales letter) one specific goal.

  • Make every sentence move the reader one step nearer to it.

  • If a sentence does not support the goal, remove it.

  • Learn the rules as a checklist until they become second nature (automatic).

  • Then write from the feel of the message, not from the checklist.

  • Mechanical rules build the letter, but the idea behind it makes it work.

Procedure to plan a new ad before writing:

  1. State the problem in plain words. For example, you want to sell this product.
  2. Restate the problem in a different way. For example, you want to introduce the unique aspects of the product to the prospect (a possible buyer).
  3. Restate it again, describing the buyer's experience. For example, the product should be easy to buy and seem fun.
  4. Choose the restatement that feels closest to the ad you want to write.
  5. List several big ideas or concepts for that restatement. Then pick the one or two that make the most sense.
  6. Visualize how the concept fits into the ad. Check it against the restatement.
  7. Stop and incubate (let the idea grow in the background while you do something else).
  8. Write the headline, then the subheadline (the smaller line under the headline), then the first sentence.
  9. Block-diagram the finished ad (sketch its parts as boxes) to check its flow.

  • Persuasion principles: Cialdini's six principles of persuasion (from the psychologist Robert Cialdini) are reciprocity, scarcity/urgency/FOMO (fear of missing out), authority, social proof, commitment and consistency, and liking.

  • Getting these right makes an ad strongly persuasive.

  • Reciprocity (the urge to pay back a gift): giving value first makes people more likely to buy when the offer arrives.

  • Examples: free ebooks in exchange for an email, free content, or value delivered up front.

  • Authority: people trust experts and people with an air of power.

  • Being featured in a magazine or endorsed (backed) by a doctor increases persuasion.

  • The audience assumes they know what they are talking about.

  • Commitment and consistency: people want to stay consistent with their identity (their sense of who they are).

  • Asking for small commitments is easier than asking for a big sale up front, which can overwhelm people.

  • Example path: an email signup, then a small purchase, then a larger one.

  • Liking is built from three parts: similarity, genuine compliments and cooperation.

  • Similarity signals safety to the viewer. The brand should show it was founded by people like the audience.

  • Compliments must be genuine.

  • Cooperation means positioning the brand as a helper that moves the customer toward their goal, not as an obstacle.

  • Value proposition formula (Hormozi's value equation, from the entrepreneur Alex Hormozi):

  • Value = dream outcome × perceived likelihood of achievement, divided by time delay and effort and sacrifice.

  • Dream outcome means the result the buyer wants most. Perceived likelihood means how likely they think they are to get it.

  • Increase the buyer's trust in reaching the outcome. Then reduce time, effort and sacrifice, so the product seems quick, easy and costless.

Incubation: letting ideas form

2 min
  • Problem solving through incubation:
  1. Think intensely about the problem and gather all the relevant information, including the possible options.
  2. Define the desired end result clearly.
  3. Stop working on the problem. Put off the decision until later, dismissing it from your conscious mind (the part of the mind you are aware of).
  4. Before sleep, tell yourself you want the answer or idea by morning. Keep a notepad by the bed.
  5. Write down any idea or solution as soon as you wake, before it fades.

  • Creative solutions and insights often appear when the conscious mind is relaxed or busy with something else, such as a routine activity or a walk.

  • Continuing to struggle and worry during that time usually delays the solution.

  • Forcing a solution with strained effort tends to jam the automatic problem-solving process.

  • It then produces anxiety instead of answers.

  • Trust the process and act on the best available information instead.

  • Incubation procedure for copy:

  1. Gather all material about the product and the customer.
  2. Think through what you have read. Optionally, jot down (write quickly) headlines, key points, the product's nature and the strongest reasons customers would buy.
  3. Stop working on the project. Do something pleasant and unrelated, without thinking about the copy.
  4. When an idea appears, write it down at once.
  5. Write a first draft from the material your mind has organized. Do not correct spelling or grammar yet.

  • Incubation runs in the background even when you do not work on the project.

  • Thinking about the project again interrupts it and weakens the result.

  • Give the project real breaks rather than forcing continuous effort.

  • Incubation speed and quality depend on time, creative orientation, environment and ego (your pride and self-image).

  • Some pressure, such as deadlines or pride in the result, speeds up the process.

  • Too little pressure slows it down.

  • Keep the creative team separate from routine operations.

  • Copywriters need freedom to incubate.

  • Their output drops when they are held to the same visible schedule as operational staff (the people who run daily work).

Lateral thinking

1 min
  • Lateral thinking (solving by connecting unrelated things) exercise for a stuck ad concept:
  1. Pick a random source of words, such as a shuffled set of words or a dictionary.
  2. Pull out the first three words you see.
  3. Force a connection between those three words and the product you are selling.
  4. Build an ad concept from the connection, keeping the selling goal in mind.
  • When solving a marketing problem, step outside the original frame (the way you first see the problem).
  • Do this by relating it to something unrelated.
  • A solution that looks impossible inside the problem often appears when the problem is connected to a different context.

Constraints and assumed limits

1 min

  • Limitations force focused decisions.

  • Setting a firm constraint (a hard limit) on time, budget, materials or scope is a practical way to beat creative block (being stuck).

  • Waiting for unlimited resources usually stops the work entirely.

  • Tight limits make creative choices easier. Examples: one color for a product ad, a 60-second maximum for a video script, a fixed 100 USD test budget for a campaign, or a set vocabulary for a product description.

  • Open-ended options often cause paralysis (being unable to act).

  • A hard boundary forces a decision.

  • Test assumed constraints instead of accepting them. Assumed constraints block good ideas.

  • Common self-imposed limits include believing a product cannot sell at a high price or through mail (selling by post), or that a certain audience will not respond.

  • Test these limits. Do not accept them.

  • Disproved limits: a $190,000 limit on a used airplane was beaten by a $240,000 sale in 10 days. A $600 product sold more than 3,000 units through mail order.

  • Experiment freely with low-stakes projects (small risks) before you have public attention.

  • Attention brings expectations that limit experimentation.

  • Use the early period to test ideas without worrying about audience reactions or revenue pressure.

Practice, daily output and drafting

2 min

  • Consistent small output compounds (adds up over time).

  • Writing one page per day adds up to about 365 pages in a year, enough for a full novel.

  • Measure progress by daily output over months, not by single big wins.

  • Practice produces the largest gains in copywriting.

  • Write every day in small forms such as letters, postcards, articles and ads.

  • Volume builds skill faster than study alone.

  • Accept that a first draft will be weak.

  • Judge the draft by its emotional release (the feeling it lets out), not by how it reads.

  • The real quality comes from editing.

  • Draft with the intuitive, emotional side of the mind. Then edit with the analytical side.

  • Ignore spelling and grammar while drafting.

  • Switch to logic when editing.

  • In editing, refine copy so it expresses exactly the intended meaning with the fewest words.

  • You may rearrange sentences, cut low-value words, substitute stronger words or add words for clarity.

  • Keep the same emotional feel.

  • Waiting to know who you are or what your brand stands for before you start producing keeps you stuck in planning.

  • Identity and style usually emerge from doing the work regularly.

  • Base your creative output on subjects that genuinely interest you, not only on what you already know.

  • Work drawn only from familiar experience tends to be dull.

  • In e-commerce, this means choosing products, angles and offers you would actually want to buy or use yourself.

  • Side projects and experiments often produce the best results.

  • Keep several projects running and switch between them when one stalls (stops moving).

  • Returning to a paused project later is a productive way to deal with a lack of motivation.

  • When you are unsure what to do next, ask which option would make a better story for your work or career.

  • This question helps you choose a direction that is interesting and worth pursuing.

  • Staying relevant after a viral win (a post or ad that spreads widely) is the hardest part.

  • You need to produce many new video ideas every day to keep views steady.

Idea capture

1 min
  • Idea capture method:
  1. When a new idea appears during work, write it down in the Notes column (a notes field in your tracking sheet) or as a card. Do not leave the current tracking screen.
  2. Return to the current task right away.
  3. Review the Notes panels regularly so recorded ideas are not lost.

  • Writing down an idea removes it from working memory (the mind's short-term holding space).

  • Focus can then return to the current task without losing the idea.

  • Ideas that are not recorded are often forgotten by the end of the day.

  • Feeling stuck or blank when brainstorming is normal.

  • It does not mean you lack the ability to find a market.

  • Ideas often arrive later, when you are not forcing them. Keep the list open and add ideas as they come.

Influences and reference collecting

1 min

  • Almost every new idea is a combination of earlier ideas.

  • Original work comes from recombining existing influences, not from starting from nothing.

  • Treating influence as normal, not as a weakness, frees you to build on what already works.

  • The quality of your reference inputs (what you collect to learn from) caps the quality of your output.

  • Collect selectively. Keep only sources that genuinely appeal to you.

  • Indiscriminate (random) collecting dilutes your taste and makes your work average.

  • Influence tree procedure:

  1. Pick one creator or brand you admire. Study everything about their work.
  2. Identify three people or brands that this creator was influenced by. Study them the same way.
  3. Repeat this chain as far back as you can go, building a tree of influences.
  4. Start your own work as a new branch that grows from that tree.
  • Copy-and-amplify procedure:
  1. Copy the work of the creators you admire closely enough to understand how it is built.
  2. Note exactly where your version falls short of the original.
  3. Amplify (make bigger) those differences, since they show what you add that is unique.
  4. Develop that difference into your own product, ad or content.

  • Experience and broad knowledge give the copywriter more parts to combine into new ideas.

  • Curiosity, varied hobbies (activities you do for fun), travel and learning new skills all add material.

  • That material later becomes an unexpected angle.

  • Once you truly absorb (understand deeply) the no-brand-loyalty principle (buyers do not stay loyal to one brand), product opportunities become visible everywhere.

  • Look in shopping malls, home interiors, magazines and television.

  • This speeds up your ability to find viable (workable) dropshipping niches. Dropshipping means selling products that a supplier ships for you, so you do not hold stock.

  • Copying what already works is a legitimate starting point.

  • Recognize the model behind a successful product, ad or store, and adapt it.

  • Then build your difference on top of it.

Working environment: analog and digital

1 min
  • Analog means done by hand, with no electronics.
  • Computers are strong for editing and publishing. They are weak for generating ideas (coming up with new thoughts). Deleting and perfecting starts too early.
  • Generate ideas by hand or in a sketchbook first. Bring in the computer only when the thinking is mostly done.
  • Set up two separate workstations (a workstation is a place set up for one kind of work):
  1. Set up two separate workstations: an analog station (no electronics) and a digital station (with your computer, scanner and other devices).
  2. Stock the analog station with paper, pens, markers, sticky notes (small paper notes that stick) and index cards (small paper cards). They can be bought for about $10.
  3. Generate and sketch ideas at the analog station. Stand up if possible. Pin ideas on walls to find patterns (a pattern is a link that repeats between ideas).
  4. Move to the digital station to execute (carry out), edit and publish the ideas.
  5. When you lose momentum (the energy to keep going), return to the analog station and keep exploring.

Walks, breaks and stepping away

1 min
  • When stuck on a problem, leave the computer. Take a walk, go to the gym, or do a routine task (a simple task you do the same way each time). Solutions often come when you are not actively searching.
  • Searching online is still useful. Do not replace stepping away with constant thinking about the issue.
  • Schedule unstructured time (time with no goal and no plan). Boredom and routine tasks, such as washing dishes or walking, often trigger new ideas.
  • Keeping a daily routine matters more than finding large blocks of free time.
