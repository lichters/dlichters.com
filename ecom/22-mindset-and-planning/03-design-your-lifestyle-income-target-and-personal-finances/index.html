Playbook › 22. Mindset and planning

Design your lifestyle, income target and personal finances

Section 22.3 · 3 of 26 in this chapter · 16 min read

In short

  • Plan your ideal life with a yearly price tag first. That price is your money target.
  • Count net profit (money left after all costs are paid), not gross sales (total money coming in).
  • Quit your job only after three months in a row of net profit that matches your job's pay.
  • Keep what you earn: save a fixed share first, pay off high-interest debt, then invest slowly.

Why a personal target comes first

3 min
  • Building a business takes steady effort before it earns money.
  • Clear personal goals keep you motivated while you are not paid yet.
  • Dreamlining means planning your ideal life with a price tag on each part of it.
  • Use this routine:
    1. Write down your ideal life: location, home type, vehicle, vacations, lifestyle.
    2. Calculate the yearly cost of that lifestyle.
    3. Keep this document visible while you work.
    4. Refer to these goals when you do difficult, unpaid work.
    5. Remind yourself that you work toward your defined life, not toward hoping the business works.
  • Goals without a cost give the business no money target and no clear way to measure progress.
  • Each goal should have a cost.
  • Define your values and priorities before you cut time or cost.
  • Freedom means something different for each person.
  • Every plan involves trade-offs (giving up one thing to get another):
    • free time versus maximum money
    • more stuff versus less stuff
    • spending versus investing
    • stability versus uncertainty
  • Answer these in writing. Do not copy what others do:
    1. Decide what financial freedom means to you: a large comfortable house, a small studio, or something in between.
    2. Decide how much luxury you need in cars, hotels and possessions.
    3. Decide whether you value location independence (working from anywhere) or a stable home base with room for guests and family.
    4. Decide whether you want more free time or the maximum possible income.
    5. Decide whether money should go to experiences now or to investments for later.
  • A billable career (you earn only when you work hours) is capped by available hours.
  • Profit above your current income is what lets you grow beyond that cap.
  • The first goal is to replace the income from your current job.
  • Source A used 100,000 dollars per month as a goal.
  • He tied it to a life decision, such as a partner leaving a job or starting a family.
  • Set income targets as trigger points (the moment you act) for life decisions, not as fixed dates.
  • Most drop shippers (sellers who ship products directly from suppliers) face money problems sooner or later.
  • People usually fix financial control only after a problem forces them to. That costs more than planning ahead.
  • Know your numbers. This lets you step out of daily operations and reach higher levels of success.
  • Without this knowledge you may reach some levels but stay stuck in daily firefighting (fixing one urgent problem after another).
  • Write down your money questions as homework:
    1. Set aside about five minutes.
    2. Write down every question or problem you have about money and the numbers in your business.
    3. Mark which common questions apply to you: product profitability, sales profitability, where money goes, supplier debt (money you owe suppliers), hiring affordability, and selling the business.
    4. Add any other financial questions or problems you need to solve.
    5. Keep the list and use it to guide your next steps in financial learning.
  • Test your knowledge: try to answer your key money questions within about ten minutes of looking at your records.
  • If you cannot, you have a knowledge gap (something you do not yet know) to close before you scale (grow) or sell.
  • Set your first goal around controlling your time and earning enough to cover current household costs. Do not aim for extreme wealth yet.
  • A realistic income target that supports your current lifestyle is a sound starting point.
  • Every growing dropshipping store meets the same bookkeeping questions (tracking where money goes). Examples: where the profit went, or why the bank balance is unexpectedly high.
  • Meeting them is normal. It does not mean you are doing something wrong.

Dreamlining and the cost form

2 min
  • Dreamlining turns your ideal life into a money target. Follow these nine steps:
    1. Decide the business type: a lifestyle business (freedom to work from anywhere) or a corporate-style company with employees and many stores.
    2. Define where you want to live: city, state or region, and country.
    3. Decide whether you want to rent or own, and whether you prefer a house or a condo (an apartment you own).
    4. Define your car: the specific model, its total price, and its monthly payments.
    5. Define vacations: how many per year, how long each one lasts, and where you go.
    6. List other lifestyle costs, such as dining out, a winter location, a personal trainer, or a chef.
    7. Note any debt you want to pay down each month, or the amount you want to save each month.
    8. Price every item with exact figures. Then you know the total cost of your ideal life.
    9. Calculate how much you must earn to fund that life. Then derive (work out) your goals from that number.
  • A shorter version of the same method:
    1. Write down what you want: where you live, what car you drive, what vacation you take, and what else you want.
    2. Estimate what each item costs.
    3. Add the costs. The total is what the business must earn to pay for these goals.
    4. Use the items as rewards that keep you working toward the target.
  • The 4-Hour Workweek Dreamlining Form is a template for listing personal life goals with their costs. It turns them into a business money target.
  • A downloadable cost form is based on Tim Ferriss's The 4-Hour Work Week. It lets you total the costs of your ideal lifestyle and the income needed to fund it.
  • It replaces guessing with exact figures.
  • Set dreamline targets as a stretch (a goal slightly beyond what you think is possible).
  • Do not go so far that almost nobody achieves them. Donating $5 million to charity within 18 months is unrealistic for nearly anyone.
  • Upgrading to a comfortable apartment within a year is a fair stretch for someone living with parents.
  • Knowing your wants in advance lets you spend your earnings on purpose. Lifestyle items can serve as rewards.
  • Owning a house is not an automatic goal just because income rises. The right choice depends on how much flexibility you value.
  • Before buying an expensive item such as a luxury car, write down:
    • why you want it
    • The exact model
    • its price
    • its monthly payment
  • This prevents purchases you regret once income arrives.
  • Targets can follow different patterns. Examples: one two-week vacation per year, or one one-week trip every quarter (every three months).
  • Choose the pattern and destination. Then price it, so you know the annual travel budget your income must cover.

DEAL model and time-versus-money trade-offs

2 min
  • Apply DEAL in this fixed order. DEAL is a four-step method for simplifying your life.
    1. Define: decide what you actually need from life, what you value, and what your priorities are.
    2. Eliminate: cut every expense, possession, responsibility and daily task you can drop.
    3. Automate: set up a system (a tool or routine that runs by itself) for each task you cannot remove.
    4. Liberate: hand off (outsource) what still needs a human. Do this only once the process is clear and written down.
  • Eliminate in layers, in this order:
    1. expenses
    2. material possessions
    3. responsibilities
    4. daily routines
  • Whatever cannot be made smaller or removed should be automated before it is delegated.
  • Track time freedom as hours per week.
  • Weekly working time fell from over 40 hours to under 20 hours after cutting responsibilities and automating tasks.
  • Money can buy time back in many ways, such as direct flights, ride-hailing (booking a driver through an app), delivery, and cleaning services.
  • Judge each time-saving purchase by comparing its price with the hourly value of your time.
  • Otherwise convenience spending can quietly consume your income.
  • Count money and time together.
  • A casual restaurant meal in a US city such as San Francisco can cost about 20-25 USD with tax and tip.
  • Cooking at home also has hidden time costs: grocery shopping, prep, cooking, cleanup and car errands.
  • Compare both before choosing the cheaper-looking option.
  • Cost versus comfort, with examples:
    • Paying a few hundred dollars for a direct flight instead of a flight with two or three layovers (stops) is often worth it.
    • A private jet at around 20,000 USD per hour is only worth it if your time is valued at that level.
    • Compare the extra cost with the hours saved and the value you place on them.
    • A traveler chose premium economy and paid cash for it instead of business class.
    • The flight stayed comfortable. The large price difference went to investing.
    • Choose a middle option when the top option costs more than the gain in comfort justifies.
  • Every purchase is a trade-off. You can have many things, but not everything at once.
  • Buying several major items can leave no cash for investing or emergencies.
  • Decide on purpose which items deserve the money and time they consume.
  • Financing large purchases on long terms can keep you paying for items long after you stop wanting them. Examples:
    • 72- to 96-month car payments or leases
    • 30- to 40-year mortgages (home loans)
  • Match the loan length to how long you realistically will keep the item.
  • Possessions and downsizing: a single 60-liter carry-on duffel (a soft travel bag) can hold everything a full-time traveler owns, including home, office and wardrobe.
  • That minimalism means giving up items such as golf clubs or many pairs of shoes.
  • Decide individually which possessions are essential before you downsize.

Location arbitrage and slow travel

1 min
  • Location arbitrage means living where costs are lower than where you earn. Living costs vary widely between cities and countries.
  • Moving from a high-cost US city (about $4,000 to $6,000 per month in California) to a low-cost place (about $600 to $1,000 per month in Chiang Mai) can free roughly $3,000 per month for saving and investing.
  • Living costs of about 4,000-5,000 USD per month in a large US city fell to about 1,000-2,000 USD per month in places such as Chiang Mai, Eastern European cities or Prague.
  • This roughly halves spending through location arbitrage.
  • Running a business from anywhere needs structure and routine.
  • Location independence is built on a set daily rhythm, not on freedom alone.
  • Slow travel means staying a long time in one place. Broadening your horizons (learning from new places and people) is the other element.
  • Together with location independence, these two make this lifestyle sustainable.
  • Stability and uncertainty pull against each other. Frequent travelers often later buy a home base.
  • A realistic plan combines slow travel with a stable base. It chooses accommodation for comfort over months, rather than the lowest nightly price.
  • Slow travel example: stay two to three months per location in monthly rentals of decent quality. A good rental has a couch, a TV and a separate bedroom.
  • This reduces logistics (planning and moving) and fatigue, compared with moving every two weeks.
  • It costs more than the cheapest room, but it is chosen for comfort.
  • Living outside the US can make good private medical care affordable.
  • It is often paid in cash, at much lower cost than insurance-based care in the US.
  • Include this when planning a move abroad.

Relocating with family

2 min
  • A move abroad needs a strong personal reason.
  • Copying others who went to the same place is not enough motivation to carry a family through difficulties.
  • Every family member should agree to the move before it starts.
  • Relocating with children is harder when they lose close daily contact with grandparents and relatives.
  • Before deciding, discuss the impact on each family member.
  • Agree how family back home will stay in touch.
  • Before travelling abroad with a family:
    1. Plan flights to the last detail.
    2. Confirm visas (official entry permits) and the exact dates you will be in each location.
    3. Double-check those dates to avoid overstaying a visa. Overstaying can cause legal trouble.
  • Before arriving, research the new location thoroughly. Find out where the hospitals, shops and essentials are.
  • A detailed destination guide with an interactive map helps.
  • Local expat groups (groups of people living abroad) on Facebook are a good source of answers where no guide exists.
  • Check practical questions before committing to a location:
    • nearby shops
    • how children will be cared for
    • daycare availability (places that look after small children during the day)
    • The cost of a nanny (a paid child carer)
    • seasons that make living conditions poor, such as heavy rain or burning season (months of smoke from farm fires)
  • A long-term base with several short retreats of two to three weeks suited one family better than living abroad permanently.
  • Test a relocation before making it permanent. Expect to change plans if the first choice does not fit.
  • Case: The starting point was one online store, run part time alongside a 9-to-5 job, with no team.
  • The owner gambled on the business, quit the job, and within twelve months had three stores, two full-time virtual assistants (remote helpers who do office tasks), and a mastermind group (a small group of business owners who meet to help each other).
  • The mastermind group formed at an industry retreat. The family then sold its belongings and moved to Chiang Mai, Thailand, with a three-year-old daughter.
  • The owner still answered store calls from about 4:30 a.m.
  • Burning season made the air unlivable after about two months.
  • The family moved on the spot to Phuket for about three weeks. They found it too touristy (full of tourists) and returned to Australia to live with parents. In total they were abroad about three months.
  • At this stage the owner hired a first virtual assistant. This person ran the store day to day during a three-week trip to Bali, where the owner handled only minimal tasks.
  • The result: three stores, two full-time assistants, and a home base with occasional mini-retirements (short breaks).
  • Southeast Asia rules:
    • Food and water hygiene can be lower than in many home countries. Choose restaurants carefully.
    • Do not drink tap water or brush teeth with it. Make sure children follow the same rule.
    • Do not carry a family on a motor scooter in busy Southeast Asian towns. The risk to life is too high.
    • Expect poor pavements (sidewalks). Plan routes for pushing a stroller.
    • Use safer transport for the whole family.

Income needed before quitting a job

1 min
  • To find the income you need before leaving a job:
    1. Use the mindset and goal-setting template to map the costs of your ideal life.
    2. Look up real rent and list every other expense.
    3. Add the totals to get your monthly income target.
  • Quit a job only after at least three consecutive months (three months in a row) in which net profit from the business equals or exceeds the salary of the job or jobs you leave.
  • Net profit, not gross sales, is the number that counts.
  • Months spread apart do not count.
    • Source note: the 2024 version calls this true net profit. It says to keep the job and build the store part time until the test is met.
    • Source note: the 2020 version says to keep the day job until online business income matches job income for at least three months. It does not require the months to be consecutive.
    • Then decide whether to quit, since you then have two incomes to choose from.
      • Example: the jobs pay $3,000 per month. The store makes $3,000 net profit in each of three months in a row.
  • The business has then replaced that income.
  • Total monthly income reaches $6,000, and the owner can devote more time to growing the business.
  • Quit when the business reliably earns what the job pays. Factor in how much time you need for the transition.
  • An accountant (a person who manages business books and taxes) can give peace of mind, but is optional.
  • Many founders run as a sole proprietor (one person owning and running the business with no separate company) first. They add an accountant once revenue arrives.

Net worth and progress measures

1 min
  • Net worth equals savings plus investments minus debt.
  • It shows whether income is actually kept and built into assets (things that hold or grow value).
  • That makes it a better progress measure than gross revenue or salary.
  • Judge your business and personal finances by the profit you keep after all costs, not by gross revenue.
  • Retained money (money you keep) is what builds wealth.
  • The Cashflow Quadrant from Rich Dad Poor Dad sorts income into four types:
    • employee
    • self-employed
    • business owner
    • investor
  • The source states that employees and self-employed people control about 5% of total US wealth. They make up about 95% of the population.
  • The aim is to move toward owning a system (a business that runs without you), then owning investments.
  • Use the cash the business generates to fund long-term passive income (money that keeps coming in without daily work), such as real estate or shares in other companies.
  • Set a longer-term vision, for example five years. It defines what the investments look like and sets the income goals for the business.

Pay yourself first

1 min
  • Move a fixed share of income into savings or investments before covering other spending.
  • This makes saving automatic. It does not depend on what is left over.
  • Saving whatever remains after spending usually leaves little or nothing.
  • Even a small amount such as $300 per month should go into savings as soon as income arrives, before any spending.
  • A small fixed amount beats waiting for a larger surplus (extra money) that may never come.

Profit First

1 min
  • Profit First is a cash management method from Mike Michalowicz's book Profit First.
  • It splits incoming revenue into percentages. Each part is sent to a separate account before money is spent. Each dollar then has a set purpose.
  • Pay the owner a regular salary from a planned account.
  • This reduces burnout (exhaustion from overwork) and makes personal income predictable.
  • Irregular withdrawals create uncertainty and anxiety for the owner and household.

Saving and investing basics

2 min
  • Do not try to time the market. That means predicting short-term rises and drops, then buying or selling on those predictions.
  • Use low-cost index funds (funds that hold many companies at low fees) as the core of a long-term portfolio (your collection of investments).
  • Hold investments long term. Do not keep trading in and out of positions (buying and selling holdings).
  • Dollar-cost averaging means investing the same amount at regular intervals, regardless of price.
  • Over time this spreads purchases across high and low prices. It reduces the risk of buying everything at a peak (the highest price).
  • Limit speculative investments (risky bets) to 0% to 5% of the total investment portfolio. The rest goes into core long-term holdings.
  • Compare investments by the time and responsibility they demand, not only by expected return.
  • A passive fund or loan platform can be worth a lower return if it removes the work of tenants, repairs and property management.
  • Do not sell investments during market downturns (when prices fall).
  • Selling in panic turns temporary paper losses (losses on paper that have not been realized) into permanent ones.
  • It usually means missing the recovery.
  • Long-term investors should expect downturns and stay invested.
  • Investing books for beginners:
    • Unshakeable by Tony Robbins is a shorter version of his longer book Money Master the Game.
    • The Only Investment Guide You Ever Need by Andrew Tobias is a classic introduction to low-cost index investing.
    • When reading investment books, skip any section that promotes a specific portfolio or company the author has a stake in (owns part of).
    • Keep the general principles on low fees and long-term holding.
    • Money Master the Game has a portfolio section of this kind. Ignore it.
  • Personal finance reading list:
    • The Millionaire Next Door: habits of wealthy people who live modestly.
    • Rich Dad Poor Dad: buy assets, not liabilities (things that take money out of your pocket).
    • The Total Money Makeover by Dave Ramsey: for getting out of debt.
    • The Millionaire Fastlane: test whether an expensive lifestyle purchase is worth it.

Debt and emergency fund

1 min
  • Compound interest works in both directions. It means interest earns interest over time.
  • The mechanism that grows investments also makes credit card and other high-interest debt grow fast.
  • Clear high-interest debt before serious investing begins.
  • Start investing only after debt is cleared and an emergency fund (money set aside for surprises) is in place.
  • Build the plan around a fixed monthly amount.
  • Investing money you still need for debts or bills is a common reason people sell in a panic.
  • Case: an online store owner had $70,000 in debt. They paid all of it off first and became debt-free.
  • Afterward, their dropshipping stores reached about $10,000 in profit per month.
  • Clearing debt before building a passive-income system was the key step.
  • Keep a rainy-day fund (emergency savings) in a separate bank account.
  • Months of runway (how long you can keep going) equal the fund divided by monthly costs.
  • This shows how long the business can cover its costs if income drops to zero.
  • Credit mistake: assuming that no credit or bad credit blocks business cards for good is wrong.
  • Start with a business debit card (a card linked to your bank money, not borrowed money). Then get a secured card (a card backed by a deposit you pay in).
  • This builds credit that leads to better cards within one to two years.
