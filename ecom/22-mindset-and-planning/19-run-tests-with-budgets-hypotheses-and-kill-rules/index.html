Run tests with budgets, hypotheses and kill rules

Section 22.19 · 19 of 26 in this chapter · 30 min read

In short

  • Treat every idea as a guess. Test it before you trust it.
  • Set a fixed test budget before you start. Only spend money you can afford to lose.
  • Set your stop rules (rules for when to drop a product) before you spend. Then follow them.
  • Test one product at a time. Expect several failed products before one winner (a product that sells at a profit).
  • Most early tests lose money before one wins. Plan for that.
More key points
  • Set a stop rule (kill rule) before you test, then follow it.
  • Judge products by the numbers, not by how much you like them.
  • Keep testing many products. One win or one loss does not decide the business.
  • Wait about three weeks before making big changes.

Testing culture and hypotheses

  • No ad strategy is a silver bullet (a magic fix that always works).
  • Mentors give educated guesses (informed guesses based on experience), so every opinion must be tested.
  • Treat each recommendation as a hypothesis (a proposed explanation that still needs proof), not as a fact.
  • Testing produces proof for decisions. Advice from tools and mentors only gives guidelines.
  • When you are unsure and the budget allows, test.
  • When the budget is limited, put money where it already converts (turns visitors into buyers).
  • Treat every idea as a hypothesis, and test it rather than debate it.
  • Rejecting an untested idea should require a detailed written argument.
  • The default is to run the test.
  • Treat every ad test as a hypothesis.
  • Decide on the one variable (the single thing you change) to test.
  • Keep the other things the same.

  • Find and test products systematically, rather than repeating a few products.
  • Repeated testing of a few products without a method wastes time and money.
  • Facebook ads are not a fixed science with guaranteed rules.
  • Verify results with ongoing tests.
  • Market conditions change, so continuous testing is a standard part of the process.
  • Intuition and personal taste often misjudge what the market will buy.
  • Check ideas by response, not by how good they seem.
  • Expect to be wrong often. Let results correct the plan.

  • Trust the data over your own opinion.

  • This includes changes to ads, pages and budgets.

  • Base decisions on factual data rather than gut feeling.

  • Only trust an ad when its tracked cost per customer is known.

  • Change one asset at a time, judged on its own metric (the number that measures it).
  • Do not guess.

  • Move to a live store with a real paid ad only after each step is understood.

Testing budget and capital

3 min
  • Set a firm testing budget before you buy traffic (paid visitors) for product tests.
  • Avoid spending large amounts on tests that do not follow the research method.
  • In the mentor's example, unfocused tests on other markets and platforms cost about $500 in total.
  • Disciplined testing lost only about $100.
  • Testing requires spending money, because product results only show up after real spend.
  • Set a fixed test budget in advance.
  • Judge the product on that data, not on the first few clicks.
  • Only test with budget you can afford to lose.
  • Small samples (small amounts of data) give noisy results.
  • The testing period also needs enough spend before the data can be trusted.
  • Set a fixed testing budget that you can afford to lose completely.
  • Decide in advance that losing it will not upset you.
  • Do not treat the budget as a limit that ends the business once it is spent.
  • Before seeing any profit, a beginner should expect to lose about $2,000 in testing, or whatever amount their budget is.
  • The lower the starting budget, the smaller the expected loss.
  • Write this expectation down before starting.
  • Losses grow with business size.
  • A larger operation may lose $30,000 or $200,000 in testing.
  • Losing money is a normal cost of doing business, not a sign to quit.
  • Giving yourself a budget and quitting once it is spent is the wrong approach.
  • Accept the testing budget as expendable (money you can use up).
  • Keep testing.
  • When testing a new product, spend no more than 50 percent of your available investment capital (money set aside to invest) on the test.
  • For example, with 5,000 USD in capital, keep at least half in reserve (money kept aside).
  • How long to test before judging depends on your personal budget and how much risk you can take.
  • Products can fail quickly, so keep spare capital rather than going all in.
  • Test a new product on a small budget first, to see whether the market responds.
  • Testing an untested product with the full budget means risking it all on an unknown outcome.
  • Test one product per week with a budget of about 200 to 300 USD per product.
  • Keep going until there is enough data to either move on or keep trying.
  • Ad spend on a product with zero sales is learning money, not lost money.
  • Move on after about 300 USD of spend with zero sales.
  • Spending profits as soon as they arrive, or switching between many new ventures, starves the core business.
  • Keep the budget for the next product, and for branding the current store.
  • Plan a fixed test budget and a schedule in advance for influencer campaigns (paid posts by people with followers).
  • Do not spend freely.
  • Influencer campaigns often fail. Some of the first creators contacted will not convert (will not bring sales).
  • Keep the first sales only as proof.
  • The core is a repeatable process: test products, read results, move on.
  • Spending on research tools and paid training is a trap.
  • The main work is testing ads with real budgets.
  • Research can show ideas, but only ads prove them.
  • Early paid testing minimum: about 100 clicks per product is too small a sample for a firm conclusion.
  • Treat the first 100 clicks as a learning and optimisation (tuning) period.
  • Test early ads over an extended period (about 25 days at 10 dollars per day for a product) before dropping it.
  • Include optimisation time.
  • Do not stop at a fixed dollar amount if sales are missing.
  • Test before scaling: start with a small test budget, and only scale an approach if it performs.
  • Each ad account is different, so CBO (one budget for the whole campaign) may not work.
  • ABO (a separate budget for each ad set, meaning each group of ads with one audience and budget) or other scaling methods may be needed.
  • Getting comfortable with large ad budgets is part of scaling a profitable store.
  • Spend levels that seem extreme at the start can be necessary to grow tenfold later.

Kill rules and stop criteria

4 min
  • Set kill rules (rules that say when to drop a product) before you test it, and follow them.
  • Keeping a product you like, without results, wastes time.
  • A product with a strong visual wow factor but no clear problem it solves often gets views without sales.
  • Set cut rules before the test starts, and follow them strictly.
  • A failing product blocks budget that another product could use.
  • A strong product can make 5-10 sales on day one.
  • Stop a product that keeps losing money after a set test budget.
  • Do not hope for a turnaround.
  • Fixed stop rules keep the test budget away from unproven products.
  • Set a clear kill rule before spending on a new product.
  • If a product gets no sales after a fixed test spend and a few days of ads, cut it.
  • Move to the next product instead of pouring more money in.
  • Kill a product early when it shows no signs.
  • Test about $50 to $100 on day one, with a maximum of $200 to $250.
  • On TikTok, consider killing it around $60 if creatives and CTR (click-through rate, the share of viewers who click) are poor.
  • Give a product 3 to 5 days after fixing the offer (the deal you sell) and the store.
  • If about $200 has been spent across 10 to 15 audiences at $5 to $10 per day (an audience is the group of people an ad shows to) without any sign of profitability, move on to the next product.
  • Spend is one of the limits, but the numbers decide.
  • Before running ads, know three numbers: COGS (cost of goods sold, what the product costs you), break-even return on ad spend (the sales per dollar you need so you do not lose money), and break-even cost per purchase (the most you can spend on ads per sale).
  • These numbers set the limits for every later scaling decision.
  • Switch off an ad that spends past its margin (profit left per sale after costs) without a sale.
  • In the example below, the margin is too thin to scale at the current CPA (cost per acquisition, the ad cost of one sale).
  • So also raise average order value (the average money per order).

One product at a time

2 min

  • Beginners should test one product at a time, with one account per product.

  • Running several products in parallel splits attention.

  • It makes the learning curve harder to manage.

  • Test one product at a time with full focus.

  • Move to a new product only after the current one fails to get traction (gain attention and sales) within about one to two weeks.

  • Once a product converts, keep it, and open more accounts to push it rather than switching products.

  • When working alone, test one product at a time for about two weeks, with two products at most.

  • Spreading small effort across many products usually fails.

  • Commit fully to one product, then expand once you are more experienced.

  • Testing one product at a time is the discipline.

  • Testing a single product, deciding it is a scam or saturated (the market is full and buyers are tired of it), and quitting is the most common failure pattern.

  • Testing multiple different products in rapid succession, instead of focusing on one product through all six phases (stages of the method), disrupts the learning cycle.

  • It prevents reaching profitability by Phase 6.

  • Consistency requires staying with one product long enough to gather meaningful data.

  • Testing sequence for one winning candidate (a product that looks promising):

    1. Test one product at a time.
    2. Try different store designs.
    3. Try different prices.
    4. Try different bundles.
    5. Try different audiences.
    6. Once a winner is found, scale it.
    7. If the results are not what you need, move on to the next product.

  • Patience with one product: a store takes months to form its brand.

  • Keep a product in testing until its data confirms it works.

  • Only then add more products to the store.

  • A failed brand can be tested again once you have improved offers, upsells (extra offers after the first purchase) and creatives.

  • Apply what you learned from the first attempt before retrying.

  • Do not use it as a reason to abandon the brand category (the whole type of product).

Scaling, automation and channel notes

2 min

  • Procedure for paid traffic automation work (software doing ad work for paid visitors):

    1. Test automated bidding (software that picks how much to pay per click) with a controlled experiment (a test where only one thing changes).
    2. Improve the product feed (the product list sent to ad platforms) to get more traffic.
    3. Use Smart Shopping (an automated Google shopping ad type) in controlled tests, not on the whole account at once.
    4. Shift the time saved to higher-leverage activities (tasks that gain the most).

  • Automating everything at once and checking performance only once a month lets underperformance run for weeks unnoticed.

  • Introduce automation in steps with regular review.

  • Blind trust in automated results is the most common error.

  • Automation works like an exoskeleton (a wearable frame that helps a person lift more).

  • It lets the marketer handle much more volume without more strain.

  • It does not replace judgement.

  • Use it to get more done with the same effort.

  • The basics of paid advertising stay the same whatever the interface (the screen or tool you use).

  • Show the right message to the right person at the right time.

  • If a campaign ignores this, for example by targeting (choosing who sees the ads) an audience that has no reason to buy, it will underperform.

  • This happens regardless of tool changes.

  • Build the mindset of right traffic, right time and right price before setting up any campaigns.

  • The later campaign work depends on these concepts.

  • Checking targeting against the funnel stage (where a visitor is in the buying path) and the cost of acquisition (cost to win one buyer) is the core test for every paid campaign.

  • Paid ad management should be based on profitability (making more than you spend), rather than raw traffic volume.
