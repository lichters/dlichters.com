Playbook › 22. Mindset and planning

Decide fast and launch imperfectly

Section 22.18 · 18 of 26 in this chapter · 22 min read

In short

  • Do not overthink. Act first, then fix mistakes once the work is live.
  • Pick a niche (a small group of buyers with one shared need) in days, not months.
  • Launch a basic store early. Real visitor data teaches more than planning does.
  • Pick one niche and stick with it. Do not keep switching.
  • Keep working on a niche that passes the checks. Switch only after repeated failures.
More key points
  • Build the store and get one real paying customer before you chase growth tactics.
  • You need at least two supplier approvals before you launch.
  • Give each step a time limit. If a step takes too long, ask for help.
  • Start cheap and simple. Improve the store based on real numbers and real customers.

Overthinking and analysis paralysis

2 min

Overthinking means thinking so long that nothing gets done. Analysis paralysis is the same trap: too much study, no action.

  • Overthinking stops execution, which means it stops the work itself. Think less and do more.
  • Rely on your coaches. Solve problems as they come up during the work. Do not try to solve all of them in advance.
  • The best way out of analysis paralysis is taking action, even with an imperfect first version. **
  • Things can be fixed once they are live.
  • Progress comes from moving forward and fixing mistakes on the way. **
  • This works better than waiting for a perfect first try.
  • Making mistakes is normal in business.
  • Business decisions use what you know now, plus instinct. **
  • Waiting for 100% confidence before acting is a common trap.
  • That confidence never arrives.
  • Move forward with what you know.
  • Be ready to pivot (change direction) when results show a different path.
  • Overthinking the niche choice holds many beginners back. **
  • Pick a niche that passes the criteria (the checks a niche must pass). Then start the work.
  • Do not wait for certainty.
  • Overthinking a product costs money and loses chances.
  • Choose products with proof of sales (proof that people already buy them).
  • Run ads (paid online adverts). Let the Facebook data decide, not your own assumptions.
  • Overanalysing the niche decision makes people go in circles. **
  • They start doubting their own reasoning.
  • For a first business, this can keep people stuck for months.
  • Searching for the perfect niche, product or customer leads to endless second-guessing. **
  • This can last weeks or months.
  • This delay causes frustration.
  • It often ends with the person giving up.
  • Do not keep cycling between unsure ideas without deciding. **
  • Endless re-checking delays building the business.
  • When research is complete, either confirm a market or get an outside check.
  • Use a niche verification tool (an outside check on your niche). **
  • Get the green light, then move forward.
  • Overthinking prevents progress.
  • Research-heavy work takes time. **
  • Taking longer to choose a niche is fine if it avoids a blind launch (launching with no real checks).
  • Do not let indecision stop you indefinitely (forever).
  • Once an idea is validated (proven to be sound), act on it.
  • The longer you delay a decision, the less likely you are to act at all. **
  • Choose a viable (workable) niche. Start the store. Learn from real results.
  • Founders often keep many ideas open out of fear of missing other chances.
  • Commit to one anchor category (main product group) and a few concrete steps.
  • That is usually what makes progress start.

Perfectionism and waiting for the perfect launch

3 min

Perfectionism means wanting everything flawless before you start. A "launch" means putting your store online for real visitors.

  • Perfectionism kills progress in business.
  • Waiting for a perfect store, perfect ad creatives (ad images and videos) or perfect timing is usually resistance disguised as perfectionism.
  • A launch produces real market data (facts about what buyers actually do).
  • No preparation can replace that data.
  • The sooner a basic version is live and measurable, the sooner you can make the decisions that matter.
  • Delaying a launch while polishing details keeps the business at zero results.
  • It hides the real bottleneck (the thing that slows everything down).
  • That bottleneck is usually the lack of live feedback.
  • Treat the feeling of "not ready yet" as a signal.
  • Define a minimum launch version (the smallest version that works).
  • Set a fixed go-live date.
  • Perfectionism delays collecting real customer data. **
  • A store with its core elements working should go live.
  • Launch day is only the starting point.
  • Improve the store from its own data after that.
  • Waiting for every supplier approval and a perfect performance score before going live is a mistake. **
  • That wait postpones the only thing a launch is for: data from real visitors.
  • The goal at launch is learning, not a big first-day sales spike.
  • The main reason to launch early is to learn three things. **
  • You learn what customers search for, how they compare the store with competitors, and what they think of it.
  • These insights come only from real visits. Theory cannot fully replace them.
  • Time cannot be recovered.
  • Waiting months to pick the perfect niche, start ads or call a supplier costs more than launching and refining.
  • Launch the store early instead of over-planning.
  • Overplanning can stop action. **
  • A minimum viable product (MVP, the smallest version that works) is often better than waiting for perfection.
  • Go live, then improve from real customer feedback.
  • Balance speed with quality: launch early and improve over time.
  • Over-complicating the brand document (a written plan of your brand's name, look and voice) delays the launch. **
  • Write down as much as you can quickly.
  • Use what you already know about the product and customer.
  • Testing in dropshipping (selling products that the supplier ships to customers, so you never hold stock) means the brand is launched early and refined later.
  • Waiting for a complete brand before going live slows learning.
  • Test every element. **
  • Do not wait for a perfect product or relationship before launching.
  • If a test fails, learn from it, apologise where needed and move on.
  • Templates (ready-made starting designs) let beginners reach the market faster.
  • Start with ready-made templates, such as a landing page (a single page that turns a visit into a sale) or an ad template.
  • Learn design and psychology while ads run.
  • Analysing why a live ad works builds skills in parallel.
  • Waiting until all learning is finished before launching is not sustainable.
  • Pursuing a perfect niche is a common trap. **
  • Choose a niche that passes all tests. Start building.
  • Improve through real traffic (visitors) and sales.
  • Do not chase the perfect niche before building. **
  • Even imperfect stores often beat expectations.
  • Each store builds experience for the next one.
  • A perfect market does not exist. **
  • Every market idea has downsides as well as a reasonable number of upsides.
  • The goal of niche selection is to find a good market, not an ideal one.
  • When selecting a market, aim to identify good ideas and avoid bad ones. **
  • Do not aim to find perfect ideas.
  • It is hard to predict whether a niche will become a very large store or a modest one. **
  • Screen niches efficiently and start, instead of researching endlessly.

Time limits on niche choice

2 min
  • Niche selection must stay within a strict time limit, because perfectionism kills progress. **
  • It should normally take no more than one week.
  • It often takes only one to two days.
  • Market and product selection should be done within a matter of days. **
  • Some people finish it the same day.
  • Do not let it stretch into months.
  • Set a fixed deadline for niche research, such as three days or one week. **
  • Choose a niche on that date, no matter what.
  • This prevents endless research that never leads to a launch.
  • Use the forum to explore niche selection before choosing. **
  • But do not stay in research mode for long.
  • Balance learning with a firm decision date.
  • Moving quickly through market selection matters. **
  • The goal is to launch and start earning as early as possible.
  • Getting stuck at this stage is the main risk to avoid.
  • Market selection is about choosing a good-enough niche quickly. **
  • Then you can move on to building the business.
  • Many niches and products can work. **
  • So a choice that meets the criteria is better than waiting for a better option.
  • If niche selection stalls for a long time, narrow the options to about five serious candidates. **
  • Then commit to one.
  • Continued research without a decision delays the store more than a reasonable choice would.
  • Do not brainstorm (generate ideas) until you stall.
  • But do not stop at a few ideas either.
  • See the research depth section below for list sizes.
  • Ad launch on a single product: taking two weeks or more is too slow. **
  • Research properly and launch within about two days.
  • Then make decisions from data.

Break a niche decision block (procedure: the steps to get unstuck)

  1. Take your top two niches from the research list.
  2. Set a specific deadline for choosing, such as a date only a few days away.
  3. Submit the top two for niche verification if you need outside input.
  4. Choose one and start building the store.

Niche criteria and tiebreakers

3 min

A tiebreaker is a rule that decides between options that look equal.

  • Competition happens at the product level within a niche, not the niche level.

  • Multiple stores can succeed in the same niche if they differ in product selection and pricing.

  • To judge whether a niche is too competitive:

    1. Use the method "How much competition is too much competition".
    2. Apply it to the top-selling products in that niche.
    3. Check whether the top competitors have healthy profit margins (the money left after costs).
    4. Decide based on product-level competition, not overall niche saturation (too many sellers).

  • At the competition research stage, the only question is whether the niche is too crowded or too empty. **

  • Working out how to stand out comes later, once the niche has passed the research criteria.

  • Do not choose or avoid a niche purely because of size or weight. **

  • Calculate shipping costs properly using the Freight Center tool.

  • Large, heavy items are viable if margins support them.

  • Judge a niche by the criteria and by whether you can see margin. **

  • Do not judge it by fear of big or heavy products.

  • A good niche with high-ticket items can be profitable even when the product is large.

  • A basic low-margin, low-price product, such as a plain t-shirt, cannot support paid advertising. **

  • Higher-priced products leave room for ads and profit.

  • Replace low-priced niches with higher-priced ones.

  • Do not try to fix them.

  • Sell only what you can handle. **

  • If you cannot take on the customer service load of expensive items, start in a range you can manage.

  • Move up later.

  • If a niche has few products but meets the other criteria, it is not an automatic no-go. **

  • Start it only if you are confident you can get enough supplier approvals to make money.

  • Choose a niche that passes all criteria and whose products keep selling for years. **

  • Then the store has no natural end date.

  • You can sell the store later if you stop running it.

  • Choose a recent product with broad market appeal, not a heavily saturated one.

  • Saturated products need a strong marketing angle to stand out.

  • Start with low investment.

  • Optimise the funnel (the path from visit to sale) before spending on branding.

  • Pick products based on market data and proof.

  • Do not pick by personal taste, intuition or a product that merely looks appealing.

  • Beginners who choose by feeling take on the full risk of an unproven item.

  • Personal passion for a product category is one of the weakest criteria for choosing what to sell.

  • It should rank far below market opportunity.

  • Treat passion as a minor factor, not a deciding one.

  • Choosing a product only because it matches a hobby or long-standing interest leads to pursuing low-potential opportunities.

  • Effort, time and capital then go into a niche that will not grow.

  • You do not need to be an expert in a niche to start selling it. **

  • You learn the product details by answering real customer questions.

  • Check those answers with the supplier.

  • Niche expertise is not required. **

  • Many successful stores were started without experience in the product area.

  • Learning happens while building.

  • You do not need to be an expert in the niche you eventually choose.

  • You also do not need to be passionate about the product.

  • The goal is to become interested in the business.

  • Knowledge of the niche grows over time.

  • A store can be launched while the owner is still learning the product. **

  • Customer questions teach what they need to know.

  • Being willing to learn as you go is what matters.

  • Once the data checks are done, use personal excitement to choose between approved niches. **

  • Excitement here means enjoying selling these products day to day through the website.

Conflict on passion (two rules that seem to clash):

  • Passion is a minor factor overall.
  • A hobby is a mistake to choose on.
  • Excitement breaks ties only between niches that have already passed the data checks.

Committing to one niche

2 min
  • When several niches are approved, the expected chance of success is roughly equal. **
  • Committing to one approved niche matters more than finding a slightly better one.
  • Make the niche decision in one sitting if possible. **
  • Or sleep on it for one night at most.
  • Then move on without spending more time on the choice.
  • Second-guessing the chosen niche after committing wastes time. **
  • Once the decision is made, lock it in and move to the next phase.
  • A niche can be changed later. **
  • It is reasonable to narrow the choice to the top two or three candidates.
  • Then commit to the strongest one.
  • Be sure of the niche before committing. **
  • Use verification and competitor checks.
  • Do not keep relying on a single niche idea when competition is high.
  • Avoid shiny-object syndrome (jumping to every new idea).
  • Focus on one niche where you can build a reputation, rather than chasing many unrelated niches.
  • Founders who keep many ideas open should commit to one anchor category (main product group).
  • Then add a few concrete steps.
  • Build one store and make it profitable before starting another. **
  • Fixing one mistake once is far cheaper than repeating it across several stores.
  • Once the first niche is chosen, build its store before starting another. **
  • Later stores in other proven-winner niches (niches that already sold well) can be added.
  • This creates several income streams without shutting the first store down.
  • Do not abandon a half-built store to chase a new business model that sounds appealing.
  • Reach the current goal first, then consider the next model.
  • A first store should be simple and use the proven process. **
  • More complex brand or seasonal ideas can wait for stores two to ten.
  • Continuous improvement is better than endless research. **
  • If a first store's niche is not perfect, it can still be profitable and provide a learning experience.
  • While it generates profit, build a second store with better niche research.

When to persist and when to pivot

1 min
  • A niche that passes all criteria deserves persistence. **
  • Improve the store before abandoning it.
  • Only sell or rebuild once it has had a fair chance.
  • When many failures appear in one niche, keep pivoting from the closest candidate rather than starting from zero. **
  • Use both rules together.
  • Persist with a niche that passes the criteria and improve it.
  • Pivot to the next candidate when the niche shows repeated failures.
  • Working on the store through improvements before abandoning it, and pivoting only when the closest candidate keeps failing, are both needed.
  • Together they avoid two traps: quitting too early and researching forever.
  • Niche research can be repeated. **
  • A candidate that fails should be retested before it is dropped.

Launch before you know everything

1 min
  • Build the store first. Test it with at least one paying customer before you spend time on advanced strategies. **
  • Advanced material covers scaling (growing sales) and optimisation (improving the store step by step). Save it for later.
  • Your first sale is the checkpoint. Once the core operations (the basic parts that run the business, such as orders and payments) work, growth tactics come next.
  • Start with one basic store in a proven niche (a small market where others already sell). Do not start with many products at once.
  • Follow this order: setup, then supplier approval (a supplier agreeing to work with you), then traffic (visitors to your store). This path is reliable. **
  • Do not spend time on paperwork or heavy setup before the store has anything to show.
  • Start with what you need to sell. Add structures (systems and processes) later. **
  • You can build the store before you know the wholesale price (what the supplier charges you).
  • You can do the calculation for each supplier once the costs are confirmed.
  • Starting without the calculation gives you no data to decide what to sell. **
  • Do not rush the store to completion either.
  • Finish niche selection (picking your market), supplier approvals and the store build before the launch month.
  • Then traffic can start the moment the store opens.
  • Work on the store in small daily steps, not in the final weeks. **
  • Test and iterate (try, measure, improve, repeat). Refine the brand (your name and look) after launch, not before.

Cash flow and lean launch

1 min
  • Cash flow (money coming in and going out) matters more than perfection.
  • Find small profit margins (profit per sale) quickly.
  • Do not wait to build the perfect brand before launching.
  • Product research follows the same rule. Do not wait for a winning product.
  • Launch fast. Test offers (deals) on real audiences (real shoppers). Then improve based on data (numbers).
  • Example: test three offer variations. Keep the one with the highest CTR (click-through rate: the share of viewers who click) and conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy). Then scale it (grow it).
  • Speed beats perfection.
  • Launch the store as cheaply as possible. Reinvest money as it earns.
  • Stores started with too much money behind them tended to perform worse than bootstrapped ones (stores built with the owner's own limited money). **
  • Build the business in stages. Each stage is tied to profitability (making more money than it costs).
  • Even a store with modest results can already make profit.
  • That profit can pay for the next stage. **
  • Finish niche research and verification (checking that the market is real) before you build a store.
  • A store in a weak niche wastes money.
  • A few extra weeks of research are better than a live store that cannot sell. **
  • Your launch timeline depends on your available time and experience.
  • See the time budget table above for the 21-day and seven-week options.
  • Start with low investment (little money put in).
  • Improve the funnel (the path from first visit to purchase) before you spend on branding (your name, logo and look).

Store setup and launch steps

3 min

First high-ticket store sequence (high-ticket means expensive products; procedure, older, 2021-22)

  1. Set up the business (the store and your supplier agreements).
  2. Launch the store.
  3. Get the first sales.
  4. Then shift focus to growth: marketing systems (repeatable ways to find customers) and scaling the business.

Store launch checklist

  1. Get approval from at least two suppliers.
  2. Upload their products to your Shopify store (online store software).
  3. Remove all demo products (sample products).
  4. Test the checkout process (the steps a buyer takes to pay).
  5. Turn on Google Shopping ads (paid product ads in Google).
  • Open the store for business and let customers find you.
  • Keep adding suppliers and improving the store in the background (alongside everything else).

Supplier approval and launch readiness

  • The checklist needs at least two supplier approvals before launch.
  • A launch that waits for every supplier approval is delayed.
  • So the minimum is two approvals. The rest can follow.
  • Get enough supplier approvals to make money before you start a niche with few products.
  • When supplier outreach (contacting suppliers) gets no reply, change the call timing or approach first.
  • Most brands can still be reached this way.
  • Widen the niche only as a last resort. **
  • Contact suppliers now. Do not delay out of fear of rejection.
  • Waiting too long often leads to dropping the niche before any contact. **
  • Do not overthink the call.
  • Feeling awkward about calling customers also stops the programme from happening.
  • These calls are positive. Customers who have bought generally like to hear from the business. **

Launch from the Source D self-onboarding form (a set of questions that shapes your plan)

Setting: Work in your own copy of the self-onboarding Google Doc (an online document). Go to File > Make a Copy. Fill in the copy so the original template (blank master form) stays clean.

Complete the form in this order:

  1. Answer what your business differentiator (what makes you different) is. Explain how you stand out from the rest, covering your products and mission (your purpose).
  2. List two to three things you wish every customer knew about your products.
  3. Name your best-selling product. If you are unsure, check store sales by product in Shopify and paste that product's URL (web address). If you have just started, skip this question.
  4. Describe how you want your brand to be perceived by visitors, for example luxury, premium, wholesome or witty.
  5. List what stops customers from buying: complaints, comments and feedback from people who viewed the website but did not purchase.
  6. List influencers (people with large followings) or authorities who work with or have used your products. Include their social media handles (usernames). Check Pinterest or Google to find them. Include social media influencers, actors, artists, celebrities and doctors.
  7. Describe any seasonal buying patterns (times of year when customers buy), or whether sales are year-round.
  • Answer all seven questions fully before you move on to scaling, ad production (making ads) or any next stage.
  • Skipping questions leaves gaps that later decisions depend on.
  • The self-onboarding questions clarify your niche, your goals and whether you are ready before you go deeper into a course or spend on ads.

Product chain and product choice

  • Plan the full chain from product idea to test-ready product (ready to sell in a test).
  • The steps are research, recipes (the product's written formula or instructions), covers, images and a PDF.
  • The whole chain takes about three to four days.
  • Choose products with proof of sales (evidence that people already buy them).
  • Run ads and let Facebook data (the numbers from your Facebook ads) decide.

Finishing steps and staying on one store

1 min
  • Do not rush through setup just to look finished.
  • Work through each step in order, using the checklist above.
  • Do not spend time polishing a step once its core output (its main result) works.
  • Move to the next one.
  • Start with ready-made templates (ready-to-use forms).
  • Move forward while learning in parallel (at the same time).

Deadlines, time budgets and signs of being stuck

1 min
  • Give each launch step a realistic time budget (a set amount of time).
  • If a step clearly takes longer than its budget, you are probably stuck.
  • Ask a mentor, a community or peers for help. Do not keep going alone.
  • Set a launch deadline for the first store, for example 30 to 60 days, so the launch is not delayed forever. **
  • Set a fixed deadline for niche research, such as three days or one week.
  • Choose on that date. **
  • Use the time budgets in the table above as the reference points for each stage.

Asking for help when stuck

1 min
  • Market selection (choosing your market) is about picking a good-enough niche quickly.
  • Asking for help along the way is encouraged. It is not a sign of weakness. **
  • Rely on coaches. Solve problems as they arise.
  • Do not try to plan everything alone.
  • If you are stuck between two niches, use verification (an outside check) or review.
  • Do not use it to delay the decision further.
  • Once research is complete, either confirm a market or get an outside check.
  • Do not keep cycling alone. **

Focus on high-leverage work

1 min
  • High-leverage work gives big results for the time you spend.
  • Page speed work (how fast your pages load) is not high-leverage for a business owner.
  • Once a site is near the 5-second mark, do not spend days or weeks tuning it.
  • Move your time to core conversion work (the work that turns visitors into buyers). **
  • Time spent on low-leverage work is time not spent on launching and learning from real customers.
