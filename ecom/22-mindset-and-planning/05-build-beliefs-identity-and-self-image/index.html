Playbook › 22. Mindset and planning

Build beliefs, identity and self-image

Section 22.5 · 5 of 26 in this chapter · 24 min read

In short

  • Your beliefs shape your results more than your actions do. Change the beliefs at the root, not only the tactics.
  • Your mindset, the way you think, often limits results more than knowing the steps does.
  • Every thought, word, and action is a vote for the person you are becoming.
  • Take full responsibility for your business. Blaming others gives away your control.
  • Keep your word to customers, suppliers, partners, and yourself.
More key points
  • Give a new mindset practice at least 21 days before you judge it.
  • Spend most of your time on the steps toward your goal, and check the end result only once or twice a day.
  • Picture things in detail, with sounds, feelings and textures. Vague pictures work much less well.
  • Rehearse your mistakes and their fixes, not only your wins.

The chain from belief to result

1 min
  • Your beliefs shape your results more than the actions you take right now do.
  • Beliefs shape thoughts. Thoughts create feelings. Feelings lead to actions. Actions produce results. So to change results, you must change the beliefs underneath.
  • The chain runs: Programming, Thought, Feeling, Action, Result.
  • Programming is the set of automatic beliefs and learned habits from your past. It is the root that shapes every thought after it.
  • To change results, change the programming at its root. Do not only change the tactics you apply.
  • Most people add more skills or tactics but keep the same programming.
  • That gives the same results, because actions still come from faulty automatic thoughts.
  • Thoughts often show up before you make any conscious decision. So review them on purpose.
  • Skills such as niche selection (choosing a narrow group of customers to sell to), store building, copywriting (writing words that make people buy), negotiation, and paid advertising are actions.
  • Actions come out of beliefs. Beliefs about how to think as an entrepreneur matter more than any single skill.
  • Faulty beliefs lead to poor actions, even when your skills are good.
  • Inspired ideas also come from programming. So better beliefs produce better ideas.
  • Things have no meaning on their own. The person who interprets them gives them meaning.
  • Only a few things are facts. Most of the rest is interpretation.
  • Choose the interpretation that helps you act. Avoid the one that limits or discourages you.
  • Pain and pleasure are the main drivers of human behavior.
  • Learn to direct them on purpose. Create mental links on purpose: connect discomfort with the behavior you want to stop, and connect reward with the outcome you want.
  • Directing them on purpose, instead of being driven by them without noticing, is the base of business success.
  • Treat your mindset as the foundation of your business.
  • Protect it from negative influences. Sharpen it with regular practice.
  • Use your mindset to guide every decision in the business.

Mindset as a limit on results

2 min
  • In e-commerce (selling products online), mindset usually limits results more than technical knowledge or strategy does.
  • When you stall even though you know the steps, the cause is often a mental habit, not a missing skill.
  • Mindset is roughly 80 percent of what separates operators who succeed from those who do not.
  • So attitude and the way you read problems matter more than tactics alone.
  • Tactics change fast. Examples are the latest ad targeting (choosing who sees your ads) and platform strategy (the rules and methods of a sales platform).
  • Mindset decides your long-term results.
  • The more time you spend on personal growth and a business mindset, the less the tactics matter.
  • Cautious habits from a risk-averse upbringing (being raised to avoid risk) clash with e-commerce.
  • E-commerce rewards comfort with uncertainty.
  • Expect the business to test that comfort zone again and again.
  • Early business mindset traps include thinking short-term.
  • Other early traps: being easily distracted, expecting fast results, and expecting something for nothing.
  • Spot these traits early and change how you approach results. This is necessary before any success shows up.
  • Limiting beliefs about how far a product can grow can hold back revenue (money coming in from sales). This happens even when the numbers are strong.
  • Talking with experienced operators can help you break through a growth plateau (a stretch where growth stops).
  • Your price is limited by how much you believe you deserve.
  • If you struggle to raise prices, examine your self-worth (how much you value yourself) and your belief about the value you deliver.
  • Money flows along the path of least resistance.
  • Limiting beliefs about earning money act as resistance that blocks that flow.
  • Removing negative emotional attachments to money makes income easier to attract and keep.
  • Money has no agenda and no judgement about its owner.
  • Feelings of inferiority come mainly from measuring yourself against other people's standards. They do not come from real gaps in skill.
  • Measure yourself against your own standard. Accept that everyone is unique. This removes most of that feeling.
  • Negative beliefs are usually built through repetition (doing something again and again) and emotion.
  • Stronger, more consistent beliefs backed by emotion weaken them.
  • Picture a desired outcome vividly and often. This creates good emotions, in the same way that worry creates bad ones.
  • A salesman earned about $5,000 a year no matter how good or poor his sales area (territory) was, or how his commissions (pay per sale) changed.
  • A consultant found the cause. The salesman believed he was a $5,000-per-year man.
  • With a poor territory, he worked hard to reach that figure. With a good territory, he slowed down once he got close to it.
  • So a self-evaluation (the judgement you make of yourself) can limit earnings more than outside conditions do.

Self-image and identity

2 min
  • Your self-image is the mental picture you hold of the kind of person you are.
  • Your self-image sets the boundaries of what you can achieve.
  • Results and behavior tend to match that picture. So lasting change usually starts by updating the self-image first.
  • Positive thinking aimed at one goal fails when it clashes with a negative belief about yourself.
  • Such thinking only works after the underlying self-belief has shifted.
  • Identity comes before results.
  • Decide who you want to become. Label yourself as that person.
  • Think, talk, and act in line with that person. Each daily thought, word, and action is a vote for who you are becoming.
  • Every thought, word, and action counts as a vote on the person you are becoming.
  • Build habits, through thoughts, words, and deeds, that match your desired identity.
  • Build a deliberate identity that drives your behavior:
    1. Decide clearly who you want to become.
    2. Identify and explicitly label that identity.
    3. Take consistent actions that reinforce it.
    4. Actively seek and collect proof of the identity.
  • To take on the role of a designer, first learn beginner Photoshop techniques, such as blending (mixing layers of images).
  • Then complete small finished projects.
  • Each finished project is concrete proof that the new identity is real. That proof reinforces the behavior.
  • Adopt a gaming mindset toward business and personal development.
  • Choose your desired character. Keep improving your skills.
  • More skills, relationships, and resources unlock bigger opportunities and more significant games to play.
  • Think like a successful entrepreneur. Ask what a successful person would do if they started from zero without that money.
  • Reverse-engineer (work backward from) a lazy person's habits to find the opposite behavior.
  • Successful people delay gratification (wait for a bigger reward later).
  • Successful people also avoid overspending and emotional decisions.
  • Ignore opinions from people who have not done what you are trying to do.
  • Their doubts often reflect their own beliefs about what is possible. They do not reflect the reality of your goal.
  • Living the benefits of a successful business before the money arrives supports confidence.
  • Waiting for the money first, and only then enjoying its benefits, tends to erode confidence.
  • Confidence in the sense of self-efficacy (believing you can succeed in a specific situation) is a belief.
  • It also includes believing in your own self-worth.
  • Confidence is not the amount of activity you produce.
  • When your business persona (the face you show in business) clashes with your real self, stress builds.
  • Confidence falls when that clash happens.
  • Align both into one consistent identity. This restores confidence without tricks or forced self-presentation.
  • Treat yourself with the same kindness you would show a good friend.
  • Avoid harsh self-criticism.
  • Reward good work with something positive.
  • When you feel guilt about an action your reasoning says is not wrong:
    1. Stop and name the cause of the feeling.
    2. Examine that cause in detail. Test whether it holds up.
    3. If it is absurd, state clearly why, and reject it.
    4. Whenever a similar irrational thought comes up later, examine and reject it the same way.

Role models

1 min
  • Identify people you admire.
  • Study their mentality (way of thinking), habits, and behaviors deeply.
  • Copy their actions and approaches. This speeds up skill development.
  • Choose role models whose behavior you can copy.
  • Examples are how they outsource (hire others to do tasks) and how they focus on their strengths.
  • Deliberately copy their habits and ways of thinking.

Acting as if

1 min
  • Belief in yourself is the base of action.
  • If real belief is missing, act as if you have it until the belief catches up.
  • You are not your past. Check whether old beliefs still serve you.
  • Act as if you already hold the role you want. Do this before you feel it or achieve it.
  • Look for small pieces of evidence that support the new identity. Then the behavior builds on itself.
  • A writer decided to act as if she already had the ability she suspected she had. She stopped acting tentatively.
  • Within a year, her output and sales as a writer had increased many times over.
  • She also found a talent for public speaking that she had never used.
  • The change came from a deliberate decision to act on an assumed ability. This happened before any proof appeared.
  • Two psychologists asked 45 men in hospital for neuropsychiatric conditions (conditions that affect brain and mental health).
  • The men retook a personality test. This time they answered as a typical well-adjusted person would.
  • Three-fourths of them scored better. Some changes were large.
  • Imagining the role of a well-adjusted person was enough to change how the men answered and how they felt.
  • In a demonstration, a participant's grip strength measured 100 pounds on a dynamometer (a device that measures grip) in his normal state.
  • After a suggestion that he was much stronger than he had ever been, the same grip reached 125 pounds. His physical ability had not changed.
  • A self-imposed limit can hold back ability that already exists.

Core beliefs: the ten beliefs

1 min

Commitment and responsibility

1 min
  • Commit fully to succeeding. Do not only wish for success.
  • Commitment is the belief that separates operators who make it from those who only hope.
  • Belief 1: Commit to the outcome instead of only wanting it.
  • Saying "I want" declares that you do not have it yet. It keeps the lack in place.
  • Commitment treats the outcome as already decided. It keeps you moving through setbacks.
  • A commitment test: imagine an opportunity with a 99.9 percent chance to return 10 times a minimum investment of 20,000 dollars.
  • Now imagine the 0.1 percent failure happens, and the money is lost.
  • If you would still keep pursuing your goal, your commitment is strong.
  • Commitment can be built up over time through repeated practice.
  • Take full responsibility for everything that happens in your business, both good and bad.
  • Blaming markets, suppliers, or others removes your ability to change the outcome.
  • Belief 2: You are 100 percent responsible for everything that happens in your business. This includes the actions of staff (the people who work for you).
  • If a team member makes a mistake, the owner who hired and trained them shares the responsibility.
  • The owner must fix the training, the procedure (the set way a task is done), or the hiring.
  • Full responsibility gives full control over outcomes.

Learning and growth

2 min
  • The more you learn, the more you can earn.
  • Continuous learning is a direct driver of income.
  • Belief 3: The more you learn, the more you earn.
  • Learning never ends, because markets and platforms keep changing.
  • Dismissing a new channel (a way of reaching customers) because you dislike it can cost a lot.
  • Many large social platforms became major sales channels after early owners ignored them.
  • Keep learning current platforms, even when they feel unattractive.
  • The more you earn, the less you need to work.
  • Growing income should eventually reduce the hours you personally put in.
  • Working harder as the only way to grow leads to burnout (total exhaustion) and a hard ceiling on income.
  • Hours in a day are limited. So restructure the business through staff and systems before growth can continue.
  • Growth can come from two sources. One is repeated painful trial and error. The other is a sudden shift in understanding when a belief clicks.
  • Consciously reflect on a new belief until it feels true. This can lock it into the subconscious (the part of the mind that runs on autopilot).
  • That can change behavior faster than years of trial and error.
  • Review the belief list regularly to give this process time to work.
  • Personal growth happens in two ways. One is painful experience. The other is sudden moments of clarity.
  • Both paths produce learning. The second can come faster.
  • Adopt a growth mindset (believing ability can grow with effort).
  • Treat every result, good or bad, as information for the next step. Do not treat it as a final verdict on your ability.
  • Failure is a lesson, not an identity.
  • Do not judge yourself by old beliefs.
  • Do not accept others' feedback as the final answer.
  • Learn from your experiences yourself.
  • Difficult results still carry useful information if you look for it.
  • A fixed mindset treats a failure as proof of inability. This leads to giving up.
  • Avoid it by treating every failure as a lesson and a cue to improve.
  • Test every limiting belief by asking whether it is useful for growth. Do not ask only whether it is true.
  • Discard beliefs that do not serve progress.

Customers, money and tools

1 min
  • Expect that customers will pay you for real value.
  • Build the business on the assumption that people are willing to buy.
  • Belief 5: Treat money as a tool.
  • Spend to make money.
  • Do not let emotional ties to money stop you from investing where there is a proven return (money back from the spend).
  • Belief 6: People pay you money, not PayPal, Stripe, or any other tool. PayPal and Stripe are payment services that move the money.
  • Keep customer psychology (how customers think and feel) at the centre of planning, marketing, and offers.

Think big

1 min
  • Think big when setting goals and planning the business.
  • Small thinking limits the size of the store and the decisions behind it.
  • Belief 7: Always think big instead of playing it safe.
  • Aiming high takes similar energy as aiming low.
  • Set goals that would be worth reaching even if only part of them succeeds.

Receiving

1 min
  • Be open to receiving money, help, and opportunities.
  • Resistance to receiving blocks growth, even when the business is working.
  • Belief 8: Learn to receive well.
  • Accept gifts, money, and compliments. Look the giver in the eye and say thank you. Do not deflect (brush it off).
  • Practising this regularly builds receiving into a habit.
  • It does not block others from giving.

Play to win

1 min
  • Play to win instead of playing not to lose.
  • A defensive mindset makes you avoid the bold moves that growth needs.
  • Playing not to lose means aiming only to cover bills or break even.
  • This keeps a business small. It also makes the owner give up quickly under pressure.
  • Starting a business is emotionally and mentally demanding. So aim to win clearly, not just to survive.
  • Belief 9: Play to win instead of playing to not lose.
  • Set ambitions that go beyond covering costs. The effort of building a business is similar either way.

Keeping your word

1 min
  • Always keep your word to customers, suppliers, partners, and yourself.
  • Reliability is part of the business's long-term value.
  • Belief 10: Keep your word. Integrity means what you say and what you do stay whole, meaning they match.
  • Every unfinished promise to yourself leaves an open loop (an unfinished task that stays in your head).
  • Open loops fill your thoughts.
  • Each broken promise lowers how much you trust yourself to follow through.
  • To close open loops and stay in integrity, take one of these two options for each commitment you have made:
    1. Complete it when you said it would be done.
    2. If you no longer want it, cancel it clearly and explicitly. Tell yourself it is over. Then focus on the next priority. Do not keep unfinished goals in the background.
  • Integrity can be scored as a rough percentage. It shows how often you keep the commitments you make to yourself and others.
  • Honest first ratings tend to come out low. Many people score below 50 percent.
  • Tracking the score over time shows whether you are improving.

Effort and hustle

1 min
  • Hustle is the only success factor you fully control. So it is the most important lever (the thing that moves results most) in the early stage of a business.
  • Hustle requires courage, confidence, self-belief, and self-determination.
  • You need these to keep working until the opportunities you want appear.
  • Hustle is effort you control. It combines daily commitment with a real desire for the result.
  • It means working consistently, not only harder for a short time.

Gratitude and confidence exercises

2 min
  • Daily gratitude exercise for quick confidence:
    1. Set a timer for two minutes at most.
    2. List five to ten things you are grateful for. Ordinary things count, such as family, food, good people, or the electricity that powers a light.
    3. Do this every day, for example after waking up.
    4. Rate your confidence from 1 to 10 again. Compare it with the earlier score.
  • Gratitude shifts the mind from a scarcity mindset (feeling there is never enough) to an abundance mindset (feeling there is plenty).
  • This makes opportunities around you easier to notice.
  • In the test, most people rose one or two points on the 1 to 10 confidence scale.
  • Income or revenue often starts to rise about six to eight weeks after consistent work on mindset and self-alignment steps.
  • In that period, it sometimes doubles or triples.
  • Build confidence with the Sacred Six:
    1. Forgive yourself for past mistakes.
    2. Commit to doing what you say you will do.
    3. Rack up wins, no matter how small.
    4. Remove negativity from your environment.
    5. Visualize your desired outcomes.
    6. Seek knowledge continuously.
  • Sacred Six daily practice for building self-confidence. Do it each morning:
    1. Forgive yourself for yesterday. Accept that you are human.
    2. Commit only to what you say you will do. Then do it, so you can trust yourself.
    3. Collect small wins through the day instead of counting losses.
    4. Remove negativity from your environment, such as news and negative content. Process negativity without letting it consume you.
    5. Visualize your goals in as much detail as possible. This helps the brain filter for relevant opportunities.
    6. Seek more knowledge, and apply what you learn.
  • Write a short reminder into each calendar block (a time slot in your calendar).
  • The reminder states the kind of person to be during that block, or it is a brief affirmation (a short positive statement).
  • Reading it at the start of the block helps shift attention to the intended mindset.
  • Review your core beliefs every day to keep them active in your subconscious.
  • Write down which beliefs you still struggle with. Act on the ones that feel uncomfortable.
  • Repeated review helps new beliefs replace old automatic thoughts.
  • A new belief has settled into the subconscious when reviewing it gives you an immediate felt sense of recognition. It feels like a sudden "I get it" moment.
  • Intellectual agreement alone is not enough.
  • After reviewing each belief, check for that felt response.
  • If it does not appear, reflect on that belief again before moving on.

Testing limiting beliefs

1 min

  • Rewrite a limiting story that blocks a task:

    1. Write down, in one sentence, the story you tell yourself when a task becomes hard.
    2. Identify the expectation behind it. For example, what should feel comfortable, or what should already be true.
    3. Compare that expectation with what is actually happening. Note the gap.
    4. Write a new sentence you can accept. Do not pretend the discomfort is gone.
    5. Repeat the new sentence before the next hard task. Track whether the old story still drives your decisions.

  • Testing a limiting belief with six questions:

    1. Name the belief behind the behavior. For example, that you cannot do a certain task, or that you deserve less.
    2. Ask whether it rests on an actual fact, or only on an assumption or a false conclusion.
    3. Ask whether there is any rational reason to hold it.
    4. Ask whether you could be wrong. Ask whether you would reach the same conclusion about another person in the same situation.
    5. If there is no good reason, ask why you keep acting as if it is true.
    6. Answer each question seriously and emotionally, not casually. A flash of healthy indignation (anger at something unfair) can help break an old false idea.

  • Choose a past experience in which a result matched a belief you held about yourself:

    1. Write down what happened. Include what you expected and how you reacted.
    2. Explain the experience using one principle. Examples are self-image, a limiting belief, or the role of an automatic goal (a goal your mind pursues without you choosing it).
    3. Use the explanation to decide which belief to test next.

    Start the procedure by choosing that experience.

  • Negative beliefs are usually built through repetition and emotion.

  • Trying to prove or disprove a new mental method through intellectual argument or endless debate stops you from testing it.

  • The method can only be judged by practicing it and observing your own results.

Visualization and mental rehearsal

1 min

Timing and daily practice

1 min
  • Give any new mindset practice (a routine that changes how you think) at least 21 days before you judge whether it works. About that long is needed for an old mental picture to fade and a new one to settle. During this time, do not keep checking your progress or debate whether the method is valid.
  • During the first 21 days of a new mindset practice, review the core principles (the main ideas) about three times a week. Look for examples in your own life and in people you observe. Do not measure your progress or argue with the method during this period. The point is to test the practice, not to judge it early.
  • Practice visualization (picturing something in your mind) every day, since consistency (doing it regularly) matters more than length. Sessions of 10 to 15 minutes can produce noticeable change. 30 minutes can be built up to once the daily habit is established.
  • The nervous system (the body's control network for feelings and reactions) responds to a vividly and richly imagined experience almost as it does to a real one. Mental rehearsal (practicing in your head before you act) can therefore build skills and confidence that carry over into action.
  • Spend most of your time on the process and the next steps toward a goal. Revisit the end result briefly, once or twice a day, during visualization, then return to the process.
  • Forcing the answer to how a goal will be reached, before the goal is clearly defined, blocks the mind from finding it. Define the goal first. The practical steps tend to appear when you are relaxed.
  • When willpower (forcing yourself through effort) and imagination conflict, imagination usually wins. Goals should therefore be pictured calmly, not forced through strained effort.

Daily 30-minute visualization

1 min
  1. Set aside about 30 minutes alone and undisturbed each day.
  2. Sit or lie down comfortably and close your eyes.
  3. Picture yourself on a large screen, or see the scene from your own eyes, whichever feels more vivid (clear and strong).
  4. Add small sensory details to the scene: sounds, smells, textures (how things feel), objects, and the surroundings.
  5. Picture yourself acting, responding, and feeling in an ideal way in the situation. Ignore how you acted yesterday.
  6. Do not tell yourself you will act this way tomorrow. Imagine acting this way now, during the session.
  7. Let the emotion of already being that person come up as you watch the scene.
  8. If your mind wanders, return to the picture calmly, without self-criticism (being harsh on yourself).
  9. Repeat the session every day, starting with 10 to 15 minutes if 30 is too much.

Relaxation before imagery

1 min
  1. Sit comfortably, or stand. Lying down works, but it may lead to falling asleep and reduce the benefit.
  2. Scan your body for tension (tight muscles) and consciously let go in the head, neck, torso (chest and belly), and legs.
  3. Smile inwardly (smile in your mind, without showing it) to help the body relax.
  4. Breathe deeply and follow each inhale (breath in) and exhale (breath out).
  5. Spend about five minutes relaxing with conscious effort, then stop controlling the muscles and let imagery bring on the relaxed state.
  6. Use one relaxation image: heavy limbs as if made of concrete sinking into a surface, a body held loosely like a marionette (a puppet) with slack (loose) strings, or a body deflating slowly like a balloon.
  7. Or recall a calm past scene in detail, noting background sounds, temperature, and textures.

Anchoring a positive self-image

1 min

Self-image means how you see yourself. Anchoring means linking a feeling to a picture so the feeling comes back when you see the picture.

  1. Relax first, using the breathing and relaxation steps.
  2. Recall any past occasion when you did something well. It can be trivial, such as learning a basic skill. Its size and date do not matter.
  3. Replay the memory in detail until it brings back a positive, pleasant feeling in the present.
  4. Picture yourself in a future situation the way you want to be.
  5. Attach the same feeling from the past memory to that future picture, adding emotion to what you see.
  6. If attention drifts, bring it back without judging yourself.

Motor rehearsal

1 min

Motor means related to body movement. Motor rehearsal means practicing a movement in your mind first.

  1. Form a clear mental image of the shot, movement or task you are about to perform.
  2. Rehearse it in your mind in full detail, including how it feels when it goes right.
  3. Step up and perform, relying on the practiced pattern rather than consciously controlling each movement.
  4. If the result misses, note the difference and adjust the mental image before the next attempt.

Evidence from studies and cases

3 min

A controlled study is a test that compares a group that practices with a group that does not. Anecdotal means based on stories, not tested data.

Study or case What was practiced Result Ids
Basketball free throws (shots from the foul line), controlled study Imagined shooting for about 20 minutes a day over 20 days Scoring improved 23 percent. Physical practice improved 24 percent. No practice showed no gain.
Basketball free throws, correction of misses Imagined practice that included correcting each missed shot after it happened 23 percent scoring gain over 20 days, close to the 24 percent gain from physical practice
Darts, controlled experiment Sitting before a target each day and imagining throws Accuracy improved about as much as real throwing practice, suggesting mental rehearsal can match physical rehearsal for well-defined motor skills (skills that use the body, such as throwing)
Salespeople rehearsing conversations Imagined customer conversations and objections (customer reasons for saying no), with the best reply worked out for each Sales increases of 100 to 150 percent in some groups and up to 400 percent for individuals. These figures are anecdotal, not a reliable benchmark (a standard for comparison).
  • Mental practice works best when it includes correcting errors, not only replaying successful shots.
  • Pitching academy: a coach taught high school and college baseball players mental imagery and relaxation exercises to help them reach 90 miles per hour. Many players could not reach it by effort alone. Many hit 90 for the first time once their bodies relaxed. Their self-images then adjusted to the new velocity (speed of the throw). Within 18 months, the number of players at the facility who broke 90 mph rose from 18 to 98. The coach's account is anecdotal and not a controlled study.
  • Golf: a beginner who had never played sat in an easy chair for five minutes each day and visualized the key points of a correct swing, with no practice on the course. Several days later, with no physical preparation, the beginner joined a regular foursome (a group of four players) and shot nine holes in even par, 36. Even par means the expected score for those holes. The instructor's core rule was that a clear mental picture of the correct action must come before performing it. His claim that the mental side makes up 90 percent of golf, the physical side 8 percent and the mechanical side 2 percent is an unverified estimate (a guess that has not been checked).
  • Violin: a violinist was thinking of retiring because a wrist injury made regular practice difficult. A coach asked him to play the violin in his mind without holding the instrument. A week later, he gave what he considered the best concert of his life, and he dropped the idea of retiring.
  • Chess: a challenger reportedly prepared for a championship match by playing only in his mind for three months, after giving up smoking and drinking and doing calisthenics (body exercises such as push-ups). He defeated a champion who had been expected to win easily. The case suggests that structured mental play (planned practice played in the head) can prepare a competitor for a specific contest.
  • Dentist fear: a visualization exercise did not work until the person added concrete sensory details, such as the smell of antiseptic (a cleaning liquid), the feel of the chair's armrests, and the sight of the dentist's hands approaching. Once the imagined scene was detailed enough, it produced an effect close to real experience. Vague imagery gives a much weaker result than detailed imagery.
  • Interview preparation:
    1. Plan the interview in advance and list the questions that are likely to be asked.
    2. Think through a clear answer to each question.
    3. Rehearse the whole interview in your mind, including questions you did not prepare for.
    4. Practice reacting spontaneously (answering without a planned reply), because rehearsal builds confidence and the ability to adapt even when the actual questions differ.

Roles and self-view in practice

1 min
  • Ignore the outside verdict (what others say about you) and measure progress against your own standard, because the goal is a stable self-image rather than external approval (praise from others).
  • Practice the role before the proof arrives, then collect each completed piece of work as evidence for the new identity (who you are becoming).

Further details

1 min
  • Success loop (a repeating cycle that improves results): 1. Take action. 2. Collect feedback on what the action produced. 3. Correct your approach based on that feedback. 4. Take action again with the correction and repeat the cycle.
  • Follow the success loop described by Jim Rohn in this order: 1) take action, 2) get feedback from the result, 3) make corrections based on that feedback, 4) take more action. More action produces more feedback, which allows better-informed actions in the future. Most people stall (get stuck) because self-limiting thoughts (thoughts that hold you back) stop them from taking the first action.
