Playbook › 22. Mindset and planning

Handle setbacks, emotions and the long game

Section 22.20 · 20 of 26 in this chapter · 29 min read

In short

  • Building an online shop is a long run, not a sprint. Expect slow months.
  • Steady daily work matters more than any secret trick or one big lucky moment.
  • Most people quit in weeks two to four, just before results start to show. Keep going past that point.
  • Failed tests and lost money are lessons to learn from, not proof that you should stop.
  • Judge yourself by real numbers and your own past, not by moods or other people's best cases.
More key points
  • When you feel like a failure, check your real money numbers first.
  • Stay calm and keep acting. Quitting before results show up is the main reason beginners fail.
  • When a store or niche underperforms, find one fixable cause and fix that. Do not abandon everything.
  • Measure your progress against your own past results, not against other people's.

Endurance and the long game

4 min
  • Running your own business is an endurance effort, meaning a long effort that lasts years, not weeks. It has emotional swings, meaning mood ups and downs. Plan for long periods of doubt. Do not assume steady work always brings steady results.
  • Running a business is a long endurance effort with emotional highs and lows. Your mind can invent stories about failure. Base your judgment on actual figures, not on feelings.
  • Paid traffic (visitors you pay to bring in with ads) is a long-running effort, not a quick win. Judge progress by year-over-year growth (compared with the same time last year) and quarter-over-quarter growth (compared with the last three months). Stay consistent with the plan and keep improving it over time.
  • Linear, predictable growth (growth that rises in a straight, steady line) does not happen in e-commerce (selling products online). Expect large peaks and large lows as a normal part of the business.
  • Burnout (exhaustion from stress and repeated failure without rest) comes from repeated failures without rest. Expect failure in advance to keep your effort sustainable over the long term. Long-term persistence in e-commerce depends on this.
  • Succeeding as an entrepreneur requires a higher tolerance for short-term pain, such as hard work and uncomfortable tasks. Look for the pain you are avoiding. If you are not getting the results you want despite working hard, you are probably avoiding a specific kind of pain.
  • Treat building an online business as a marathon (a race meant to run for years), not a sprint. Set realistic expectations about pace. Do not expect everything to be finished within a week.
  • High-ticket dropshipping (selling expensive products without keeping stock, where a supplier ships each order) has no hidden trick that separates winners from losers. Results depend mainly on focus, consistency and patience, not on knowing a secret strategy.
  • Three qualities are required to succeed with an e-commerce business model: focus, consistency and patience. Build these deliberately, because they determine whether you reach the goal.
  • Most operators fail at the consistency stage, not at the knowledge stage. They do not apply the marketing work repeatedly over months. A system produces predictable growth only when its parts are run every week without long gaps.
  • Consistency and commitment decide success more than any single method. Owners who quit or switch to a new idea each time something feels hard rarely build a profitable store. Do not switch ideas every time something feels hard.
  • Change does not happen overnight. Real results often come only after many long working hours, many tested products and repeated mistakes.
  • Consistency combined with trust in the process is what lets a beginner later recognize a turning point (the moment results start to change for the better). Keep executing the same routine steadily, instead of expecting a single breakthrough moment.
  • Successful sellers treat the work as an ongoing game with no final victory. Once one level is reached, there is always a next level to improve. The aim is steady improvement and enjoying the process, rather than waiting for a single finish line.
  • Growth from continuous testing compounds slowly and unevenly, so results can feel flat or up and down for months before a winner appears. Growth can then be explosive or linear. Keep testing patiently while you wait.
  • Persistence over a long period produces success in e-commerce, not a single breakthrough. The payoff of consistent effort is expected to be much larger than the short-term sacrifices. Avoid "silver bullet" products, meaning magic fixes, programs or events that promise instant results.
  • Searching for a single silver-bullet solution (a magic fix) wastes time. Business growth is an iterative cycle, a loop of trying, learning and refining, in which progress comes through repeated attempts. Expect the journey and do not be disheartened during it.
  • Winning in dropshipping requires persistence and discipline. Keep testing products without quitting. Expect months of work before profit.
  • Work every day with discipline, because consistent effort is what produces results. Expect channels (the places where you reach buyers, such as social media or search) and tactics to change. Stay resilient and open to new approaches rather than worrying about the wider economy.
  • Dozens of product tests without a winner still count as progress if each round improves the ads and the pages. Consistent testing over the long term is the path to a winning product.
  • Expect months of work before profit, and treat the early stage as a long runway (a long starting period) of testing.
  • Building problem-solving skills and resilience (bouncing back after setbacks) matters in dropshipping. Problems with suppliers (the companies that make and ship your products), stores and ads will appear constantly. Expect problems and solve them one by one.
  • The most successful dropshippers keep working consistently with focused effort until the business works. Ask questions in the community early on.
  • Learning online marketing through repeated testing and correcting mistakes builds a strong practical knowledge base over time. Treat early failed campaigns (ad runs that did not work) as the cost of learning, not as proof that the model does not work.

Process over outcomes

1 min
  • Adopt a process-driven mindset (focused on daily steps) instead of an event-driven mindset (focused on single big moments). Let go of excessive emotional weight on individual outcomes. Focus on enjoying and executing the process consistently, because process builds skill.
  • Progress follows an iterative loop (a repeating cycle): set a goal, move toward it, fall, learn, improve and repeat. Count each failure in the loop as learning, and as part of the path, not as a reason to stop.
  • Build a process-driven rather than event-driven approach. Daily work accumulates over time. Judge progress by the consistency of the process, instead of waiting for a single big moment.
  • Enjoy the process of building your brand. Sustainable, high-quality work flows from a positive mindset throughout the entire journey.
  • In business, the goal is steady progress rather than perfection. Perfection often cannot be reached, takes too long, and can make you miss opportunities or quit. Measure yourself against your own past work, not against the best in the field.
  • Progress matters more than speed. Build the store step by step, and keep making progress every day rather than trying to finish everything at once.
  • Avoid perfectionism (the need for everything to be flawless) when writing sales copy (the written text that sells). A letter that follows the guidelines and has a worthwhile offer will usually work, even if it is not perfect. Practise regularly, since each letter makes the next one easier. The confidence from one skill carries over to others.

The gap: weeks 2 to 4 and the first months

4 min
  • The gap is the period between starting a store and the point where results show up. It feels like failure, but in the early stage it is normal progress. Progress at this stage is largely invisible, so a person without visible results tends to conclude that nothing is happening.
  • Week two to week four is the phase in which new dropshipping operators most often quit. The urge to quit usually comes from discomfort growing while results have not yet appeared, not from the strategy failing.
  • A full mindset change takes about two months of consistent work. In the first two weeks people are excited. Around week four many want to quit, so continue for about two more weeks.
  • Most beginners quit just before results start to show, so impatience is a major cause of failure. Keep going past the point where early progress is still invisible.
  • Beginners most often fail by stopping before testing 12 products, either by not investing enough time or effort or by questioning the process too early.
  • Expect early months to be slow. Momentum usually comes from steady small actions, such as adding suppliers, shipping orders and checking ads (looking at how your paid ads perform).
  • Reaching a first winning product (a product that sells well and makes profit) can take around a year for some sellers, while others get there faster. Plan for a long runway of continuous testing instead of expecting a quick win within weeks.
  • Expect the first sale from ads to take a few weeks. Keep optimizing (improving) and increasing spend (the money you put into ads) rather than abandoning a campaign too early.
  • Stick with one opportunity long enough to see results instead of switching to new ones. Growth tends to come from consistent effort over time, even when the first sale takes weeks or months.
  • Do not expect Google Ads (paid ads shown in Google search results) to produce strong results immediately, especially for a new store. Results usually improve over several months as the account gathers data and the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) improves.
  • Feeling overwhelmed is a normal reaction when starting a new online business, especially with many tasks ahead. Follow a fixed, step-by-step order to make the start manageable.
  • A long, slow start can be treated as the cost of learning the business rather than as proof that it does not work. Judged against the eventual result, the value of not quitting can far exceed the losses taken during the struggle. Do not quit during the slow start.
  • Early work in e-commerce compounds (small gains add up over time). Skills, test data and understanding of copy and market sophistication (how much buyers have already seen similar offers) build on each other. So results can stay flat for months and then rise sharply. Because early gains are not visible, judge progress by whether you are learning and testing, not by short-term sales alone.

Case: eight months before the breakthrough (the breakthrough is the moment results finally take off)

A student took the same course and used the same strategies, tools and information available to everyone. For eight straight months nothing worked as expected. Stores did not convert (visitors did not buy), products flopped, and ad spend went out without coming back, month after month. Most people in this situation quit at month two or month three and move on to something else. This student took short breaks but kept coming back. He treated every failed store as data rather than defeat, and refined his research process with each attempt. He also built a deeper understanding of copy, creatives (ad pictures and designs) and market sophistication over time. In month nine he reached $67,000 in revenue (money from sales, before costs) for a single month. A store of his later reached roughly $6,000 in one day in May 2026. A dashboard (a summary screen of key numbers) screenshot from another of his stores showed yesterday's total sales at $5,937.77. The lesson framed the eight months as tuition paid for the skills behind that result.

Plateaus, random swings and slow first results

2 min
  • Results in a new store can come early or late, and early success is not guaranteed to continue. Treat slow results as a signal to optimize, not as a failure of the whole plan.
  • Random swings in early sales are normal. Do not overreact to a single slow week or a single great day. Judge performance over a longer period of data.
  • Expect randomness in early results, such as a strong day followed by several days with no sales. Judge progress over longer periods and keep following the process.
  • Accept that early results are random and may take months to stabilise (settle into a steady pattern). Do not interpret a short stretch without sales as proof that the store has failed.
  • Early store problems such as low sales are often learning-curve issues (problems that come from still learning how things work). Do not shut down a store because the niche (the small market group you sell to) seems hard. Successful stores exist in many niches with the same model.
  • Setbacks such as an ad that does not return money, a store that does not convert, or competitors' high revenue claims are normal parts of the process. The compound effect (small gains adding up) of consistent work usually becomes visible only after a long period.
  • Reaching a plateau (a flat period with little change) is not the same as failing. Growth from testing is uneven, so flat periods are expected before a winner appears.
  • Low engagement (few likes, comments and views) does not mean a platform is dead. It means strategy matters more than before. Clarity and consistency build momentum over time.
  • Low engagement should not lead to stopping or random posting. Keep posting consistently, review what works and what does not, and adjust the strategy for the next two months.
  • Early content with few views is normal. Continued testing usually leads to a viral result (content that spreads very widely). Going viral once builds confidence for repeating it.
  • Dozens of product tests without a winner still count as progress if each round improves the ads and the pages.
  • Focus on consistency sooner rather than waiting for sales to arrive. Consistent traffic (visitors arriving to your store) and steady monthly results come from working on the store each week, not from waiting.

Losses and failed stores as the cost of learning

3 min
  • Accept early losses as the cost of collecting data and learning. Beginners who lose around 50 to 100 USD early and then complain or blame the process will not succeed. Such losses are not proof that the business model fails.
  • Money lost during testing can be earned back, while time cannot. Keep going and do not quit after early losses. Quitting is the only outcome that guarantees failure.
  • Expect the first store you build to fail and generate no profit, but treat it as a learning investment. Going in with this assumption protects you from burnout when early tests do not win.
  • Expecting a failed first store in advance reduces its emotional impact, so the reaction does not carry into the next attempt. This keeps you from burning out and quitting between product tests.
  • Treat every failed test and every unprofitable day as market data. A poor first day usually means the pixel (a small tracking code that records visits and sales) is still learning who buys. Each failed product narrows down what will work.
  • Treat every failure as data that costs time and money but shows what to change. Each failure proves you can repeat the work, do it better and do it at a larger scale.
  • Setbacks such as an ad that does not convert or a product that does not sell are normal in dropshipping. Treat each one as data to analyze and use for the next decision, not as a personal failure.
  • Failed products and slow starts are part of learning, not a reason to stop.
  • Learning any skill is trial and error, where each miss is corrected on the next attempt until the desired performance is reached. Mistakes and short-term setbacks are part of that process. They should not stop you.
  • A single failure does not end a business. The willingness to keep going after setbacks matters more than avoiding them. Never pass up the learning that a bad experience offers.
  • Failed stores and lost money are part of the e-commerce learning process. The skills gained along the way, such as editing product pages, building landing pages (single pages built to get a sale or sign-up), improving conversion and running ads, have value on their own. They can be sold as a service if the store does not succeed.
  • Case: the eight-store seller. An early-stage seller built about eight dropshipping stores, and all of them failed, with roughly 50,000 to 60,000 lost across that period. The failures were treated as an ordinary part of the process and as the cost of gaining skills in store building, ads and conversion. Experienced operators point out that this stage often lasts one to three years before results become visible.
  • Frustration and early losses are normal. Record each failure and what it taught you before changing the next test. Pushing through with a learned adjustment is the difference between people who quit and people who succeed.
  • Treat a project that does not go as planned as a source of knowledge for the next attempt. Reuse what worked and improve on what did not.
  • Making a loss is not failure in itself. Go back to the hypothesis (the guess you were testing), the mistake and the next step.
  • A setback is feedback, not failure. Write down what happened, form assumptions (educated guesses) about why, change the next effort based on those assumptions and test again before spending more money.
  • Persistence through the hardest phase is the decisive factor. Operators who stop are the ones who fail, while those who stay in long enough usually figure it out and see results. Stay in long enough.

Fear, desperation and panic

2 min
  • Operating from fear and negativity is inefficient, resource-intensive (it uses up a lot of energy) and produces decisions that are less rational and intelligent than decisions made from calm or positive states.
  • Acting from fear or desperation produces worse decisions than focused, strategic action. Healthy caution is useful, but desperation is harmful.
  • Operating from fear and negativity wastes mental capacity that should go into solving problems. After a setback, allow a short emotional reaction, then clear it and refocus on the task. Put losses in perspective, and face fears through deliberate exposure (doing the scary thing on purpose, in small steps) instead of avoiding them.
  • Want an outcome strongly enough to drive action, but let go of its perceived importance. Desperation about the result weakens decisions, so enjoy the process while working toward the goal.
  • Persistent anxiety and worry about results usually reduce performance. Focusing calmly on the goal and the immediate action improves it. Effort aimed at forcing outcomes tends to work against itself.
  • Doubt and fear of never succeeding are normal when you start a business alone, especially with no one confirming you are on the right path. Do not treat these feelings as a reason to stop or change course before your planned checkpoint.
  • Anxious self-talk (the inner voice that worries) usually predicts a much harder experience than what actually happens.
  • Much of the perceived difficulty comes from the mind exaggerating the challenge, not from the task itself. When such a thought appears, recognize it, move past it and take the action anyway.
  • The fear that every supplier will refuse and the business cannot start is a worst-case scenario that rarely happens. Even if a full refusal did occur, the result would be a return to product research and finding new suppliers, not a dead end.
  • Common early mistakes include acting out of panic after small losses, such as the first small ad spend, a seasonal dip (a normal slow season) or a supplier problem. These reactions lead to poor decisions. Stay calm and strategic instead.
  • Panicking in the ad account and turning to no-shipping orders (fake orders that never ship, used to fake sales) or scam tactics to win is a dead end that destroys the business. Never use these shortcuts. They harm customers and the brand's reputation.
  • Retreating to safe, low-risk activity after a mistake stalls growth. A mistake is a reason to adjust and keep going, not to withdraw.

Staying calm under pressure

2 min
  • When you feel helpless, remember you always have a lever to pull: time, resources or skill. Find which one you have right now and use it.
  • Quick action, not overthinking, staying calm instead of emotional, and continuing to act are the basic keys to success in dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships to your customer, so you do not hold stock yourself). A founder must be willing to wake up early and do the work.
  • Staying calm and continuing to execute when things go wrong is the most important skill in e-commerce (selling products online). Failing to manage the chaos is what causes most failures.
  • Focus your effort on what you can control. Take the action first, and do not spend energy on outcomes outside your influence. Stoic reasoning (an old philosophy that says to accept what you cannot change) is a practical way to manage stress.
  • Do all your worrying, risk assessment (judging what could go wrong) and information gathering before you commit to a decision. Once the decision is made and you have started, let go of worry about the outcome.
  • If you decide to do something unpleasant, commit fully, in your mind as well as in action. Fighting a decision you already made only creates stress and makes the experience worse.
  • Plan the future in advance, but respond to the present moment in action. Limit your focus to a 24-hour window, and do not worry about how you will react to events that have not happened yet.
  • When you feel tense or jittery (restless, on edge), ask yourself: what can I act on right now? Much anxiety comes from reacting to a past situation or to something that cannot be done in the present.
  • Replaying past mistakes and criticizing yourself for them keeps the failure in focus. Treat errors as feedback, correct course, then forget them and dwell on (keep thinking about) the response that worked.
  • Make business decisions logically, not emotionally. Respond to results with calm analysis (careful study of the numbers) rather than emotional reactions.
  • Running a paid ad that makes no sales and reacting with anger or frustration damages long-term success. Control your reaction before you decide the next step.
  • Running a business is highly stressful, and regular school does not teach stress management. Without deliberate emotional control, you risk burnout (being exhausted and empty) and quitting.
  • Stay emotionally stable through daily swings in results. Do the planned work regardless of yesterday's results.
  • When sales drop, step back emotionally and handle the problem logically instead of riding the emotional roller coaster (the up and down feelings). Focus on the long-term picture rather than daily or weekly sales swings.

The seven strategies for setbacks

1 min
  1. Use exposure to difficulties to build strength.
  2. Gain perspective: you are alive.
  3. Remember that perception (how you see and interpret things) governs reality. Your interpretation of events matters more than the event itself.
  4. Focus on the task at hand without wasting resources on worry or rumination (replaying the same worry over and over).
  5. Practice mindfulness (paying full attention to the present moment) and observation.
  6. Understand your own psychology (how your mind works) and patterns.
  7. Ask what your heroes or role models would do in this situation.

Revenue dips and reading the numbers

1 min
  • When anxiety tells you that you are failing, check the real money numbers (your financial data: sales, costs and profit) first. Accurate numbers often disprove the worst assumptions and reduce the stress that drives poor decisions.
  • If you want a business to change your life, treat it as a real business, not a hobby. That means tracking your numbers consistently, even when you feel confident.
  • A seven-figure operator mindset (earning over one million dollars a year) means making decisions based on data rather than emotions. It also means playing the long game instead of chasing quick dopamine hits (short bursts of good feeling).
  • A drop in revenue (all the money coming in from sales) is not automatically a crisis. Before reacting, check whether profit (what you keep after costs) held or improved. A busy store with low margin (a small share kept from each sale) can be worse off than a quieter store with higher margin.
  • Feeling the need to stay busy and chasing revenue back up after a sales drop can erode profitability (hurt how much you keep). Let the profit numbers guide your action, not the urge to look productive.
  • Real progress shows up as a slow, steady upward trend over time, even with daily ups and downs. Judge performance over longer periods, not by single-day swings.
  • Reading every downturn as a sign to quit is a common mistake. Lows are a normal part of the process and do not mean the business has failed.
  • Fixing your focus on specific daily or monthly revenue targets, such as $1,000 per day, $10,000 per day or $10,000 per month, is a fragile way to think. When a target is missed, the mindset can collapse and read the miss as failure. Focus on building skills instead.

Case: the record year and the panic

1 min

A high-ticket (expensive product) dropshipping owner had a record year. Then revenue (sales income) fell sharply, starting in January of the following year. For months the owner kept panicking, felt like a failure and feared for the family's income. In August or September, the owner was advised to sell the store while it still had value. The owner listed it with a business broker (a person who finds buyers for businesses). The broker required the full four years of financials (money records). The owner had relied on accountant reports without understanding them and did not trust them. So the owner rebuilt and analyzed the numbers personally. The analysis showed that revenue had fallen, but profit had become higher on less revenue, with only small ups and downs. The business was in better shape than the panic suggested. Preparing accurate numbers for the sale also led to actions the owner had already started. These paid off within a few weeks, and the store grew quickly in the month it was listed.

Root-cause problem solving

2 min
  • If a niche (a narrow group of buyers with one shared interest) meets all the niche selection criteria but the store underperforms, troubleshoot the underlying issues rather than abandon the niche. Identify the root cause (the real underlying problem), such as too few suppliers, low traffic (few visitors), poor conversion (few visitors buy), weak product pages or slow customer service. Fix the issue. Do not pivot (switch) to a new niche, because the same problem transfers to the next niche.
  • If a niche passed every selection criterion but still does not sell, the niche is usually not the cause. Re-check the criteria before abandoning the niche, and do not give up after a short period.
  • Do not switch niches because of a few slow months if the niche passed validation (proof that it works). Inconsistent sales usually point to fixable issues such as suppliers, conversion rate, product pages (the page for one product) or traffic quality.
  • When a store shows no results despite long working hours, reframe the situation as elimination rather than failure. Each product, angle (the main message or approach) or offer that gets no traction (no real response) shows what to drop, which brings you closer to the combination that works. For example, replace "I have worked six hours and nothing works" with "I just eliminated what doesn't work, so I am closer."
  • Setbacks should be analyzed as cues (signs) for what to change, such as a model that does not fit your tolerance for support work (how much customer help you can handle). Before you scale up order volume (grow the number of orders), make sure your support systems (how you help customers) can handle it.
  • When a product underperforms, attribute the result to a specific, fixable cause, such as the angle or the creative (the ad image or video). Concluding that the product or the whole business model is dead is the mindset that leads to quitting.
  • Write down your learning from each failure, then change one thing at a time. Diagnose a problem before changing the whole business.
  • Persistence (not giving up) and problem-solving skills are the foundation. Problems will appear, and a community such as a Discord channel (a chat room in the Discord app) is a useful source of help and peer support (help from people at your own level).

Procedure: the four steps when you hit a wall

1 min

When you hit a wall (feel stuck), follow these four steps in this order:

  1. Close the dashboard (the screen that shows your numbers). Stop refreshing the ad account (the account that runs your paid ads), since you cannot optimize (improve) what has not finished learning. Step away and let the algorithm (the ad platform's automatic system) do its job.
  2. Go deeper on customer research. On an unprofitable day, do not touch the ads. Use the time for customer research, because the best use of that time is sharpening the next creative rather than watching numbers.
  3. Diagnose, don't panic. Ask whether the problem is traffic, creative, landing page (the page visitors see after clicking an ad) or product. Bad results have a cause, so find that cause and fix that one thing without burning down the whole business.
  4. Remember what you are building. You are building a skill set that nobody can take from you, not just a store. Every day you stay in, profitable or not, you become someone who knows how to do this, and that skill has real value.

Store revival and the keep-or-close decision

1 min

Procedure: reviving a stalled or shut-down store

1 min
  1. Take a break from the store.
  2. Review it from a high level to find missed opportunities.
  3. Decide what should be cut and what should be scaled.
  4. Apply the lessons to a fresh restart.
  • Giving up on a store before it gets a fair chance is one of the most common failures. Work through optimization (improving step by step) and proven steps first.
  • Do not close a store after a few months just because it underperforms. Use the time to work on the store's main drivers (the biggest things that move sales) and try a realistic next target.
  • Do not give up on a store that is slow to get approved or has few sales in its first months. Keep building suppliers and products, and optimize.
  • Reconciling "keep" and "cut": Do not close a store because of a few slow months, but do decide what to cut and what to scale once you have reviewed it. Close a store only after the review shows that the problem is not fixable, and carry the lessons into the next restart.

Supplier outreach: fear and rejection

1 min
  • Feeling nervous before contacting suppliers (the companies that make or ship your products) is normal. It comes mainly from fear of rejection, not from the task being objectively hard. Most people feel this before their first supplier calls.
  • Expect the first supplier call to feel a little awkward, the second to go better and the third to feel good. After a few calls, the process becomes much more natural.
  • Some suppliers will refuse or be dismissive (brush you off), but they are a small minority. Treat rejection as a normal part of doing business, and do not let it stop the outreach.
  • Do not talk yourself out of supplier outreach (reaching out to suppliers) or frame it as begging. Suppliers are generally waiting for your call, and most will agree to work with you.
  • Expect early supplier rejections even for experienced sellers in a new niche, because a rejection often means the supplier wants to see a track record (a history of past results) first.
  • Do not take a rejection personally. A no is usually caused by a missing requirement, such as a minimum (the smallest order they accept) or a capacity limit (how much they can make or ship). Only a rejection caused by your own lack of preparation or honesty is a sign you should improve before trying again.
  • How you move through difficult periods strongly determines long-term success in business. Build the habit of continuing after setbacks from the start, because difficult times always occur.

Not comparing with others

1 min
  • Do not compare your revenue or progress with the results other operators post on social media or in community groups. Such comparisons ignore differences in time invested, products and starting points, and only create discouragement.
  • Comparing your timeline with other people's is misleading, because results can take a week, a month, a few months or a year, depending on the person. The only fair benchmark (standard to measure against) is your own previous performance.
  • Avoid expecting quick results from reading success stories. The visible results of other successful sellers are usually the end of a process that included failures, losses and often two to three years of work.
  • Avoid chasing short-term wins, such as posting screenshots of a single good sales day. The aim is a long-term sustainable business (one that lasts). One good day is tiny in comparison to the largest players in the market.

Not quitting early

1 min
  • Most beginners fail because they lack focus, consistency and patience, and they quit when results do not come as quickly as expected. Quitting before results appear is the main cause of never reaching the potential of the business.
  • The most common reason beginners fail is that they stop before testing 12 products, either by not investing enough time or effort, or by questioning the process too early.
  • Difficult periods, such as losing money or facing a tough patch (a rough stretch), are temporary. Pushing through them is what separates operators who succeed from those who give up.
  • Set goals compelling enough to keep driving daily action through unpleasant tasks. Remind yourself that most people quit at the moment of difficulty.
  • Set goals larger than your obstacles. When goals exceed obstacles in significance, you maintain focus despite setbacks.
  • Set goals larger than the obstacles, such as paying off family debt, being financially independent or having money that does not need to be justified to anyone. Reconnect with these goals when results lag (come in slow).
  • Keep motivation alive through small signals of progress, such as seeing customers wear your products and receiving orders from abroad. These moments help you keep working toward the long-term goal during slow periods.
  • When motivation drops, focus on the result of doing the work now. Someone else will take the opportunity if you do not. Consistent action over time separates successful owners from others, who win by doing the work more often than they skip it.
  • Most people fail at the consistency stage... see Endurance and the long game above; the stop-before-results pattern is described in.
  • Write down the moment you wanted to quit and what triggered it. Then write down what you did in that moment and what a successful operator from the case would have done instead. Keep this note somewhere you will see it regularly.

Procedure: the quit note

1 min
  1. Write down the moment you wanted to quit and what triggered it.
  2. Write down what you did in that moment.
  3. Write down what a successful operator from the case would have done instead.
  4. Keep this note somewhere you will see it regularly.

Complacency after the first win

1 min
  • Do not get comfortable after your first winning product. Resting on initial revenue without hiring teams, creative strategists (people who plan ad ideas) or scaling (growing) eventually leads to business collapse.
  • After the first milestone (an important achievement), progress needs steady endurance over a long period rather than a short burst of effort. Plan for sustained, consistent work instead of expecting early results to make the rest effortless.
  • Successful sellers treat the work as an ongoing game with no final victory. Once one level is reached, there is always a next level to improve.
  • Retreating to safe, low-risk activity after a mistake stalls growth.

Validation, praise and social media

1 min
  • Seeking validation (approval) from outside sources is unreliable, because once work is published you cannot control how people react. Expect to be misunderstood or ignored at times, and keep your focus on improving the work.
  • Going viral (spreading widely and fast) once builds confidence for repeating it. Early videos that get few views are normal, and continued testing usually leads to a viral result.

Procedure: the praise file

1 min
  1. Keep a praise file of positive messages, reviews, reader emails and kind feedback about your work.
  2. Delete hostile messages immediately instead of keeping them.
  3. When motivation drops, open the praise file and read a few entries.
  4. Use the file sparingly, and then return to work.

Daily habits for staying in the game

1 min
  • Work every day with discipline. Consistent effort is what produces results, and the praise file and quit note above are short tools for keeping that effort steady.
  • When you feel tense, return to the question of what you can act on now. Use the 24-hour window to keep your focus on today's work.
  • Make business decisions logically and respond to results with calm analysis.

Further details

1 min
  • Ad accounts can be shut down, and campaigns (ad runs) can stop working for periods. Keep applying the same method after setbacks, because consistency over several months is what produces the results.
