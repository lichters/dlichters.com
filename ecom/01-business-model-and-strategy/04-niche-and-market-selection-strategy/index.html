Niche and market selection strategy

Section 1.4 · 4 of 23 in this chapter · 18 min read

In short

  • Build one focused niche store, not a shop that sells everything. Shoppers trust a focused shop more, so it sells better.
  • Check demand, price range and suppliers before you commit to a niche.
  • Avoid seasonal products for your first store. Pick products that sell all year.
  • Watch shipping costs: heavy, large or cheap items often lose money.
  • Know your buyer first, then pick products. Plan for years, not weeks.
More key points
  • You do not have to love the products you sell.
  • Put your passion into building and growing the business.
  • Profit matters more than how much you like the products.
  • Your first store can be a stepping stone, not your final one.
  • A profitable first store pays for what you learn for the second.

Niche (a narrow group of products or buyers) versus general store (a shop selling many unrelated kinds of products)

4 min
  • Build a niche-specific store rather than one general store that mixes unrelated categories. Niche-specific means focused on one niche. This applies as of 2020.
  • Mixed general stores convert much worse. "Convert" means turning visitors into buyers.
  • Mixed general stores also make it hard to stand out from competitors.
  • Combining unrelated categories is usually not worth it.
  • Sources differ on the stage. A landing page (a single web page built to sell one offer) is a common way to test a product, and a general store is also common for testing in the current landing page environment.
  • A single proven product can later move to its own store. Use a general store to test, then move to a niche store once a product has proven itself.
  • Wayfair.com is an example of a general store that sells almost everything.
  • Well over 90% of its products are dropshipped. Dropshipping means the supplier ships each item straight to the buyer, so the shop holds no stock.
  • Wayfair grew into a billion-dollar, publicly traded company (a company whose shares anyone can buy on the stock market) with hundreds of employees.
  • Most dropshipping businesses build niche-specific stores instead.
  • Wayfair.com was earlier known as CSN Stores. It was renamed Wayfair about three years before this teaching material was made.
  • Its general-store model differs from the niche-specific stores most dropshippers build.
  • A narrow niche builds authority (trust as an expert) with shoppers.
  • This tends to raise conversion rates (the share of visitors who buy) and make paid ads (advertising you pay for) more profitable.
  • A store that tries to sell almost everything loses that focus and the advantage that comes with it.
  • Single-niche stores that meet the niche criteria (the rules a niche must pass) convert better and produce more profit per ad dollar than multi-niche stores. (2023)
  • Build a niche-specific store rather than combining unrelated niches. Focused stores convert better and make paid ads more profitable.
  • Add cross-sell accessories (add-on items sold with the main product) only when they are used together with the core product.
  • Products that people do not buy together belong on separate stores.
  • Keep the store focused on one niche, with only products that belong to that niche.
  • Add products from parallel niches (other niches at the same level) only if the research numbers stop matching. That is called niching up (widening to a broader niche).
  • Choose one product type rather than one single product, and build the whole store around it.
  • A focused niche lets the store establish credibility (trust) and authority in that category.
  • Do not build a store with only one product. A single-product store is not recommended.
  • A store that sells random, unrelated products, with no niche and no long-term plan, is a burner store (a quick-sale shop with no future plan). It looks built for quick sales, is unlikely to grow into a real business, struggles to earn trust, and is hard to sell later.
  • Narrow a new business to one niche category at the start, instead of keeping many product ideas open.
  • A single focus makes it much easier to gain traction (early attention and sales) and sustain growth. (2025-26)
  • Serve one identifiable group of customers (a group you can clearly describe), defined by a shared interest rather than by a single product.
  • This means you always have several profitable products to sell. You do not depend on finding the next winning item (a product that sells very well).
  • Focusing on one winning product creates a turn-and-burn business (a quick-profit shop that is dropped when its one product stops selling). It falls apart when the product's popularity fades.
  • Defining the niche by customer interests makes marketing easier and keeps the business stable.
  • Large e-commerce (online selling) companies such as Amazon cannot match the detailed service a niche business gives one customer group.
  • You do not need to beat Amazon's size to earn a good income, only a small share of its market.
  • Most winning dropshipping products are not tied to a narrow niche. They appeal to a broad audience, such as anyone who wants to look good or take care of their body.
  • Narrow niches tend to cap volume (limit how much can be sold). A very deep niche may only reach around 50 to 100 orders per day.
  • Sources differ on breadth. Broad-appeal products suit the product-winner search (the hunt for a product that sells well).
  • Niche-specific stores suit a lasting business. Use the broad product to find a winner, and the niche store to build on it.
  • Plan the store for the long term from the start.
  • A store focused on one product type is more valuable if you later want to sell it. It is also more likely to last for years.

Defining the audience first

2 min
  • When a product idea looks too competitive, change the focus from the product to the customer.
  • First define who buys, then decide which products they need. Niching down (making the niche narrower) on products alone does not change what the site says to the buyer.
  • Case: a general e-bike store (a shop selling many kinds of electric bikes) struggled for a long time with broad positioning (a wide, unfocused message).
  • Its owner narrowed the whole site, content and design to one buyer group: hunters who use e-bikes built for hunting.
  • The store reached about $5 million per year in revenue (money from sales), while most general e-bike stores in the same market earned little.
  • A niche does not need to be a single-brand or single-category niche.
  • If the products are sold on sites that also cover other niches, the niche is still fine. A sub-niche (a smaller niche inside a bigger one) can be split off and owned as its own niche. (2023)
  • Enthusiast (people who love a hobby) or hobby niches are not a required filter.
  • The highest-revenue businesses in this example were not hobby-driven. Niche choice should rest on other criteria such as search volume (how many people search for a product) and supplier availability (how many suppliers, the companies that stock or make the products, sell it). (2023)
  • Shopping for a store's niche should first target products people already search for.
  • This gives a store traffic (visitors) and sales as soon as it launches.
  • Once a store has traffic, an email list (a list of contacts you can email) and social reach (followers and views), new brands can be introduced to the store and promoted.
  • A market can be worth shortlisting (adding to a short list to check) even if you have no personal use for its products.
  • This is true when demand is clearly growing in your target country. Growth in a market is a valid reason to keep an idea in the running.
  • Do not niche into the example products often used to explain this model, such as chandeliers and stand-up paddle boards.
  • Many beginners pick them for that reason alone, and the market is crowded. (2023)
  • For a low-interaction business (one with few customer contacts) that can be automated (run by systems with little manual work) as far as possible, the upper middle class is the preferred target demographic (a group of people defined by income, age and similar traits).
  • Lower interaction means less customer service load. This lets the owner avoid being tied up in support work and build a lifestyle business (a small business run to fit the owner's life).

Goal and time horizon

1 min
  • Decide your goal before searching for products. The options are a quick short-term profit, a dropshipping business, or scaling (growing) a product you already have.
  • The goal determines which kind of product to look for.
  • Choose products and niches for a horizon (a time span) of three, five and ten years, not the next few months.
  • Temporary events such as COVID-19 should not decide the niche on their own.
  • Choose evergreen niches (products that keep selling for years, not just for one season), so the store has no natural end date. (2023)
  • You can sell the store later if you stop operating it. (2023)

Research sequence and first-niche pragmatism

2 min
  • Niche research works as a fixed sequence, where each stage depends on the one before it.
  • Finish a brainstorm (a list of ideas) of at least 50 niche ideas before running the validation tests (checks of whether a niche is worth it).
  • The validation tests cover: price points (the prices a product sells at), margins (the money left after costs), demand, seasonality (how demand rises and falls through the year), loyalty and competition.
  • A niche can be submitted for a verification review (a check of an idea before you spend time on it) before any time is invested in it. (2023)
  • Physical product niche ideas can be checked. The review tells you whether to proceed, pivot (change direction) or niche sideways (change the niche at the same level). (2023)
  • The first niche does not need to be perfect.
  • A viable (workable) but non-ideal niche will still generate profit and provide operational learning (learning how to run the shop day to day).
  • While the first store runs profitably, build a second store with superior niche research.
  • Continuous improvement and dual-store operation beat endless research with no launches.
  • When a niche meets the criteria but is slow, work with what you have before switching.
  • Add every supplier you can and make the most of those brands.
  • A store netting (earning after costs) about $2,000 profit per month is worth keeping and building on rather than starting over.
  • If a niche fails the criteria, add sub-niches.
  • If it still fails, narrow to a higher-level niche. (2023)
  • Choose a niche where one customer can be sold several different products over time.
  • Repeat sales should be built into the model from the start, not added later.
  • Build the business on a viable high-ticket product market (a market for expensive products that can sell well).
  • The marketing system only works on a viable market. If the niche or products are weak, marketing alone will not fix the business. Product validation (proof that a product sells) must come first.

Recurring revenue (repeat income from the same customers) and lifetime value (total money one customer brings over time)

1 min
  • Choose markets with recurring revenue potential. This means customers buy from you again at least once after their first purchase.
  • Recurring revenue is a core marketing concept for this model.
  • Avoid markets where a customer buys only once every five to ten years, because you must keep acquiring new customers (finding new buyers) to grow.
  • That approach is more expensive and yields lower profit, even though it can still work.
  • A business that sells a single high-ticket product bought once every 5 to 10 years, or never again, must keep acquiring new customers.
  • Such a business cannot market profitably to existing ones.
  • Growth is capped by how many new buyers enter the market. So such a niche is a weaker choice than one with repeat purchases.

Seasonality and evergreen choices

1 min

Seasonality checks and rules:

Google Trends (a free Google tool that shows search interest over time), 5-year view, minor fluctuations, stays above 50% of peak year-round (kegerator example, a fridge that holds beer kegs) Acceptable
Search volume drops to near-zero in the off-season Avoid
First store Avoid seasonal niches
Existing seasonal store, low months Use geo-targeted ads (ads shown only in chosen places) that reach only warmer states
Later store, off-season demand strong Consider a seasonal niche
  • Use Google Trends' 5-year view of search volume to assess seasonality.
  • Acceptable seasonality shows minor fluctuations (small ups and downs) without dropping significantly from peak (the highest point). The kegerator example stays above 50% of peak year-round.
  • Avoid a niche that drops to near-zero during the off-season (the quiet months).
  • Avoid seasonal niches for a first store, because revenue falls for part of the year regardless of execution (how well you run things).
  • For an existing seasonal store, use geo-targeted ads that reach only warmer states during the low months.
  • Avoid seasonal niches for the first store, even when off-season demand is strong.
  • Seasonal niches add demand swings and many extra moving parts. Start with an evergreen niche and consider a seasonal niche for a later store.
  • Avoid seasonal products for a first store.
  • Seasonal businesses take longer to reach a level that can replace a job, while a year-round product can stabilize income faster.
  • An evergreen brand can keep a stable core of products that sell all year.
  • It can add event-based campaigns (planned sales pushes), such as Mother's Day, to capture busier periods. Event products are timed to the calendar, while evergreen products carry the business between events.

Competition and saturation

1 min
  • Competition test: if more than 50% of the products on the first page of organic (unpaid) Google Shopping results (product listings in Google search) come from 10 or more different stores, competition for that product is considered too high.
  • A competitive niche is not suited to everyone.
  • More competition usually means a bigger market, and returns can be higher. But it demands more time, attention, money and patience.
  • A competitive niche needs more time, attention, money and patience. A bigger market usually means higher returns.
  • It is a poor fit for a store you intend to flip (buy and sell quickly) or sell within one year.
  • Brand loyalty (buyers sticking to the same brands) in a niche means only a few product types dominate, not a few stores selling many brands. (2024)
  • A niche with many brands and few stores is acceptable. (2024)
  • Sources differ on competition. The test in is a hard filter (a rule that rejects a niche).
  • A niche that passes it can still be competitive and demand more resources, as described in. Apply the test first, then judge the resources you can commit.

Mass desire (a want many people share and act on) and demand direction (which way buying interest is moving)

3 min
  • Advertising can only direct a desire that already exists among many people. It cannot create that desire.
  • Treat the job as finding a shared, already-active desire and channelling it (directing it) onto your product.
  • Do not run against a rising public demand.
  • A campaign that pushes a product in the opposite direction of a strong trend fails, even with a large budget. Follow the trend and pick the product angle (the way you present the product) that matches it.
  • Automotive examples (cars) show the rule:
    • In 1948 a manufacturer built a shorter, squatter car (short and low to the ground) with more interior room, while the public wanted longer, lower, wider cars. A multimillion-dollar campaign failed.
    • In the mid-1950s the market demanded horsepower (engine power), and brands that sold power sold well. A major brand that spent heavily on safety messaging saw no results. The following year it released its most powerful engines.
    • A good, inexpensive compact car (a small, low-cost car) backed by heavy advertising also failed when demand shifted sharply to cheap, simple, economical compacts.
  • A mass desire is a private want shared publicly by enough people to repay the cost of advertising, manufacturing, selling and profit.
  • A market is born when such a shared desire becomes large enough to be profitable. It can range from a few thousand people to tens of millions.
  • Rate each candidate mass desire on three dimensions before choosing it:
    • urgency and intensity (how strongly people need it)
    • staying power (how often it repeats and never gets satisfied for good)
    • scope (how many people share it) Choose the desire that scores highest on all three together.
  • A product usually satisfies two to four mass desires. But an ad can carry only one dominant desire at a time.
  • Choosing that desire is the most important decision in writing the ad. The headline (the big title line of an ad) depends on it. No later copy (written sales text) can rescue a wrong choice.
  • Mass desires come from permanent forces, such as instincts (basic human drives like attractiveness, health and virility, meaning manly vigor) and technological problems that remain unsolved.
  • Mass desires can also come from forces of change, such as trends, style shifts and mass education.
  • Permanent forces require a fresh way to claim the same benefit.
  • Changing forces require spotting the shift early and knowing when it becomes statistically significant (big enough to be real, not just chance).
  • Channel an existing mass desire onto your product in three steps:
    1. Choose the single most powerful desire that can be applied to the product, scored on urgency, staying power and scope.
    2. Acknowledge that desire, reinforce it, or offer the means to satisfy it in one statement in the headline. Start the headline with the product if the prospect (the person you want to sell to) knows it. Start with the desire if the prospect knows only the desire, and with the problem if the prospect knows only the problem.
    3. Show, step by step in the body copy (the written text under the headline), how each product performance inevitably satisfies that desire.
  • Supplements sell far more than gym services. A pill delivers the result with almost no time, effort or sacrifice.
  • A rough claim puts the supplement market at about ten times the fitness industry. This claim is not verified.

Price, margin and shipping viability

2 min
  • Use the average price (the total divided by the number of items) from Google Shopping results, not the median (the middle price when all prices are lined up).
  • The average gives a realistic view of the price points (exact prices) you will list.
  • Niches with an average order value (the typical amount one order is worth) of about $300 to $500 are a practical option for a store with limited cash.
  • Higher-ticket niches (niches with more expensive products) around $3,000 are also viable if you can cover the cost of one order.
  • The number of products in a niche and the price range matter more than the brands on marketplaces (big online sales sites).
  • A wide price range inside a niche is normal. For example, couches sell from about $150 to tens of thousands.

Heavy and large products:

  • Large and heavy products are viable if the price is high enough to cover shipping.
  • For example, a futon (a sofa that turns into a bed) at $300, a leather sofa at $1,500 and a sectional (a large L-shaped sofa) at $3,000 have similar shipping costs. So the cheapest one may leave no margin. (2024)
  • Large and heavy products only work when the selling price is high enough to cover freight (the cost of shipping heavy goods).
  • Shipping cost stays roughly the same whatever the item's price. A product selling for $200 leaves too little margin after wholesale cost (what you pay the supplier), card fees (charges from payment cards), ads and free shipping.
  • So cheap heavy items should not be sold or advertised. (2023)
  • Worked margin example (2023),:
Item Selling price Cost basis Left for the rest
Item with wholesale cost about 50% of selling price $200 about $100 wholesale about $100 for card fees, ads and shipping
Three-seater fabric sofa $1,500 $750 $750 for shipping and ads
Custom Italian leather sofa $15,000 not stated same shipping cost range as the $1,500 sofa
  • Within a heavy-goods niche, pick a sub-niche with enough price range to make money.
  • Examples are futons at $200 versus premium sofas at $1,500 or more. (2023)
  • Cheaper items in that niche should be kept off paid traffic (visitors who come from ads). They may be kept only for organic visitors (free visitors who come from search). (2023)
  • When shipping is expensive, as in Australia where a small envelope costs about $2, start with products small enough to fit in an envelope.
  • Products with complex installation can be sold.
  • But the operator (the person running the business) must plan installation support, which adds work.
  • Hands-off products (products that need little work from you) have less friction (small problems that slow things down). (2022)

Returns and return-rate risk

1 min
  • Returns on a low-margin dropship product (one with little profit left per sale) are often cheaper to absorb (to accept and pay for) than to build a returns system for.
  • This is true if the return rate (the share of orders sent back) stays around 5%.
  • Early on, accepting the loss and moving on can be the rational choice.
  • Stricter rules can be added later for customers who abuse returns, such as buyers ordering several clothing items and sending some back.
  • Case: a motorcycle gear brand sold products at around $120 with goods costing $70 to $80.
  • Returns were costly, so the margin was too thin.
  • A higher-ticket product needs a consultative sale (the seller advises the buyer before selling) and enough margin to absorb returns.
  • Apparel (clothing) has a return rate well above the e-commerce average, mainly because customers misjudge size or material.
  • Apparel stores should plan for higher returns and watch the return rate closely.
  • Large apparel retailers can absorb very high return rates. They own their brands and have a large database of repeat buyers (a list of customers who buy again).
  • A dropshipping store without these advantages cannot absorb the same return rate. So high-return categories are risky for it.
  • If you run a lifestyle dropshipping business with limited customer service time, avoid niches with high return rates.
  • Returns require constant customer service work that does not fit a lean operation (a business run with few people and little time).

Supplier availability and niche pivots

1 min
  • If your chosen niche has fewer suppliers than expected, pivot within the niche before starting over. (2023)
  • Expand, for example, from office chairs to office chairs plus desks, or office chairs plus ergonomic accessories. (2023)
  • Niching sideways often provides enough suppliers without abandoning your research. (2023)
  • Use the supplier check together with the verification review and the competition test.
  • A niche with few suppliers is not dropped automatically. It is first widened sideways.

Branded categories and B2B or B2C fit (B2B means selling to businesses; B2C means selling to shoppers)

1 min
  • Manufacturers of branded products spend hundreds of millions of dollars on marketing to create customer loyalty.
  • This spending makes it impossible for dropship stores to compete in those categories, even if the manufacturer approves dropshipping.
  • Manufacturer approval does not guarantee dropshipping viability (whether it can make money).
  • Even if Apple approved iPad dropshipping, you could not profitably compete. Major retailers like Best Buy and the Apple Store already own customer traffic and loyalty for those products.
  • Solo e-commerce operators (people running online shops alone) cannot profitably sell branded products.
  • They lack the hundreds of millions of dollars in annual marketing budgets that manufacturers like Sony and Bose deploy.
  • Branded categories require either manufacturer distribution partnerships (deals to sell directly with the maker) or equivalent marketing spend. Both are unavailable to individual operators.
  • A drop-shipping store is not directly comparable to a brand that makes its own products, such as a luxury label.
  • The goal is to be the best-known store for a niche's buyers, not to match a manufacturer's scale.
  • Renting products to end consumers (individual shoppers) online is a poor fit for a lifestyle business.
  • It creates repeated back-and-forth exchanges and support problems.
  • Renting to businesses, such as party tents or inflatable bounce houses, is workable.

Operator fit (how well you suit running the business) and passion

1 min

  • You do not need to be passionate about the niche (a narrow group of products or customers) that you sell.

  • Put your passion into building and growing the business.

  • Profitability (earning more money than you spend) matters more than your personal interest in the products.

  • Your first store does not have to be the final answer.

  • A profitable first store pays for the learning needed for the second store.
