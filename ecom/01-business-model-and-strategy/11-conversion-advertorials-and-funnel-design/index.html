Conversion, advertorials and funnel design

Section 1.11 · 11 of 23 in this chapter · 16 min read

In short

  • Fix the store before you buy visitors. Visitors who arrive at a weak store leave without buying.
  • Fix one stage of the sales path at a time. Start with the ads.
  • Every visitor needs a next step, whether they buy or not.
  • Expensive products need a cheap first step and content that builds trust.
This section has 8 parts. Tap one to open it.

Store conversion before traffic

3 min
  • Improve conversion before you buy traffic. Traffic means visitors who reach your store. Conversion means the share of visitors who buy. Your store now has real products and active suppliers, so this work has a solid base. Doing it first gives you the best chance when you later add more visitors.
  • Product pages (pages that show one product), offers (what you sell and on what terms) and checkout (the payment steps) must turn visitors into buyers. Only then send paid traffic (visitors you pay for through ads) or free traffic to the store. Otherwise most visitors leave without buying.
  • Launching a store with a few randomly uploaded products and then turning on traffic wastes ad spend (the money you put into ads). Visitors do not turn into customers. Finish conversion optimization before you buy traffic.
  • Ad spend alone does not fix poor results. Improve conversion and product choice before you increase spend. **
  • Facebook ads (paid posts on Facebook) only send people to the store. The store must close the sale. Expecting the ad to sell by itself is the wrong starting point when a store underperforms.
  • Use this order for a store that lasts: CTR first (click-through rate, the share of people who see an ad and click it), then a great offer, then a good store conversion rate, then scale.
  • Start ads once products are uploaded and the store is optimized. Then focus your work on the top 20 percent of product pages by traffic. **
  • Analyze the funnel in stages. A funnel is the path a customer takes from the first ad to the purchase.
  • Fix one part at a time, in this order: ads, then landing page (the first page a visitor sees) and store, then checkout, then customer service and supply chain (how products get from supplier to customer).
  • Move on only when the stage before it performs acceptably. A weak early stage makes later numbers meaningless.
  • Funnel optimization covers the whole path: first ad view, landing page, add to cart (putting a product in the cart), start of checkout and purchase.
  • Fix only the weakest stage at a time.
  • Do not judge the product before every stage has been checked and optimized.
  • Tell customers the delivery time clearly when the product ships from far away. If it takes about a month, customers need to know. This message can lower conversion.
  • Plan the cart for peak season (the busiest buying period) in this order:
    1. Decide which promotions run, such as free shipping thresholds (free shipping above a set order value), free gifts, discount codes or Buy More Save More (a name for a quantity discount).
    2. Set up the tiered progress bar (a bar showing how close the shopper is to the next reward) and announcement bar (a banner at the top of the site) so shoppers see the promotion rules.
    3. Check the recommendations block (a box suggesting other products) for markdown products (products with a reduced price).
    4. Enable subscription upgrades (offers to buy on repeat) and accelerated checkout buttons (one-tap payment buttons).
    5. Preview the cart on mobile and desktop before going live.
  • Direct-response ads (ads that push one action right away) push a discount at cold audiences (people who do not know your brand), such as 10% off with a shop-now button. These do not work for high-ticket products (expensive products).

The sales system as one loop

2 min

  • Think of the whole sales system as one loop. Every traffic source sends visitors to your Shopify store (your online shop), which is the hub where revenue (money coming in) is made.

  • Each visitor then enters a follow-up path that depends on whether they bought.

  • Every visitor ends in one of two outcomes: a purchase or no purchase.

  • Each outcome needs its own follow-up funnel (a set of steps that keeps contact going).

  • Then the visit does not simply end when the person leaves.

  • First, optimize the traffic channels that bring visitors in, so qualified visitors (people likely to buy) reach the Shopify store.

  • Post-purchase funnels (steps after a sale) and remarketing (showing ads again to people who visited before) come after that. They only work with visitors who already arrived.

  • The three-step affiliate funnel (you earn a commission by sending buyers to someone else's product):

    1. A static website (a simple, fixed site) that builds trust and drives traffic.
    2. A free bribe (a free gift) offered in exchange for an email address.
    3. An automated email follow-up sequence (a series of emails sent by itself) that builds trust and asks for the affiliate sale.

    The same bribe and autoresponder (an automatic email sender) techniques also increase sales on a regular dropshipping store (a store where a supplier ships the products to customers).

  • Case: A business wanted to reach people who were not searching for its products. It built several automated funnels with bribes and email sequences aimed at this new audience. Sales tripled in the first three months.

  • Lead magnets (free gifts that collect contact details), such as a free top-of-funnel ebook (an early-stage free e-book), can be sold cheaply, even for $5. People who are not ready to buy pay for it, and their contact details are collected.

  • The business then turns those leads (interested contacts) into buyers later in the funnel. It earns budget to spend on bottom-of-funnel ads (ads aimed at people ready to buy).

Single-product and collection pages

1 min
  • Single-product stores (stores that sell one product) can use the product page as the home page.
  • 90 to 95 percent of traffic goes to the product page anyway. The home page gets only 5 to 10 percent.
  • A separate home page is therefore inefficient (it wastes effort).
  • Single-product stores should prioritize the auto-expanded product details test (a test where product details open automatically).
  • It needs a small catalog (your full product list).
  • It involves only 3 setup clicks.
  • It answers the main worry of first-time buyers, who do not know the brand.
  • A collection-page strategy (a page showing several products) is useful. It gives the customer choice and avoids testing each product separately.
  • In one seller's stores, the split is about 60% collection pages and 40% single-item pages.
  • Single-item pages are used once a product is clearly a winner (it sells well).
  • Send ads for a product to a collection page showing several similar products, such as six variants (versions, like colors), instead of only one product page.
  • Pair this with a bundle offer (a deal for several items together) to sell more units per order and raise average order value (the average amount spent per order).

Entry points and offer ladders

3 min
  • An entry point is a cheap or free first step that lets new visitors enter. High-ticket products need one.
  • Examples: opt-ins (sign-ups for something free), quizzes, digital courses, free trials, free-plus-shipping offers (a free product where the customer pays only shipping), e-books and sale items.
  • Without an entry point, visitors who only see expensive products leave without buying.
  • Create a low-cost first purchase before the high-ticket item.
  • Examples: an accessory, a part for the main product, or a consumable (something used up, like refills).
  • Find this add-on with your suppliers. The goal is to move the customer from the first purchase to the main product naturally.
  • A ladder of offers (offers at rising prices) captures buyers at different price points.
  • Free content comes first, a low-ticket paid event or product (cheap) second, and a high-ticket offer (expensive) last.
  • Most people take the free step. A smaller share buys the middle offer. Only a rare few buy the top offer.
  • Each step gets sales from a different share of the audience.
  • A deeper funnel with more offers increases what each customer is worth. That raises what you can afford to spend to win one (customer acquisition).
  • The business that can outspend competitors on customer acquisition usually wins the market.
  • Plan funnel depth with acquisition costs in mind.
  • A sales funnel is the online sequence of pages and messages that moves a prospect (a possible buyer) up the value ladder (the ladder of offers above).
  • Bait (the free hook at the top) sits at the top. Each step narrows the audience to fewer, higher-value buyers.
  • The funnel turns the ladder into a working sales process.
  • Case: A fitness media company had 1.2 million Facebook followers but lost 90% of its traffic after platform changes.
  • It spent 116 USD to sell a product priced at 97 USD, so it was no longer profitable.
  • The real problem was not traffic or conversion. It was the missing value ladder and sales funnel. Customers had no clear next step after their first purchase.
  • After it added offers that let customers move up, it could pay two to three times more per sale. It outbid competitors for traffic and gained more customers and sales.
  • Study how successful brands in your niche (your narrow market) structure tiered offers (offers at several levels), bundle names and urgency tactics (time pressure, like "ends tonight").
  • Test versions of what converts for your competitors (other sellers in your market).
  • A free ebook bundled with a multi-unit offer (a deal for two or more items) serves two goals.
  • It adds perceived value (what the buyer feels it is worth).
  • It moves the buyer from one unit to at least two.
  • It works best when the ebook delivers real value on its own, so the gift does not feel like a sales pitch.
  • An offer like buy one get one free can drive strong click-through rates (CTR) and sales.
  • The store must have a reliable supplier that ships the exact product sold. Otherwise the store can collapse under refunds (money returned to customers).
  • The instant giveaway funnel (a funnel built around a free gift at once) is expected to stay effective for six to twelve months.
  • Then it saturates (wears out and stops working).
  • Plan a replacement strategy before that point.

Funnel stages and when to build a funnel

1 min
  • Funnel stages run: awareness (people learn you exist), consideration (they weigh options), conversion (they buy), loyalty (they return) and advocacy (they recommend you).
  • The TOFU, MOFU and BOFU labels mark the top, middle and bottom of the funnel.
  • Each stage loses people. Only a small share of people who see an ad ends up buying. That is why the funnel narrows.
  • Build a new funnel from scratch only when no working funnel exists.
  • Improving a funnel that already makes sales takes far less effort than building one.
  • Higher-ticket dropshipping products (around $200 to $300 and above) need more trust.
  • They also need consultative selling (helping the buyer decide through advice) and a longer customer journey.
  • Paid ads alone are expensive for such products. A warm-up content path (content that builds interest before the sale) is often needed first.
  • Warm-up funnel for a higher-ticket product:
    1. Create an advertorial (an ad written like an article) or educational landing page that addresses the customer's problem.
    2. Or publish a YouTube video explaining the problem and solution.
    3. Retarget viewers (show a second ad to people who already watched) who watched a set share of the video, for example 75%, with an offer.
    4. For more expensive items, first sell a lower-ticket item. Then move the customer into an email list and make the larger offer later.

Advertorials and editorials

3 min

  • An advertorial is an ad written like an article. An editorial is an article that teaches the reader.

  • Sending cold paid traffic (paid visitors who do not know you) straight to a product page asks visitors to trust a brand they have never seen.

  • Only visitors who already know their problem and want a solution tend to buy.

  • An editorial step (an article that teaches first) lets the store convert the rest of the paid audience.

  • Audience segments for a product, approximately:

    Segment Share of audience Role of editorial Source
    Impulse buyers who know their problem and look for a solution About 10-15% or less Go straight to the product page; product pages mainly convert this group
    Potential prospects who partly understand their problem, may have objections and trust little 35-70% Main group an editorial should convert
    Not interested in the product About 10-20% Editorial content is not worth aiming at this group

  • Plan editorial content around the three audience groups.

  • Impulse buyers (people who know their problem and look for a fix) go straight to the product page.

  • Potential prospects need the editorial.

  • The uninterested group is not worth targeting.

  • This keeps effort focused on the largest group that product pages miss.

  • Giving value before selling builds authority (trust in your expertise).

  • Trusting customers tend to accept higher prices more easily.

  • They also take upsells (a bigger or better version) and cross-sells (a related extra product) more easily. This can raise average order value.

  • Host editorials on your own website, not on third-party publishers (other people's sites).

  • Own-site editorials convert well and keep the traffic in your store.

  • Third-party placements mainly add authority, not conversions.

  • Third-party publisher placements (articles in other people's magazines or newsletters) in your niche can add authority and bring better-quality traffic.

  • The article can be reused on your store.

  • They usually require a publication fee (a payment). The publisher must also agree that the product and claims are good and accurate.

  • Sending cold ad traffic first to an advertorial that educates about the problem, and only then to the offer page, is presented as a way to raise conversion.

  • Visitors arrive more aware of the problem and more convinced of the solution. They are warmer buyers (more ready to buy).

  • Advertorial funnel flow:

    1. Ad with a relatable story about a problem the buyer recognizes.
    2. Click leads to an advertorial that educates about the problem and general solutions.
    3. A call-to-action button (a button telling people what to do next) leads to the offer page.
    4. Checkout.
    5. After purchase, an upsell page offers extra products at a discount.
    6. Visitors who started checkout but did not buy receive an abandoned-cart email (an automatic reminder about an unfinished cart).
    7. Advertorial visitors who did not click through to the offer page are retargeted with an ad that links directly to the offer page, not back to the advertorial.

  • Advertorials fit products where the buyer can be educated about a benefit or a problem before buying. An example is comfortable shoes linked to foot or body health.

  • They fit poorly for products with no educational angle (nothing useful to teach).

  • An advertorial funnel only makes sense for products around 40 to 50 dollars or more. At that price the buyer thinks harder about spending.

  • The return on ad spend (ROAS, the money earned for each dollar spent on ads) and margin (the profit kept per sale) can carry the extra steps.

  • A 5-dollar ebook does not justify it.

Creative strategy

3 min

  • Modern dropshipping shifted from making every ad yourself to adapting proven creatives (ad images and videos that already worked).

  • Start from ads that have already run for weeks.

  • Verify them in the ad library (Facebook's public list of running ads). Then adapt them to your product.

  • Use the Facebook Ad Library to find winning creatives (ads that sell well).

  • If the same creative appears in 10 to 15 ads, the advertiser is scaling it (spending more to grow it).

  • Adapt that creative to your market, for example with a French voice-over (spoken narration in French).

  • Build the creative strategy in this order:

    1. Research the target audience and customer psychology (what drives them) in depth.
    2. Research competitors and affinity brands (brands your buyers also like) in depth.
    3. Map best-selling products, current winning products and offers.

    Focus this research on finding marketing angles and ad angles (ways to frame your message).

  • You choose between speed (copy existing content and test it fast) and customization (build your own content and ads).

  • For a brand meant to grow, full customization is the recommended route.

  • Copied content gives no lasting edge and carries legal risk.

  • Brands can set their own red lines (rules they never break). Examples: never attacking competitors, not using certain pain points (problems the buyer feels), or not creating fictional UGC creators (made-up customers in videos; UGC means user-generated content).

  • Fewer restrictions usually give a stronger creative strategy.

  • More variations can be tested, and ad platforms look for that variety.

  • Limits from the niche, funding, existing team and required approvals decide which parts of a creative system a brand should build.

  • A brand may need only a few sections rather than the whole system.

  • The purpose of creative strategy is to make ads.

  • Any process, tool or system that does not contribute to producing ads has no value.

  • Split creative effort by output type.

  • Workhorses (everyday, lower-effort ads) are the main production focus. Warhorses (larger, higher-effort ads) are an occasional addition.

  • Relevance (how well an ad fits the viewer) and volume of low-effort ads drive most results.

  • Problem-focused video ads (videos that show a problem first, then the solution) can send viewers to a store.

  • The idea's author intended to test this with AI-made ads.

  • The results of such ads are only known after testing, so measure them rather than assume.

  • A new dropshipping brand has no brand equity (trust and recognition built over time).

  • Its ads must sell to strangers.

  • Strong products can sometimes sell with weak marketing. In competitive markets, a seller needs stronger persuasion (convincing messages) and craft (skill) to connect with buyers.

  • Copywriting (persuasive written or spoken words that sell) in e-commerce replaces the salesperson who is absent from online stores.

  • Its job is to walk the visitor through deciding whether the product fits their problem or desire.

  • It applies to product pages, sales pages, ads, emails and video scripts alike. **

  • Case: Security Rare Coin achieved a 3,400% increase in sales, from $3.5 million to over $120 million in a little over a year.

  • It came from copywriting built on strong bullets (short, punchy sales points) and fascinations (curiosity-triggering statements). These were based on control marketing (persuasion methods that take control of attention).

  • This shows how far systematic bullet writing (writing sales points by a set method) can scale when applied to product promotion.

AI-supported creative systems

2 min
  • Method for building a better offer and improving product naming with an AI assistant (an AI chat tool):
    1. Ask the AI to apply the Magic Headline Formula (a named method for writing headlines) to your product and produce several product name options.
    2. Ask it to write a product description using the same formula.
    3. Edit both outputs yourself to add a human touch, rather than copying them directly.
    4. Test the candidate names against each other to see which performs best.
  • Prompt (the text you give an AI) for a lean AI setup (a simple, focused set-up) for ad creation. Use it as written:
Create a lean AI setup that helps our brand make ads that are more effective and data-backed, and expand faster. Main functions: 1. Audience understanding: review surveys, personas and behavioral data; pinpoint genuine pain points, desires, objections and purchase drivers; detect new trends across audience segments. 2. Market and rival analysis: investigate competitors and where the industry is heading; find gaps and openings so the brand can stand apart and position itself more firmly. 3. Creative direction and brainstorming: produce fresh ad angles, hooks and creative ideas from customer findings; sharpen existing concepts by anchoring them in data and established marketing frameworks. 4. Scripts and copy: draft persuasive, high-converting ad scripts and marketing text; check and polish copy so it fits the audience's emotions, vocabulary and motives. 5. Ad results feedback cycle: assess performance (ROAS, CTR, hook rate, watch time, CPC and similar); explain why some ads succeed and others underperform; convert those lessons into practical guidance for the next batch of creatives. 6. Knowledge base linkage: treat all uploaded files and research as the core knowledge base; automatically apply insights and 'Breakthrough Advertising' principles to all output; keep answers tied to actual audience data. Value for the founder: turns customer feedback into clear, usable insights; proposes creative angles and strategies suited to the audience; speeds up writing better ads using real data; drives ongoing improvement by analyzing ad outcomes and closing the creative loop.
