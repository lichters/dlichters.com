Growth levers: AOV, repeat purchases and customer lifetime value

Section 1.12 · 12 of 23 in this chapter · 19 min read

In short

  • A store grows in three ways: more first-time buyers, more money per order, and more repeat buyers.
  • Selling again to an old buyer usually costs less and earns more than finding a new one.
  • Upsells and bundles add money to each order you already get.
  • Set up your money machines and promotions once, then measure them to see if they work.
  • Fix your profit margin (the share of each sale you keep) before you chase more orders.
More key points
  • Do not add surveys, feedback loops or one-click upsells yet.
  • First get the concept approved and make the business profitable.
  • Then share your P&L (profit and loss: money in minus money out) before adding extras.
  • Different sources can give different numbers. Check which case each number covers.

Three growth levers

1 min
  • A business grows in three ways: getting more first-time buyers, raising the value of each order, and getting existing customers to buy again and more often.
  • Check each of the three levers separately.
  • A store's growth has three levers.
  • Lever one: more first-time customers, through traffic (visitors who reach the store) or conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Lever two: higher average order value (AOV, the average amount spent per order), or more profit per order, through higher-priced products, bundles (several products sold together) or better margins (the share of each sale kept as profit).
  • Lever three: a higher repeat purchase rate (the share of customers who buy again).
  • Pulling one lever alone gives linear growth (steady, straight-line growth).
  • Pulling all three at once gives exponential growth (growth that speeds up).
  • Any e-commerce business (an online store that sells goods) can grow in only three ways.
  • The three ways: attract more first-time customers or traffic, raise the average order value, or raise the rate of repeat purchases.
  • Each customer who buys again adds to growth without a new acquisition (without paying to win a new buyer).
  • The third lever is selling more than once to the same customer.
  • Repeat sales from existing customers are a separate growth path from acquiring new ones.
  • Focus on lifetime value (LTV, the total money one customer spends with you over time) before average order value (AOV).
  • First, identify the channel (a place that brings customers, such as an ad platform) or action that makes the most money.
  • Push it until its return stops growing, before you move resources to another channel.

Lifetime value and repeat customers

2 min
  • Customer lifetime value (CLV, the same idea as LTV, for one customer) and lifetime value (LTV) decide long-term success.
  • Winning back existing customers costs far less than winning new ones.
  • So focus on repeat customers (people who buy again), not just on the number of first orders.
  • Do not treat dropshipping (selling products you never stock, because the supplier ships them directly) as a quick cash grab where customer experience does not matter.
  • Running out of new customers without retention systems (ways to keep buyers coming back) leads to business collapse.
  • Running a successful business centers on creating exceptional customer experiences that build satisfaction.
  • Satisfied customers become repeat buyers and advocates (people who recommend you to others).
  • Selling again to an existing customer is usually easier and more profitable than acquiring a new one.
  • If repeat sales need no extra marketing cost, the profit margin on the second or third sale is often higher.
  • Repeat customers cost nothing extra to acquire again.
  • You can email repeat customers directly.
  • They already trust your business.
  • This makes repeat sales cheaper and more profitable than finding new buyers each time.
  • Recurring revenue (money that comes back on a regular basis) means the same customer buys from you more than once.
  • Repeat business is a core way to grow.
  • Repeat sales are easier to close (easier to sell), cost less to market, and usually carry higher profit margins on the second and third order onward.
  • A store that sells only a single one-time product must keep finding new buyers.
  • It cannot market profitably to existing customers.
  • Its growth is capped far below that of a store that can sell to the same customers again and again.
  • Large e-commerce companies such as Amazon grow mainly because customers come back to buy other products.
  • Without repeat purchases, they would exhaust their market (run out of buyers) and stagnate (stop growing).
  • The same risk applies to a small store.
  • Returning customers usually contribute about 20% to 30% of revenue (money coming in from sales) in dropshipping.
  • In good cases, they contribute up to 40%.
  • Subscription boxes (a plan where the customer pays regularly and receives items automatically) and consumable products (items that get used up and bought again) can reach much higher repeat rates.
  • In most niches (narrow, focused markets) more than 80 percent of store visitors are first-time visitors.
  • So repeat purchase rates stay low.
  • Plan customer acquisition for new buyers.
  • To add value per customer, use upsells (offering a bigger or extra item) and cross-sells (offering a related item).
  • Also use referral discounts for family and friends.
  • Also use affiliate content (links to other products where you earn a commission) for indirectly related products.
  • The 20% to 30% figure measures share of revenue.
  • The 80% figure measures share of visitors.
  • Both can hold at the same time in one store.

Customer multiplier funnel

1 min
  • A customer multiplier funnel (a series of steps aimed at existing customers) has two goals.
  • Goal one: sell more products after the first purchase.
  • Goal two: turn customers into people who bring in other customers.
  • It is not a paid advertising method such as pay-per-click (paying each time someone clicks your ad).
  • Upselling is the first stage of a customer multiplier funnel.
  • Onsells and resells follow the same logic.
  • They aim at a second purchase from customers who have already bought once.
  • So revenue per existing customer rises.
  • Upsells and resells happen only when they are deliberately planned and systematically executed.
  • Repeat purchases cannot be left to chance or to customer loyalty alone.
  • Some funnel actions take a long time to pay off.
  • Others can be set up once and then keep producing results.
  • Immediately actionable steps (steps you can start right away) can be put in place within days or weeks.
  • For most businesses that already make sales, have no other major problems, and can implement these actions, a customer multiplier funnel is expected to add 10 to 20 percent in additional revenue per month.
  • Formula: Expected additional monthly revenue from a customer multiplier funnel = current monthly revenue × 10% to 20% Example: at 100,000 USD monthly revenue, the funnel is expected to add 10,000 to 20,000 USD per month.
  • Once a customer has made a purchase, run three follow-up sequences (automatic series of messages) at the same time.
  • Sequence one: a brand ambassador program (customers who promote the brand for rewards).
  • Sequence two: lead value optimization offers (explained below) for related products or services.
  • Sequence three: email campaigns that bring buyers back to the store.
  • Set these up after the first sale, rather than treating any one of them as a separate tactic.

Upsells, cross-sells and expanding the offer

2 min
  • Expanding the offer means adding more products to a store.
  • It also means building better offers, such as bundles, upsells and cross-sells.
  • The goal is a higher average order value and more bottom-line profit (profit left after all costs).
  • Complementary products (items that go well together) sold next to a main product increase the chance the customer buys the second item.
  • They work either at cart level (added in the shopping cart) or as a bundle.
  • The stronger the link between the products, the higher this chance.
  • Example: pillowcases and hair accessories for the same audience.
  • After finding a winning product (one that sells well), sell its different variants (versions, such as colors or sizes).
  • Also sell cross-sells of the winning product.
  • This raises average order value.
  • Each additional item in a single order improves profit per customer.
  • Upsells and cross-sells raise average order value and margin from a customer who is already buying.
  • So they suit any store, including one with a single product.
  • Add them at some level, even when the store only sells one item.
  • Lower-priced accessories can be added to an expensive main product.
  • This raises the average order value.
  • It also reaches a broader customer base at a higher margin.
  • Pellet grill accessories (add-ons for grills that burn wood pellets) can serve as upsells and downsells (a cheaper offer shown after the customer says no).
  • Utensils or cedar planks can serve as lead magnets (a free item offered in exchange for contact details).
  • A lead magnet offer sent to a content page (an article or blog page) can explain the benefits of owning a pellet grill to someone who has a normal grill.
  • Selling one product only is fine.
  • Selling several products can create trust and enable bundles.
  • Monitor which products competitor stores sell most.
  • Test bundles based on those winners.
  • A niche store can run ads on one winning product.
  • At the same time, promote other products to existing customers through email.
  • Adding products to the same niche can be a second step, after the current winner has been scaled (grown bigger).

Money machines

1 min
  • A money machine is a repeatable process that turns prospects (people who may buy) into customers, or deepens customer relationships.
  • It is designed to run on autopilot (by itself).
  • It multiplies revenue through lead capture (collecting contact details), upselling, cross-selling, retention (keeping customers) and remarketing (showing ads to people who already visited).
  • A money machine is an automated process that takes people in and pays money out.
  • It is bolted onto the core store, which stays the main sales engine.
  • Five types cover the customer journey (the path from first visit to after purchase).
Type Stage of the journey
Lead generation Before the store
Cross-sell Product pages
Upsell Checkout
Post-purchase After buying
Remarketing After leaving the site, whether or not the visitor bought
  • A money machine should lift average order value by about 5 to 10 percent.
  • Example: a store with a 1,000 dollar AOV should be able to reach roughly 1,050 to 1,100 dollars.
  • Money machines are built to raise average order value, customer lifetime value and net profit per customer (profit left after costs, per customer).
  • Most run on autopilot once set up.
  • Each one needs a stated setup time and budget before you decide when to build it.
  • Setting up a lead generation machine:
    1. Write the lead magnet content in a Google Doc.
    2. Outsource the design and add footers (the bottom strip of a page) and links.
    3. Build the landing page (a single page made to collect sign-ups) with the free form or a builder.
    4. Connect the sign-up to Klaviyo (an email marketing tool) and build the flow (an automatic email sequence).
    5. Send traffic from blog banners, sponsorships, Facebook ads or influencers.
    6. Email at least twice a week, and check the Klaviyo revenue after one to two months.

Lead value optimization (LVO)

1 min
  • Lead value optimization (LVO, earning more from each visitor over time) means increasing what each customer is worth over time.
  • It works through cross-selling related products, affiliate offers and promotions throughout the year.
  • LVO is a separate lever from referral traffic (visitors sent by other people's recommendations).
  • It can be combined with referral traffic.
  • Lead value optimization means earning more from every visitor, whether or not they buy the core product (the main item).
  • Conversion rate optimization (CRO, getting more visitors to buy) focuses on getting visitors to buy the core product.
  • Start lead value optimization only after the store has real sales.
  • That is when the extra offers start to pay off.
  • High-spending competitors on top paid search placements (the top spots on search ads) can stay profitable.
  • They stay profitable because they earn more from each lead than from the core product sale alone.
  • If a lead costs about 3 dollars per click, the store needs back-end or side-funnel revenue (money earned after the first sale, or from a side offer) from that lead.
  • That revenue covers the cost.
  • Cross-promoting your own niche stores is the easiest way to raise the value of each lead.
  • Buyers who just purchased are the most receptive to another offer.
  • Most stores with strong sales still skip this step.
  • Yet it reuses an existing audience at almost no cost.

Consumables and subscriptions

2 min
  • High-margin consumables (items used up and bought again, with a high profit share) are the most valuable product type for repeat revenue.
  • If a consumable such as a filter or accessory can be added to the business, add it quickly by talking to the supplier (the company that makes or ships the product).
  • If no consumable fits, sell a warranty instead.
  • Consumable products such as protein powder give buyers a reason to reorder.
  • A customer won through a low-cost entry product can become worth much more over time than a one-time buyer.
  • That is why a break-even front-end sale (a first sale that just covers its cost) can still be profitable.
  • At a low unit price, a single-item order rarely pays for paid acquisition (ads paid to win customers).
  • So a consumable product needs bundles or a subscription to raise average order value.
  • Repeat purchases of a consumable make the customer worth far more than the first order.
  • A typical adult uses roughly 17 to 20 bars of soap per year.
  • Some sources cite up to 26 bars.
  • For conservative (cautious) planning, use 17 units per year.
  • Consumable products that customers reorder, such as filament (plastic thread for 3D printers), can be sold on a subscription basis.
  • A subscription basis creates recurring revenue.
  • Monthly recurring revenue (MRR, the same money coming in each month from subscribers) comes from subscription systems and recurring models.
  • MRR is the step that takes a store beyond one-time sales.
  • A subscription app such as Recharge (a tool that runs subscriptions) signals a subscription-based model.
  • So does the Shopify subscription payments app (Shopify is the software that runs online stores).
  • Both are used on a competitor store.
  • When you model recurring revenue, check how the competitor sells its subscription.
  • Subscription upgrades in the cart raise customer lifetime value.
  • They turn one-time buyers into subscribers.
  • Buy More Save More (a discount for buying bigger quantities) raises average order value.
  • It does this by encouraging customers to put more quantity into each order.
  • Peak sales periods such as Black Friday (the big shopping day after Thanksgiving) and Cyber Monday (the online sale day after it) bring much more traffic.
  • So cart features that convert visitors quickly become more important during those weeks.
  • Letting shoppers upgrade a one-time item to a subscription, even after it is in the cart, is a key example.

Loyalty

1 min
  • High-ticket stores (stores that sell expensive products) can still get repeat purchases.
  • This works if the catalog (the full product list) includes several products a customer might buy at different times.
  • A loyalty program adds a reason to come back.
  • It does this by giving customers a reward they can spend on their next order.
  • When a customer does a favor for the brand, such as posting a photo or writing a review, they become more connected to the brand.
  • They also remember the experience more.
  • They are more likely to buy again and recommend the store.
  • The request itself therefore builds loyalty, not only content.

Referral programs

3 min
  • Referrals (customers recommending you to others) compound over time.
  • If each customer brings in another customer, sales and revenue roughly double in theory.
  • In practice, only some customers will refer others.
  • The effect grows as more new customers send more customers.
  • Recommendations from a customer's friends or family are trusted more than a business's own advertising.
  • Customers who refer others bring in new business at a much lower direct cost than paid marketing.
  • A post-purchase referral system (one that starts after the buyer's purchase) can start from the very first sale.
  • It only needs a customer who has already bought.
  • It turns a normal one-way sales funnel into an hourglass (a shape with traffic flowing both in and out).
  • It does this by sending buyers back out as a new traffic source.
  • The referral approach is not an affiliate or paid-referrer program (paying people for sales they send).
  • It is also not a multi-level structure (a setup where people earn from the people they recruit).
  • Buyers are not paid to recruit.
  • The goal is to give every customer a strong experience.
  • Then ask for specific actions that naturally lead to word of mouth (people talking about you).
  • Referral traffic is not expected to be the largest traffic source.
  • It usually will not make up most of the revenue.
  • Word of mouth is still very effective.
  • The big advantage: the system only has to be set up once, and then it runs on its own.
  • Choose the post-purchase ask based on product type and buyer profile (the kind of person who buys).
Product type Suitable ask
Products people like to show off (trendy, cool, hobby or design items) Photo and social post requests
Products people do not want to show publicly (medical supplies, wheelchairs, products for an older demographic) Review requests
Mixed range Many stores use both
  • Customers who feel well looked after are more likely to recommend a store to others.
  • A referral program adds a deliberate nudge (a small push) on top of good service.
  • Rewarding each successful referral costs little extra.
  • It makes the recommendation more likely.
  • Set up a referral program for the store.
  • Reward every successful referral.
  • Customers must know about the reward before they refer anyone.
  • A referral program can run as a separate system.
  • Or it can be integrated with the loyalty program.
  • How the two work together depends on the tool chosen.
  • Referral rewards can be loyalty points (points you collect and exchange) redeemable for future purchases.
  • A referral reward can also be a discount on the store, or cash.
  • Each option suits a different business model.
  • Choose deliberately, not by habit.
  • There is no single right way to reward referrals.
  • One example is a cash affiliate payout (money paid to partners who send sales) made once every 30 days.
  • That payout covers referrals from the previous 30 days.
  • Choose the reward structure while configuring the app (setting up the software), based on what fits the business best.
  • Expect to test and adjust it over time, as with any marketing activity.
  • Create a dedicated referral page on the website.
  • It should explain how to sign up and how to refer others.
  • Link this page in the site footer (the bottom strip of every page), so customers can always find it.
  • Referral program procedure:
    1. Implement a referral program for the store.
    2. Pick a referral tool, exploring the available options and choosing the one that fits best.
    3. Set up the chosen tool in the business.
    4. Build the referral emails into the email marketing system.

Promotions calendar logic

2 min
  • The growth plan has three parts that run together over 12 months.
  • Part one: build audiences (groups of people who may buy).
  • Part two: create offers that are hard to refuse.
  • Part three: promote those offers to the audiences.
  • The aim is more sales and profit that you can predict.
  • Plan monthly promotions so they add roughly 20 to 30 percent of monthly revenue on top of regular sales.
  • This gives a sales floor (a base level of sales) that still works if paid ads are turned off.
  • It also works if organic traffic (free visitors who come from search, not ads) drops.
  • The system is meant to add 20 to 30 percent to monthly revenue.
  • Treat that figure as a target to measure against, not a guaranteed result.
  • Dropping monthly promotions after one underperforming offer (an offer that sells too little) is a mistake.
  • Consistent testing shows which offers a given audience buys.
  • The contact pool (the list of people you can reach) grows with each month.
  • Shoppers who did not buy during dynamic remarketing (ads that show visitors the products they viewed, about 7 to 30 days) still form a warm pool (people who know you and are close to buying).
  • Shoppers who did not buy after an abandoned-cart email flow (emails sent to people who left items in the cart, up to about 9 days in Klaviyo) also form a warm pool.
  • Monthly offers are a way to reach that pool again.
  • Promotions aimed at existing audiences should not be started on day one.
  • They become worthwhile once traffic, remarketing audiences and the email list have grown.
  • Core traffic sources such as Google Ads, Facebook Ads, Microsoft Ads and SEO (free visibility in search engine results) should come first.
  • Peak periods such as Black Friday and Cyber Monday are the weeks when cart-level conversion features matter most.
  • Next steps for the growth plan:
    1. Write down at least 4 offers you can promote to your audience.
    2. Assign them on a rotating basis across the next 12 months.
    3. Create offer assets: emails and ads for each offer.
    4. Schedule the offers in Klaviyo, Google and Facebook.
    5. Modify future offers based on the results of each monthly promotion.
    6. Track whether the system adds 20 to 30 percent to monthly revenue.

Margin improvement priorities

1 min

  • Profit margin is the most common financial problem for dropshipping stores once they reach a certain size.

  • Growing revenue without fixing margin multiplies the problems along with the sales.

  • Growing revenue at a 9 percent net margin (profit left after all costs, as a share of sales) brings little extra profit for a lot of extra effort.

  • So raising gross margin (profit left after paying for the product, as a share of sales) is usually the better lever.

  • Example: a flat-revenue year (the same sales as before) with 20 percent gross margin would be worth more than doubling sales at the current margin.

  • More orders means more work and more operational risk (the chance that running the business goes wrong).

  • Margin gains bring cash without extra workload.

  • Choose improvements that raise profit per order before spending on sales growth.

  • Margin improvement priorities, in order:

    1. Find new suppliers and product categories with higher margins, and add only products that clear the minimum margin including shipping.
    2. Renegotiate pricing with the current top suppliers, using the volume they receive from the store as leverage.
    3. Renegotiate shipping costs with suppliers or freight providers.
    4. Test price increases on products where a small volume loss is acceptable.
    5. Limit or stop discounts, including custom quotes.
    6. Move marketing and SEO to maintenance mode and keep the current sales level while putting energy into margin.

  • Stores that scale (grow bigger) often run out of capacity to take new orders.

  • They then stop adding suppliers and products.

  • The fix: keep adding products, but choose suppliers selectively by margin.

Discounts and offers

1 min
  • A discount shrinks margin.
  • But losing a customer you could have won usually costs more.
  • A deal can be the right call when it closes a sale that would otherwise go to a competitor.
  • Every sale you make also limits your competitors' budgets.
  • They cannot outspend you on marketing without revenue.

Sequencing and guardrails

1 min
  • Do not start these extras yet:
    • Post-purchase surveys (short questions sent to the buyer after a sale).
    • Feedback loops (ways to collect customer opinions and act on them).
    • One-click upsells (an extra offer the buyer can add with one click).
  • Wait until the concept is approved and the business is profitable.
  • Share your P&L (profit and loss: money in minus money out) before moving to these steps.
  • First comes concept approval and profitability. Then comes customization.
