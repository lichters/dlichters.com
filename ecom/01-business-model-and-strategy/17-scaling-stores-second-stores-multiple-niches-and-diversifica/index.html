Scaling stores: second stores, multiple niches and diversification

Section 1.17 · 17 of 23 in this chapter · 13 min read

In short

  • Build and profit from one store first. Only then open a second one.
  • Open a second store only when the first is close to its limit and runs with little help. Sources give different checklists for this.
  • Scaling (growing a lot bigger) needs about 50 hours a week for roughly a year, more cash and more risk. Decide first if you can accept that.
  • Keep each store in one focused niche (one narrow product area). Do not mix unrelated products.
This section has 12 parts. Tap one to open it.

Deciding whether to scale at all

1 min
  • Scaling means growing a business much bigger.
  • In drop shipping (suppliers ship products straight to customers, so you hold no stock), scaling to $40,000 to $60,000 or more in monthly net profit (money left after all costs) takes a lot of effort.
  • It typically needs about 50 hours of work per week for roughly a year.
  • Scaling costs more cash than running a single store. Traffic (visitors to your store) and self-branding (building your own brand name) cost much more money.
  • Scaling also carries a bigger risk.
  • Growing a store with five-figure monthly sales (between $10,000 and $99,999 per month) tenfold rests on three levers: partnerships, branding and an X factor (one special edge that sets you apart).
  • Before you commit, decide whether the higher time need, the cash need and the risk are acceptable.

Scaling the first store before adding another

1 min
  • Start with one niche (one narrow product area) and one store.
  • Bring that store to stable profit first.
  • Only then add a second store.
  • Launching several stores at once multiplies beginner mistakes.
  • Build one store first and make it profitable.
  • Only then build the second and third store.
  • Do not launch several niche stores at the same time.
  • Build and make the first store profitable before building a second or third store.
  • If the basic setup is done and the niche criteria (the rules for picking a good niche) are followed, the first store has no obvious reason not to work.
  • It still needs real sales and profit first.
  • Once an X factor is found, the best way to improve results is to improve what already works.
  • Keep testing new ideas. A store is never finished.
  • Adding new suppliers or products can sharply increase sales.
  • Knowing your industry better than your competitors helps you choose which X factor experiments are worth running.
  • Test one idea at a time and keep learning.
  • Do not run a single campaign (one set of ads aimed at one goal) and just hope for results.
  • Focus on one store.
  • Optimize it (improve it step by step) as far as possible before opening more stores.
  • Multiple stores tend to be neglected.
  • Grow one niche store until it is maxed out (at its full limit) before you open a second store.
  • Growing an existing store is easier than building a new one.
  • A store that is not actively improved will decline.
  • The decline can be slow, taking years.
  • Keep adding products and optimizing the store so that it keeps growing.

Maintenance-mode criteria: when a store can support a second one

2 min

Sources describe maintenance mode (the store is set up and only needs upkeep) in different levels of detail. The versions are listed below as given.

2019 version:

  • A store should only expand to a second store when the first is in maintenance mode.
  • All suppliers must be approved (accepted as sellers).
  • Products must be uploaded and optimized.
  • Google ads (paid ads on Google search) must run with biweekly optimization (improvements every two weeks).
  • Email sequences (automatic emails sent in order) must be set up.
  • Facebook remarketing (ads shown again to people who already visited) must be in place.

2021 version, ten-point check before opening store two:

  1. Are you approved with all gold and silver suppliers (the top supplier levels) in your niche?
  2. Are all of their products uploaded to your store?
  3. Are your product pages optimized (improved so visitors buy)?
  4. Is your customer support, by live chat (a chat box on the site), email and phone, as good as it can be?
  5. Are your traffic sources diversified across Google, Facebook, Bing and SEO (free results in search, without ads)?
  6. Have you scaled your ad spend (raised how much you spend on ads)?
  7. Do you have email marketing flows for different scenarios (automatic emails for different situations)?
  8. Do you run monthly promotions to your social audiences and email list?
  9. Do you create content (the posts and articles you publish) and have a loyal following?
  10. Are you objectively better than your competitors, with a clearer unique selling proposition (USP, the clear reason to buy from you) and a better buying experience?

2025 version:

  • Start a second store only when the first one is maxed out.
  • All supplier approvals available at this stage must be obtained.
  • All their products must be uploaded.
  • Product pages must be optimized to convert (turn visitors into buyers).
  • Ad campaigns must be actively optimized for the best return on ad spend (ROAS, how much you earn back for each dollar spent on ads).

Comparison of the three versions:

Version Number of conditions Source
2019 Five: suppliers approved, products uploaded and optimized, Google ads with biweekly optimization, email sequences, Facebook remarketing
2021 Ten, as listed above
2025 Four: supplier approvals obtained, products uploaded, pages optimized to convert, ads optimized for return on ad spend
  • A store is growing on its own when these four things rise: direct visits (people typing your web address), referral traffic (visitors sent from other sites), organic search traffic (free visitors from search results) and repeat purchases (customers buying again).
  • This happens without the ad budget (money for ads) being increased each month.
  • A good Google search position alone does not show that the store is the dominant player (the biggest seller) in its niche.

Exception to one-store-at-a-time:

  • Several stores can be launched and grown at the same time, instead of selling one store before starting the next (2025).
  • Parallel work (working on several stores at once) is easier in related niches, because the audience of one store can be cross-promoted (advertised to) on the others.
  • Parallel projects divert focus (pull attention away). Optimizing a store that already works is a better use of time than researching suppliers for a new store.
  • In practice, the 2025 source allows parallel launches but treats them as a higher-risk choice. This is why most sources keep one-store-at-a-time as the default.

Related versus unrelated niches

1 min

Starting with an unproven market: A market is proven when stores in it already sell well on their own.

  1. Build one store that sells both product lines (two groups of products) when neither market is proven as a standalone market.
  2. Once sales start, track which products sell better.
  3. Pick the stronger niche and make it the main focus of the site.
  4. Keep the other products only as complementary items (extra items that support the main ones).
  • Run separate stores for two niches only when each market is already proven as a standalone market with successful stores.
  • Otherwise, combining them in one store is the safer test.
  • Do not mix unrelated products together just to add variety.
  • A combined store is a temporary test to find the stronger niche.

Unrelated categories:

  • Products from unrelated categories, for example furniture and home appliances, should run as separate stores, each made profitable on its own.
  • Once established, the stores can cross-link (add links between them) and refer customers to each other using lead value optimization (a way of targeting visitors by their likely value).
  • The 2019 combined-store test and the 2022 separate-store rule fit together. The combined store is a temporary test while a market is unproven. Separation follows once the markets are proven or clearly unrelated.

Product groups and category authority:

  • Build a separate store for each product group that customers would not buy in the same visit, so each store becomes the authority (the go-to expert) for its category.
  • Example: paddle boards and surfboards should be separate stores, while paddle boards and their paddles, wax and add-ons can share one store.

Keeping a niche store focused:

  • Keep a niche store focused.
  • If its niche passed all tests, do not expand it with unrelated products.
  • Sell other product types in related stores instead.

Store networks and structure

1 min
  • One domain (one web address) equals one Shopify store (an online shop built on the Shopify platform).
  • You cannot run multiple stores from a single domain.
  • If high-end brands reject you because your store already carries lower-tier brands, open a separate spin-off store (a new store split off from the first).
  • The spin-off store carries only the higher-end products in the niche.
  • Keep it under the same company, but give it its own store name, so the lower-tier store does not hurt your approvals.
  • A second store in a related niche can cross-promote to the audience of the first store.
  • Stores in related niches can cross-promote to each other's audiences, which makes running them in parallel easier.
  • Keep a backup store ready, and switch traffic links to it if the main store is shut down.
  • Once established, stores can cross-link and refer customers to each other using lead value optimization.
  • This applies to stores in unrelated categories.

Overhead of running several stores

1 min
  • Multiple stores create duplicated overhead (extra costs and work that repeat for each store).
  • Marketing systems, bank accounts, email marketing, VAs (virtual assistants, hired helpers), phone numbers and phone systems must be separated per store.
  • Running many stores also spreads focus and tends to slow the growth of each store.
  • So growing one store to its full potential is generally recommended before starting another.
  • Starting a second store while the first one is not working doubles processes such as customer service and splits focus.
  • This doubles the problems instead of solving them.
  • Running several stores early splits attention, and mistakes in the first store get repeated in the second.
  • Building several stores at once multiplies the same setup errors.
  • Fixing one mistake on one store before repeating the process is cheaper.

Focus versus many stores

1 min
  • The largest stores in the examples generally ran one or two stores.
  • The highest-revenue stores were focused on a smaller number of stores.
  • So concentrated focus is linked with high earnings.
  • The choice between many small stores and one large store also depends on the owner's preference, including whether they enjoy building or prefer to build, sell and hold.
  • Running several stores in different niches is possible, but focusing one store more heavily is a sensible longer-term plan.
  • The other stores should still be maintained and improved while the main niche gets more budget.
  • When a broad store is hard to make work, narrow the existing store to one customer segment (one specific group of buyers) instead of launching a second store.
  • A second store almost never fixes a struggling first store.

True diversification and risk spreading

1 min
  • Real diversification (spreading money and risk across different things) means running different business models (ways of making money).
  • Examples include e-commerce combined with real estate, car washing, RV rental, affiliate marketing (earning commission for sales you send) or information products (selling knowledge).
  • Multiple stores in the same business or model usually suffer together when demand for that model or e-commerce (online selling) drops.
  • Selling through a single traffic source (one place that sends visitors) or a single supplier is a business risk.
  • Use several traffic sources such as Google Shopping (product ads in Google search), Google Ads and Facebook, plus many brands.
  • This way, one blacklisting (being banned) or closure does not stop the business.
  • Stable year-round revenue from one store can act as a safety net (a backup for hard times) while you build the next store.
  • There is no fixed answer on whether to focus on one product, one channel and one market, or to diversify.
  • The seller's approach was to first improve the single product and its Facebook strategy, then differentiate to another channel (a different way of reaching buyers).

Relaunching a store

1 min
  • If an old store never ran ads and its products are back in stock (available to sell again), you can restart in the same niche.
  • You can restart under the same LLC (a US company type) and supplier accounts, applying the skills you have gained since.
  • Pausing for inventory reasons (stock) does not damage supplier relationships (2023).
  • A previously successful store can be relaunched instead of rebuilt from zero.
  • Rebuild the advertising campaigns from scratch and do not reactivate the old campaigns (2025).

Lean starts for a new store

1 min
  • Once a store runs consistently, a second niche can be launched faster by cloning the site (copying its setup).
  • Change the logos (brand images), names and products to the new niche's.
  • Use suppliers already approved.
  • This avoids repeating supplier applications from scratch.
  • Do this only after the first niche is stable (2023).
  • Keep the full niche list after choosing one niche.
  • An approved niche can later be used for a second store without repeating the research.
  • But recheck its data if enough time has passed.
  • Once the basics of the first store are maximized, grow in this order (no year given):
    1. Replicate the same products on another sales channel such as eBay (another online marketplace), connected to the same Shopify feed (the product data file that keeps your store updated), provided the supplier agrees.
    2. Once the first store is maximized, start a second store.
    3. Use a proven store blueprint (a ready template of a working store) or evergreen course (training that stays useful over time) to replicate the setup quickly.
    4. Keep the setup updated when platforms such as Google Ads change their interface (screen layout).

Examples and outcomes

1 min
  • Case, Source C (no year given): A seller built a one-product store on Facebook ads over about one and a half years.
  • They started at roughly $10K in monthly revenue (total sales) and stayed there for a long time.
  • Revenue then grew to about $100K per month.
  • Image ads (still pictures) did most of the work. Video ads did not work for their fashion products.
  • A Taboola test (a test on the Taboola ad network) at the $10K level failed.
  • Later, daily sales plateaued at about $4.5K to $5K from November onward, even after duplicating the ad set (a group of ads with shared settings) horizontally and then vertically (two ways of copying a winning ad set).
  • The seller then hired an agency (an outside service company) about 10 days before the call, and opened a second store, which reached about $30K in its first month.
  • Overall revenue was about $850K, with about $500K generated in the last five months.
  • The first store reached about $110K.
  • The seller also reported that CJ Dropshipping (a supplier) caused many undelivered orders and heavy customer service work.
  • The second store in this case reached about $30K in its first month on the Shopify plan used at the time (a paid monthly package).
  • One early store generated 2,000 to 4,000 USD profit per month, enough to live well in a low-cost city.
  • The owner later sold stores for amounts such as 60,000 USD and used the money to start new ones.
