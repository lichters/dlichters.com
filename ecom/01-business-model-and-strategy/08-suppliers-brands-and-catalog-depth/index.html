Playbook › 1. Business model and strategy

Suppliers, brands and catalog depth

Section 1.8 · 8 of 23 in this chapter · 10 min read

In short

  • Build a demo store with 10 to 20 example products before you contact any supplier.
  • Get approved by about five suppliers first, then keep contacting every supplier, not only the first ones who say yes.
  • Look for at least 20 suppliers that ship orders straight to your customers before you commit to a niche.
  • Only work with suppliers that already sell in your customers' country, because shipping from there is fast and the brands are known.
  • Sources disagree about selling on Amazon, eBay and similar sites, so check each supplier's agreement before you list there.

Demo store and readiness before outreach

1 min
  • Create 10 to 20 demo products (example products for a store that is not selling yet) before contacting suppliers, because suppliers will not work with a store they cannot picture on the site.
  • Demo products are placeholder products, meaning stand-ins with no real sales behind them. They use AI-generated images, names and descriptions (made by artificial intelligence, not photographed or written by hand).
  • Suppliers see the demo products to judge what the store will look like. This happens before you ask for authorization, and it raises your chances of becoming an authorized retailer.
  • Build the website first, then pursue supplier approvals. Approvals and supplier relationships depend on a working store.
  • Plan the build for the next few months. Do not wait for a perfect launch.

Becoming an authorized retailer

1 min
  • To become an authorized retailer (a shop that a brand officially allows to sell its products), you must first look like a real business.
  • A real business has an official business registration (the government record that makes your company legal). It also has a professional business email and a separate business phone.
  • Approval also requires a proven supplier contact strategy (a tested plan for reaching suppliers). Approval is not possible without it.
  • Your first target is approval from about five suppliers, so that you are an authorized retailer for five brands in your niche (a narrow product area).
  • Approval is the milestone that matters before any live product sales (sales to real customers).
  • The real goal is approval from every supplier in the industry. Do not celebrate the first approvals.
  • Keep contacting unapproved brands every day. Follow up with brands you have already contacted.
  • Non-native English speakers (people whose first language is not English) should not hesitate to contact suppliers.
  • Several successful Source A members are non-native speakers with seven-figure businesses (sales of over one million). Most rejections are simply one supplier among many.
  • Age should not limit you. Presenting yourself professionally, including having a phone number, builds legitimacy, however young you sound.
  • For high-ticket products (expensive items), the customer's concern is business credibility (trust that the business is real), not age.

Finding and approaching suppliers

1 min

How many suppliers to list

1 min
  • Find at least 20 dropship-friendly suppliers (suppliers that ship orders directly to your customers) for a niche before you commit to it. Only a few will approve in the first days.
  • Even if only about 5 approve at the start, follow up with the remaining prospects (businesses you might work with) later.
  • Treat supplier count as a numbers game early on. Many prospects decline or stay silent.
  • A long list keeps momentum going. Treat each rejection as data, and return to the other prospects later.
  • Pick one product type and source it from many suppliers, not from a single supplier. This spreads supply risk (the chance that one supplier failing hurts you) across the catalogue (your full list of products).

Where to find prospects

1 min
  1. Open the websites of your top competitors.
  2. Go to their bestseller pages (pages listing their most-sold products).
  3. Note which suppliers make those bestselling products.
  4. Contact those suppliers first.
  • A business owner with an existing physical store (a shop you can walk into) can open an ecommerce store (an online shop) next to it. They can use existing supplier relationships as a competitive advantage (an edge over rivals). Online-only sellers usually cannot access those relationships.
  • If a niche has too few suppliers, broaden to a related category with more product types, for example from rocking chairs to outdoor furniture. This increases the number of brands you can get approved with.
  • Brands sold only through physical stores do not count as future competitors or suppliers for the drop-ship model.
  • Look for online-only brands with no warehouse (a building for storing goods) or showroom (a room where products are displayed for buyers).
  • Selling a product type that is also sold in local retail stores is acceptable, as long as the brands you contact primarily sell online.
  • Brands that depend on a showroom or physical stores are not suitable suppliers.
  • Local suppliers hold stock in the customer's own country. Shipping is typically 1 to 7 days, the products are already sold and tested in that market, and the brands are known to buyers.
  • Stock in the customer's country is a fixed requirement. Only work with suppliers that already sell in the same country as your customers.

What to ask for

1 min
  • Ask for the current product catalogue and review it before making an offer. The best sourcing decisions depend on the full list.
  • Many suppliers list products across unrelated niches. Filter those out before you start.
  • When a supplier asks for annual sales goals for a brand-new store (a store that has not sold anything yet), a reasonable answer is a figure between 2 and 500,000. The currency was not stated.

Supplier fit: margins and pricing

1 min
  • Margin is the share of each sale you keep after paying costs. A supplier with a sliding-scale margin (a margin that changes with sales volume) is not automatically disqualified.
  • Lower-margin products can still be listed, but they may be hard to run ads profitably on.
  • Pivot question (should you change direction?): if a store has about 10 supplier approvals but low margins, keep the store live.
  • Upload some low-margin products without paid ads for credibility, and keep reaching out to higher-margin suppliers.
  • Do not pivot completely.
  • In a country with no MAP policy (no rule setting a minimum advertised price, the lowest price a brand lets you show), price matching (matching a competitor's price) cannot be relied on.
  • Set prices from wholesale costs (the price you pay the supplier) and competitor data instead.
  • Tariffs (taxes on imported goods) or supplier cost increases raise wholesale prices. This raises MAP and therefore customer prices.
  • The effect applies to all competitors at the same time, so no single store loses sales to another.

Case: a MAP-priced store with weak early results

1 min
  • A store owner spent a month building a store where items priced between $1,000 and $2,000 MAP had an average margin of about 20%.
  • The first week of ads produced one sale. The advice was not to switch niches.
  • Instead, add more approved suppliers first. About 80% of sales often come from about 20% of products.
  • Until then, cut wasted ad spend (money spent on ads that bring no sales) using the optimization steps.
  • Spend early money on clicks that bring quality traffic (visitors who may buy) while testing keywords (the search words people type) and price points.

Adding value and relationship building

1 min
  • A supplier relationship depends on you adding value beyond simple order fulfilment (handling and sending orders).
  • Adding value is essential for building trust with silver and gold suppliers (supplier ranking levels).
  • Relationship tactics such as gifts, insider access and negotiated pricing belong to a later stage.
  • That later stage is roughly three to six months, or up to a year, after the business starts.
  • Do not use these tactics in the pre-launch phase (before the store opens) or in the first weeks after the store goes live.

Early traffic and brand choice

1 min
  • Early traffic (visitors to your store) is easiest to get from brands and products that people already search for on Google and Google Shopping (Google's product listings).
  • Add smaller, unknown brands later, when the store already has traffic, an email list (a list of customers' email addresses) and a social following (people who follow you on social media) to promote them.

Catalogue size and expansion

1 min

Why supplier count matters

1 min
  • More authorized suppliers and products is one of the two biggest levers for profit and revenue. The other is traffic growth.
  • Stores with 20 to 40 brands have seen one additional supplier add 30 to 50% to revenue. The impact of a single brand is hard to predict.
  • The two main drivers of store size are supplier approvals for strong brands with in-demand products, and sufficient traffic from ads and SEO (search engine optimisation, getting free visits from search results).
  • Market size sets the ceiling for how big a store can grow.
  • About 80% of revenue often comes from about 20% of products.
  • This ratio may change over time, but it remains a consistent theme.
  • Do not remove low-selling products.
  • Instead, optimise their pages for conversion (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Or reduce their advertising costs through lower CPC bids (CPC means cost per click, the price you offer for each click on your ad).

How many brands and products

1 min
  • Do not run a store with only three brands.
  • Diversify to around 20 brands, so that one supplier running out of stock does not leave the store empty.
  • A store that sells a wide range of products should start with a minimum of 100 products.
  • Over time, add all the quality products from quality suppliers in the niche.
  • A new store can start with around 100 products.
  • It can grow over months to 500, then 1,000 products, as more suppliers are approved.
  • A niche can ultimately hold 5,000 to 10,000 products.

Growing a single-product store

1 min
  • Expand the product line only after one product has proven to be a winner.
  • Then add small upsell products (a bigger or better version offered at checkout), free add-on items, complementary products (items that go well with the first) or products from a similar niche.
  • Once a product is winning, expand by adding small upsell products, free items, complementary products or similar-niche products.
  • These additions make the offer more attractive.
  • Gradually add complementary products from the same category. For example, several beauty products instead of one brush.
  • This enables better supplier volume discounts (lower prices for buying more) and creates a cohesive brand identity (a brand that looks and feels like one whole).
  • Complementary products or products from the same niche can attract a broader customer base.
  • Different shoppers relate to different products. Each new product can bring in buyers the first product would miss.
  • Several related categories in one niche help a brand build authority (trust as an expert in that niche).
  • Each category is a separate product that can be scaled with paid ads on its own. So the store gets more than one potential winner.

Case: Blissey (silk products)

1 min

Blissey started with a single product, silk pillowcases. It later added a silk sleep mask, silk hair accessories and further categories in the same silk-related messaging. It now sells several categories. The benefits shown were:

  • Complementary products encourage a second purchase;
  • New categories are launched by email to the existing customer base with no ad spend;
  • Each category can become a separate winner to scale with paid ads;
  • The brand builds authority in silk products;
  • It attracts shoppers who were not interested in the pillowcases.

Case: a UK two-store business

1 min
  • A UK dropshipping business with two Shopify stores (online shops built on Shopify, a store software) reached its biggest month to date in April 2018.
  • Sales that month were 76,000 GBP (about 100,000 USD). This came after adding more suppliers and uploading more products.
  • The owners reached 1 million USD in total sales within roughly the first year across the two stores.

Sourcing scale and cost

1 min
  • Competitors selling below your cost may be buying in bulk.
  • Source from Alibaba (a large online marketplace for bulk goods) at larger quantities, for example 1,000 units, to check whether a cheaper cost of goods is possible.
  • Start lean: source from AliExpress or CJ Dropshipping first (online suppliers that make ordering easy).
  • Move to private suppliers only when a product is a clear winner. This keeps the time you invest low until the ads prove the product.
  • Focus first on consistent daily sales, such as around 20 to 50 sales per day, before switching suppliers for lower prices.
  • Demand consistency matters more than a small cost saving.
  • Dropshipping does not require you to hold stock. The inventory (stored goods) lives with the supplier.
  • Only the top sellers need active stock monitoring.

Tier-two resellers

1 min
  • Buying stock through a middleman who already holds a supplier account (a tier-two reseller arrangement) should be avoided.
  • The reason: the margin can disappear very quickly.

Sales channels and channel conflict

1 min

Wayfair

1 min
  • Do not sell through Wayfair (a large online home-goods store) in this model. Wayfair sells directly to consumers, so it competes with your store.
  • Wayfair works directly with dropship brands, so it is not a retail channel for a dropshipper.
  • Only private label products (products you make and brand yourself) can be sold there.

Amazon, eBay and other third-party platforms

1 min

These two sources disagree. Both are kept here with the conditions under which each applies.

Source Advice When it applies
Source A Selling the same products on Wayfair, Amazon or other marketplaces alongside your own store creates no conflict; treat these as additional distribution channels. Where no supplier agreement forbids third-party sales.
Source A Do not sell on Amazon, eBay or any third-party platform. Suppliers typically forbid third-party sales in their agreements. Sell exclusively through your own Shopify store. Where supplier agreements forbid third-party sales, which the source describes as typical.
  • Selling through eBay (a large online auction and shopping site) via a Shopify app (an add-on tool) is not recommended.
  • Listing products on large third-party marketplaces such as Walmart.com (a large online store with many outside sellers) is not a recommended main sales channel.
  • Marketplaces host many outside sellers and are not niche-specific.
  • If a marketplace is considered, it needs its own separate evaluation of fees, competition and margins.
