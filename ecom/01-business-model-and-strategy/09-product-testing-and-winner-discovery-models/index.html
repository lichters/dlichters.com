Product testing and winner discovery models

Section 1.9 · 9 of 23 in this chapter · 21 min read

In short

  • Test many products fast before you build a brand. Testing beats spending weeks on one product.
  • Expect the first winner to take a long time. Plan to test several products, not one.
  • Cut a product once it has lost about 300 USD without making a profit.
  • Pump-and-dump products sell hard for a few months, then fade. They fund a real brand but are not one.
  • Test a product with ads before you spend a lot on making it.
More key points
  • Check that buyers will use it on their own and that it solves a real problem.
  • Copy a proven product into a new market where no local rival is advertising.
  • At first, speed matters more than originality. Spend more only on what sells.

Test-many-products approach

3 min
  • Before you build a branded store, test many markets, products and niches through dropshipping (you sell items that a supplier ships straight to the buyer, so you hold no stock). Dropshipping has no stock risk. It shows whether demand exists before you spend money on inventory (stock you buy and store).
  • The goal of product testing is to find winning products fast and grow them to a higher sales level by September. Testing many products quickly beats spending lots of effort on a few.
  • There are two strategies. You can spend time on one product researched deeply to build a brand. Or you can test many products quickly to find a winner, then build a brand around it. Both have trade-offs. Quick testing usually reveals winners sooner.
  • Test-and-iterate works because a product that is already trending can carry an average marketer. Keep launching products and creatives (ad images and videos) until one catches a wave of demand. Use ad spy tools (tools that show what competitors run in ads) to find trending products.
  • Store choice is not decisive. The product matters most. The domain name (your web address) and store structure play a small role. So focus on your funnel (the path a buyer takes from ad to purchase) and on testing new products quickly.
  • Do not rush a product to market and abandon it after weak early results. Test offers and ad angles (different deals and hooks) over time. Invest enough effort to learn whether the product can work.
  • Finding the first winning product is the hardest part of e-commerce. It usually takes until about 50,000 to 100,000 USD in revenue is made. After that point there is money to reinvest in learning and growth.
  • Product research is the most important skill. A good product is easier to market. A weak product cannot be marketed successfully. Improving research quality makes every other step easier.
  • Products making about 30,000 USD per month in revenue are common. Products at 1,000,000 USD per month are rare. Aim for products with steady demand rather than extreme volume.
  • Dropshipping success often takes longer than beginners expect. One profitable product after about six months, found after testing roughly four to five products, is a reasonable pace. Plan to test several products rather than expecting the first one to win. In the worst case, aim for up to about 20 products.
  • Before the first major winner, testing took between 18 and 24 products in the cases described. The product that finally scaled was the 24th tested, after the mentoring phase.
  • Product selection should be driven by quality, ethical sourcing and meeting all defined selection criteria. Personal preference should not override these requirements.
  • Choose low-risk products that are cheap to buy and need little storage. A home-based seller can then test without losing much money.
  • Before building a full ebook or other product, validate demand at the earliest stage. Use a simple landing page (a single page built to get a visitor to act) that offers pre-orders (orders taken before the product exists) or a waitlist (a sign-up list for when it is ready). Test the concept on social channels before investing in inventory or custom production.
  • Keep experimental spending limited to one or two tests at a time. Most growth projects have no large recurring cost. Money should not be spread across many new ideas.

Pump-and-dump short-term products

3 min

Definition and purpose

  • A pump-and-dump product (a product that sells very well for a short window) sells strongly for about 2 to 4 months, sometimes 4 to 5 months. It cannot be relied on as steady year-round income. It can still produce useful cash flow (money coming in and going out).
  • A pump-and-dump product is a short-term testing vehicle. It is meant to generate cash flow and momentum, which is then reinvested in a long-term brand. Its purpose is cash flow and learning, not longevity.
  • This model is a short-term, cash-focused strategy built to generate fast capital, not a lasting brand. The operator uses a product's temporary moment. The operator does not try to build loyalty or a community around it.
  • The cash from pump-and-dump stores is meant to fund a real, long-term brand with actual equity (ownership value) later. The model is valuable mainly as a source of startup capital (money to start a business).
  • Because the operator has low emotional attachment to any single product, they can enter, scale and exit without the pressure of a legacy brand (an old brand with history to protect).

Procedure

Procedure:

  1. Find a trending, attention-grabbing product, usually a gadget, gimmick or viral item.
  2. Push aggressive ads into it and scale quickly.
  3. Collect the cash it generates.
  4. Move on to the next product once momentum fades.

Exit and lifespan

  • Exit a product when its momentum dies down, its margins shrink or market saturation (too many sellers for the same buyers) sets in. Margin means the share of each sale kept as profit. Exit before the product's decline makes the ad spend unprofitable.
  • Pump-and-dump stores usually burn out within one to three months. Some decline even faster. Do not plan a store around a long lifespan.

Who it suits and what it costs

  • This model suits beginners and anyone who wants to build capital quickly without the long wait of building a brand. It is not suited to someone who wants to build equity.
  • The model creates no brand equity. Nothing lasting is built, and the store cannot really be sold as a brand. The money comes from selling the product, and then you leave.
  • The model is one of the fastest ways to generate short-term income, especially in 2026, when attention spans are shorter and virality (how fast content spreads) matters more. It fits operators whose goal is quick, scalable cash flow and who accept moving from product to product.
  • Stores running this model can generate thousands of dollars within days. In the first week, a store can sometimes reach about 10,000 USD per day.

Learning value and progression

  • The model works as a fast testing ground. An operator quickly learns marketing, product testing and paid ads. These skills are needed later when running a real brand.
  • Operators who want to build a lasting business will need to move beyond this model eventually. Knowing which stage of the journey you are in is what makes the model useful rather than harmful.

Procedure:

  1. Generate fast cash with pump-and-dump products.
  2. Reinvest that cash into building a real, sustainable brand.
  3. Build the brand toward community-driven growth, loyal customers and repeat sales as the long-term goal.

Books as pump-and-dump products

  • Book products can be used as a short-term capital builder (a way to earn starting money) for a beginner. A book trend can be a pump-and-dump rather than a long-term business. Treat each book as a test with its own lifecycle.
  • Some books are fast, short-lived products that cannot be turned into a lasting brand. They fade quickly, and the content cannot be owned. Such products are not a foundation for brand building.

Viral and TikTok-driven testing

4 min

Checking viral products

Procedure for checking a viral product's real sales:

  1. Find the viral video and note its view count and the creator's follower count over time.
  2. Review the creator's other content for context and timing (when the video was posted).
  3. Place a real test purchase on the product page from a phone or desktop.
  4. Wait for the order confirmation email. Check the spam folder if it does not arrive.
  5. Note the order number and confirmation number that the store issues.
  6. Estimate the store's order count from the visible order-number sequence, the number of reviews and the time since launch.
  7. Multiply estimated orders by the sale price to estimate revenue.
  • Judge a viral product by sales signals rather than view counts alone. A video with 700,000 views from a small follower account may still have converted (turned viewers into buyers) well. A seller who keeps uploading is likely still earning something.
  • A low follower count can be a warning sign when a seller keeps an account running. Continued posting usually means the account is still generating sales.
  • A profile image that does not show the product is a weak choice. Viewers may take the account for a bot and scroll past it.

Case: blender from a TikTok account

  • A blender sold through a TikTok account that started on March 31 reportedly generated over 600,000 USD in sales from that one product by the time of the review. The hosts estimated about 20 USD profit per unit and roughly 50% margin. They stated these were guesses.
  • The account grew fast through a follow-for-giveaway hook (asking viewers to follow to enter a prize draw). The product was fairly priced. Sales slowed in the last one to two months, after uploads had been consistent for months before.

Amazon spillover

  • When a product goes viral on TikTok, it can spread to Amazon (another online marketplace), where listings and reviews may appear. Amazon reviews from viewers who bought elsewhere can be negative. Product quality matters even on platforms outside your store.

Store and organic sequence

  • Build the store before going viral with a new account. Viral success can come fast. Viewers who see no link cannot come back, which causes lost sales and lost returning customers. Coins (TikTok's virtual gifts that viewers buy for creators) can send traffic to the store.
  • Once a TikTok account reaches around 10,000 followers, it is considered credible as a business. With over 50 to 100 videos in the library, older videos keep producing views for weeks and months. Some resurface after many months.
  • The same TikTok testing method works for personal brands or own products, not only dropshipping. Accounts with about 10,000 followers or more can be sold or turned into personal accounts if the product does not sell.
  • Start with organic content (unpaid posts) to gain experience before paying for Spark Ads (TikTok ads that boost an existing post) or TikTok ads. Organic results show which products and angles work before you spend on ads.
  • Organic TikTok selling keeps ad spend low in the early phase. The cost of testing is time rather than money. Paid ads can burn budget quickly during product testing. A budget of more than 5,000 USD was reported as spent on ads before the first major winner.

Procedure:

  1. Find a product (on TikTok, AliExpress or Facebook ads) and order a sample, starting with Amazon for speed.
  2. Create and post content on the product about 3 times per day.
  3. Study competitors and track what performs.
  4. Build the Shopify store after content gets traction (a few thousand views), rather than before.
  5. Add the website link to the bio (profile description) once the account reaches 1,000 followers, and test the link.

Organic proof of concept

  • A TikTok organic operator built an account to about 20,000 followers. It reportedly generated around 200 USD in sales, mostly from paid ads rather than organic reach. Organic content acted as a proof of concept (a small test showing an idea can work) for products that then ran as paid campaigns.
  • The same operator reported a first 230,000 USD revenue month in Q4 2020, with a best single day of about 15,000 USD. Later, the operator preferred lower revenue with higher profit.

Competition and timing

  • Competition for organically proven products tends to rise toward Q4 (the last quarter of the year), as more sellers copy the same content. Products that show early traction are worth acting on before the crowd arrives.
  • Timing a launch with peak demand can make a product sell far more easily. One example is the work-from-home surge during COVID. Check trend tools (tools that show rising search and sales interest) for upcoming trends when choosing a problem or niche.

Accounts as split tests

1 min
  • A proven viral video can be reproduced on a new account with the same product, which can give a second viral run. Running two accounts that both link to one store also protects sales if one account or store is restricted (limited or suspended by the platform).
  • Two accounts on one product are a split test (two versions tested side by side) and a safety net. Once you know which account performs better, you can merge both into one store. Consolidation (merging into one) is often the final step after the test.
  • Spark Ads and paid promotion of a viral organic video may end its organic reach. One practice was to keep separate accounts. One account stays strictly organic. Another is used for paid ads, so paid activity does not damage the algorithmic value (how the platform's system ranks and shows content) of the organic account.
  • A new account often outperforms a long-established account with a large following. Follower count is weakly tied to reach on TikTok. A product test can be moved to a new account when the existing one stops performing. Accounts with tens of thousands of followers were sometimes abandoned for this reason.
  • Multiple stores or accounts for one product can be used to expand reach, to run ads through a secondary store or to escape a restricted account. The reasons behind them are not always clear.
  • Reusing one set of clips across two accounts is a common mistake among sellers who open a second account. It often explains why the second account grows slowly.

Testing budgets and stop-loss

3 min

Budget discipline

  • Start each new product with a testing budget of 10 to 30 USD per day. Increase the budget only while the 10x ROAS target is met. ROAS (return on ad spend) is the money earned per dollar spent on ads, so 10x means 10 USD back for every 1 USD spent.
  • Ad budgets for Google and Meta should be spent on the store that already has product detail and authorised suppliers (approved sources of the product). Products with no evidence of sales should not take a large share of the budget.
  • Budget for product testing so that each product gets at least about 250 USD of spend before judging whether it works. A product is cut at roughly 300 USD of loss. Total pre-profit ad spend in this case was about 2,000 to 3,000 USD across four failed products and the fifth winner.
  • Cut a product once its losses reach about 300 USD without profitability, and move on to the next product. Setting this stop-loss (a limit on losses, set in advance) keeps testing disciplined and limits the amount lost per test.
  • Test a product fast, kill losers early and move to the next product when the first one does not show signs. Ad spend on an unproven product can be stopped around 50 to 100 USD, with 200 to 250 USD as the upper limit for a product without signs.
  • For a product with strong comment demand but high pricing, test with paid ads at small budgets to see if it actually sells before scaling.
  • In one product test that ran about four months with dozens of products and no winner, old ad accounts showed cost per click of 5 to 8 USD and CPM of 20 to 50 USD. CPC is the cost of one click. CPM is the cost per 1,000 ad views. A new ad account was launched with a new pixel (tracking code that records visits and sales from ads) and 10 new ads. After 6.60 USD in spend, CPM and CPC were already clearly better. CTR (click-through rate, the share of viewers who click) was still below the 1% target.
  • In the same case, the ad setup was changed to 10 ad sets (groups of ads with shared targeting and budget), each with a unique video. The product name was changed to a single sleep benefit. A launch price of 49 to 59 USD was considered to gather pixel data. Compare that with cost of goods (what the product costs you to buy) of about 20 USD and break-even (the price where you cover costs with no profit or loss) of about 52 USD.
  • In the same case, the countdown and the GIF were to be reviewed. The product page was to be rebuilt with benefits, how it works, FAQ, testimonials and guarantee. The agreed plan was to perfect one product page, check its add-to-cart ratio (the share of visitors who add the item to the cart), and then duplicate it for other products. No final result was reported.
  • Two founders, self-funded, first tried Facebook ads, Google Ads and influencer marketing in August 2020 after finding dropshipping on YouTube. Four Facebook ad accounts were banned almost at once. About 7,000 to 8,000 euros were lost across ads, influencers and tools with no return. They then invested in a mentorship and spent about 2,500 to 3,000 euros in a second test phase until the next winner.

Tools

  • Do not pay for spy tools such as AdSpy before you have a first winning product and some sales. Reinvest early profit into such tools once you want to find trends for your next store.
  • Tools cited as worth comparing for product research: Peakster for ad spying and store tracking, and Shop Hunter for product research. Compare each one by the specific job you need.

Product stage and fads

1 min

Kill rules and product-market fit

  • If the product itself has poor quality or weak market fit (people want it at a price they will pay), no improvement to ad creative will fix the basic problem. Remove the product from testing. Focus resources on better-performing products instead.
  • A Facebook Ads testing campaign exists to answer one question: is there product-market fit between your product and the Facebook audience you are targeting? Success means the product can be sold profitably to that audience. Clicks or website traffic alone do not count as success.

Moving from test to brand

  • Launch a new product at a lower price to get early traction and learn whether the market wants it. Only after that validation should the product be developed into a brand.
  • At the testing stage, the goal is not to build a brand. The goal is to find out quickly whether a product sells profitably. Brand work comes only after that is confirmed.
  • When a niche (narrow market segment) has many winning products, there is no need to research every product in it. Once one product passes all tests, build a store on it and keep the remaining winners for later stores.
  • Many sellers run several near-identical simple stores and Facebook pages, one per product, to test quickly. When one of them performs, they build a brand around it. Seeing many similar stores is a sign of this testing method, not proof that a product is dead.

Fads and short windows

  • The model is most useful when the operator knows the product's stage. A pump-and-dump product is a fad in the short window and must be exited before its decline.

General-store testing

1 min
  • When starting out, run a general store (a store with a generic name selling several products from different niches) rather than buying a domain and branding for a single product. This keeps the budget lean. You invest in full branding only after a product proves it sells.
  • A general store with a generic name is a valid testing ground for product proofs of concept, because it lets you test many products quickly. Later, a store with a generic name can be turned into a one-product store if one product proves itself.
  • Some sellers make money on test stores but struggle when moving to a one-product store. Do not assume a switch to a single-product store will automatically keep results.
  • Niche-only stores can lock you out of other products worth testing. In the testing phase, a general store lets you test more products faster.
  • Test new products on a general store first. Once a winning product is found, the store can move from general to single-product. A sharper brand build can come later.

Procedure for general-store testing:

  1. Launch a general store under a brandable, random domain name and test products one by one.
  2. Focus effort on strong landing pages with a clear offer and trust elements.
  3. When one product clearly performs, move it to a single-product store after about one week.
  4. Add upsell (an extra or bigger offer after purchase) and closely related products to build a niche around the winner.
  5. Sell related products through email marketing or launch a second hero product (a main star product) for ads.
  • When one product wins in a general store, keep related products in the same store. Place the winning product at the top of the collection and use its image in the creatives. Create a separate one-product store (new domain, new logo, new homepage) only when the products are unrelated.

EU and US testing

1 min

EU general-store model

  • A general-store model in EU markets runs one native-language store per market (a store in the local language, for example Dutch, French and German). It tests many products across them and scales only the products that sell. Store-level scaling happens after a product proves itself, not before.
  • In EU general-store testing, the target is 5 to 10 new product tests per day across all stores.

Procedure for the EU product test cycle:

  1. Launch the new product in the relevant native-language store with a Facebook campaign.
  2. Spend about EUR 50 on the test.
  3. If the product has no sale once EUR 50 is spent (the source equated this to about USD 25), kill the product immediately.
  4. Keep products that sell and scale them across the stores.
  5. Move on to the next product test.

US testing

  • US product testing requires about 400 to 500 USD in ad spend per product. During that test, run A/B tests (comparing two versions) of headlines on the sales page and new creatives.

Digital product tests

2 min
  • Ads with a high CPM (the cost to show your ad to 1,000 people) can still be worth it.
  • This is true for a high-ticket item (an expensive product).
  • It works when the margin (the money left after costs) can pay for more spending.
  • Be patient. Cheaper products need more spending to gather enough data (sales and click numbers).
  • Before you build a digital product (a product with no physical box, such as a file you sell online), check one thing.
  • Confirm that buyers will work through it on their own (self-study, learning alone without help).
  • In one case, a paid digital course for young children turned into requests for one-on-one help (private help from a person).
  • So the product became a service (help you sell, not a file).
  • Test self-study demand before investing in production (paying to make the product).
  • Before you write a full ebook (a book saved as a file), check with research.
  • Make sure the planned solution really helps the problem.
  • Only then treat it as a product you can sell.
  • Use ChatGPT (an AI chat tool) to make product, recipe and offer ideas quickly.
  • Treat every idea as a hypothesis (a guess that needs testing).
  • Each idea must be proven with ads and sales data before scaling (spending much more on it).
  • Test digital products, such as recipe ebooks, in markets where the main competitor (the biggest rival) is not yet advertising.
  • A market with very high competition can be skipped.
  • In the example, the US is very competitive, so choose a smaller market like Germany.
  • A test could be a short PDF crash course (a quick beginner guide saved as a PDF file).
  • It explains a topic most people have heard of but do not understand.
  • Example: the basics of crypto assets (digital coins) like Bitcoin and Ethereum.
  • Promote it with ads to see if people buy it.
  • The core idea is clear, simple teaching of a popular trend.
  • An AI-tool guide (a how-to file about an AI program) can sell for 40 to 50 USD.
  • It sells when it teaches how to get the most value from a tool.
  • Subscribing (paying to join) to a tool is easy. Using it well is harder.
  • That gap creates demand for guidance.
  • An AI-tool guide ebook for dropshipping (selling items you never stock, where the supplier ships them) can sell at 40 to 50 USD.
  • It comes with step-by-step blueprints (exact plans, one step at a time) for using one specific tool.
  • It works best when the niche (a narrow market) and the problem it solves are clearly defined.

Copying winners into new markets

1 min

Copying proven ads and products

1 min
  • When starting from scratch, the fastest way to learn whether a product can sell profitably (make money) is to test proven top-performing ads from others.
  • You do not need original content for your first wins.
  • At this stage, speed matters more than originality.
  • Ripping (copying) proven ads is a starting point, not the long-term method.
  • Collect sales data quickly.
  • Scale what sells (spend more on it).
  • Two similar brands scaled the same concept by entering different markets.
  • They added unique selling points (what makes a product stand out).
  • This shows that a different market can work even with a similar product.
  • A proven product from a successful brand can be copied into a new country.
  • In that country, no local competitor is visible.
  • This reduces the research needed to start.
  • The rest of the work lies in sourcing (finding suppliers), photos and advertising, not in inventing the product.

Markets and stores

1 min
  • Find existing, verified (checked) winning stores in another market.
  • Sell the same products in the new market.
  • Example: a store made about 1 million USD in the US.
  • The same products were sold first in Switzerland, then Germany and Austria.
  • A product that already scales (sells well and grows) in the USA or Australia can be carried into German-speaking markets.
  • Those markets are Germany, Austria and Switzerland.
  • This works with a German-language store, German-language ads and the payment options local buyers use.
  • Example: a home decor store (home items like decorations) had a price of CHF 59.99 (Swiss francs, about 80 USD CAD).
  • The test spend limit was 5 USD per ad set (one group of ads with one budget).
  • The sales target per winning item was a few sales per day.

Case: winter product in Switzerland

1 min
  • A seller with two years of dropshipping experience and some saved capital copied a proven winter product idea from a successful brand.
  • They entered Switzerland, where no local competitors were visible.
  • They contacted Chinese suppliers and ordered 25 design variations.
  • First, they had one sample (a test product) sent to check quality.
  • A low-budget photo shoot (a cheap, small photo session) was made with friends.
  • The supplier product photos were used in the first ads.
  • The product launched on 1 January.
  • The first ads brought around 6,000 USD in January and February.
  • The cat, koala and unicorn designs sold best.
  • Those designs were reordered.
  • In March and April, stock was reordered.
  • The website was to be improved.
  • For the next season, these were prepared: interests (topics used to pick who sees the ads), creatives (ad images and text) and a second photo shoot.
  • From September to February, sales reached around 31,000 USD.
  • The Facebook ad budget was low.
  • Every order was fulfilled manually (packed and shipped by hand) from a warehouse (a storage place).
  • After ads were stopped, steady sales continued through word of mouth (people recommending it to others).
