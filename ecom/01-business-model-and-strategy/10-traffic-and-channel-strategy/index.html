Traffic and channel strategy

Section 1.10 · 10 of 23 in this chapter · 33 min read

In short

  • Start with Google. It is the main source of visitors, and your first channel should be working well before you try others.
  • Test a platform with a small budget first. Do not spend big until you know it makes a profit.
  • Buyers who already search for your product are the easiest to sell to. Start there, not with people who have never heard of it.
  • Before you pick a platform, check where your buyers spend their time. Different platforms reach different people.
  • Do not guess at new channels. Learn them from your own market research and test each one.
More key points
  • Test each new channel with a small, real budget and measure it. Do not assume it works.
  • Get one channel profitable before you add the next. Do not launch many at once.
  • Spend about 10% of your ad money on tests and about 90% on what already works.
  • Free posts in marketplaces and free listings can bring your first sales.
  • Traffic quality (how well visitors fit) matters more than traffic volume (how many visitors you get).

Google First: The Core Channel

2 min
  • Start with Google. Google is your main source of visitors (traffic) because it is usually the fastest and most reliable channel.
  • For a dropshipping store (you sell products without holding stock, and the supplier ships each order), Google is generally the fastest channel to produce results, so traffic work starts there.
  • Wait to use other platforms until Google traffic is highly profitable (earns more than it costs).
  • Maximize Google traffic before you expand to other channels.
  • All traffic sources send visitors to the store, which is the central hub (the one place everything leads to).
  • Splitting your focus across channels too early weakens your results.
  • Squeeze as much volume as possible out of Google first.
  • Other traffic platforms can still produce sales.
  • Expand to them only when the store is highly profitable and Google ads cannot be scaled (made bigger) any further.
  • Use the proven traffic methods first in every country you sell in.
  • Do not start with regional shopping comparison engines (sites that list prices from many shops) or local marketplaces, even if a local platform is popular for e-commerce (online selling). The tested system is your default starting point.
  • The proven traffic blueprint (the step-by-step plan) has about 30 parts.
  • Work through all of them and carry out each one exactly as taught, rather than picking a few. Do this before you test any other channel.
  • Finishing the working system first gives it the highest chance of success, so it earns the money that pays for later experiments.
  • Switching to unproven channels while the current system works adds risk without a clear gain.
  • Common error: jumping to a new channel while your core traffic system is not yet profitable splits your attention and your cash before the business has a proven base.
  • Not needed: a Google Business Profile (a free business listing on Google Maps) is not necessary for running Google Shopping and Merchant Center campaigns (Merchant Center is Google's hub for your product data).
  • Local Inventory: if you run physical stores next to your online sales, turn on Local Inventory (a setting that shows your products to people searching near your stores) in your shopping campaign settings.
  • Local Inventory combines online and offline sales.
  • For pure dropshipping with no physical stores, this setting does not apply.
  • Analytics: Google Analytics (a free tool that tracks your visitors) is usually preferred over paid third-party attribution tools (tools that show which ad earned the sale).
  • Paid attribution tools are expensive but can improve tracking at higher spend levels.

  • These sources include Google Shopping product listing ads, Google search text ads, organic search, Bing ads, and paid social on Facebook, Instagram, Pinterest and Twitter.
  • A Dream 100 source (a list of 100 key partners or websites to contact for visitors) is also on the list.
  • The list is expected to grow as more channels are added.
  • A store should use four traffic channels together.

  • Set up profitable paid search first. It gives a strong base for later channels.
  • SEO (improving your site so it ranks in search for free), social and content tend to work better when added after paid campaigns already run profitably.

Platform Choice and Channel Fit

2 min
  • Three factors decide which platform fits you.
  • The factors are: your beginner level (Meta and TikTok), whether your product already sells (Google Ads), and your skill with platform mechanics (Pinterest, Twitter/X and influencer marketing).
  • A strong product or skilled seller earns little when the offer appears in the wrong place.
  • Put your message where buyers already spend time, both in the real world and online.
  • Different platforms reach different audiences.
  • The same product can perform differently on Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok or Pinterest.
  • Match the product to the platform where its likely buyers spend time.
  • Find new traffic sources through market research in your specific industry. Do not copy others.
  • Some sources only work for certain niches (small, specific markets). Test whether a source fits your customers.
  • Attention drives sales.
  • Customer attention is split and short, so aim to be omnipresent, meaning seen everywhere across your customer's online life.
  • Omnipresence means being seen wherever your customers spend time online in your niche, so they meet your brand most often.
  • The goal is a bigger share of your market's attention than your competitors have. The brand seen most often tends to be chosen at the point of purchase.
  • Four reasons support omnipresence.
  • The reasons are: customers take a journey before buying, that journey is hard to hold attention through, attention turns into revenue, and a brand that appears more often than competitors gains the edge.
  • Journey length depends on price and type. A low-cost t-shirt needs less thought than a $3,000 coffee machine.
  • The full marketing system has six components used together: Know, Convert, Educate, Promote, Develop and Consolidate.
  • Each component should make at least one other one better.
  • So they must run at the same time, not one after another.
  • Traditional funnels are fed by paid traffic (Google Ads, Bing Ads, paid social), organic search (SEO on Google and Bing) and outreach strategies such as Dream 100.
  • After the sale, you can push a customer to bring in new buyers.
  • This raises the value of each customer.

Platform Account Setup and Location Rules

1 min
  • A VPN (virtual private network, which hides your real location) is required when creating a TikTok account.
  • TikTok detects your location and assigns accounts to that location.
  • For Pinterest, create the account with a US VPN.
  • After that, you can run ads without the VPN.
  • Facebook Ads do not need a VPN. Target the country directly in campaign settings.
  • Google Ads also does not need a VPN.
  • But your business details must match the location you target.

Shopping and Search Intent

2 min
  • Only about 20% of Google searches have buying intent (the searcher is ready to buy).
  • The other roughly 80% are informational (looking for answers) or directional (looking around).
  • Target buying searches first. People who want to buy need less persuasion, so paid traffic is easiest to scale this way.
  • Shopping and Product Listing Ads (PLAs, ads showing a product photo and price) are shown mainly for buying searches.
  • The platform filters searches by intent, so you reach buyers without extra targeting work.
  • Informational searches are reached through content, without ads.
  • Buying searches are reached through product listing ads (Shopping/PLA).
  • Google's ad logic moves people through four stages: awareness, interest, consideration and conversion.
  • An ad only earns revenue when people click it at these stages. So the ad must make people want to click.
  • A small number of foundational changes to your ads and product data can lower CPC (cost per click), increase clicks and increase conversions at the same time.
  • The text calls these key levers (hinges) that move the whole system.
  • Visitors who are actively searching for your product convert far better than large untargeted audiences.
  • One store earned a $4,935 order from only 26 website visitors.
  • A new store with no ad history reached its first sale of $2,135 from only 10 website visitors.
  • The owner had onboarded (added) a new supplier. Then they spent about two weeks on Google PLAs with negative keywords (search words where your ad must not show).
  • The owner restricted traffic to SKU-number and brand-term searches.
  • The sale came from people already searching for the exact product or brand.
  • A coached dropshipping store first built a baseline (a working starting foundation) of 100 products and Google Shopping traffic.
  • It then spent about $700 per month on ads while losing money.
  • Because the foundation was in place, sales grew from a few thousand dollars to about $15,000 in one month after optimization (improving the ads and pages).
  • A working baseline makes growth much easier.
  • Google Ads fits products priced around $200 to $300 and above.
  • Shoppers usually search for the item before buying. So the purchase is intent-driven (planned), not impulsive.
  • Search demand fits higher-ticket goods better than impulse-driven social feeds.
  • Competition in Shopping is measured per product.
  • If one product is sold by 25 stores, appearing near the top is hard, even with an aggressive bid (the amount you offer for each click).
  • Do not spend on Google Shopping for products where competitors undercut (sell cheaper) on every listing.
  • Traffic will not convert at the price difference.
  • Do not stop paid ads just because a niche product only gets clicks without sales.
  • Instead, move the effort into optimizing keywords, bids and product pages. Data is already coming in.
  • A store running on free traffic alone is a valid option for products where ads do not convert.
  • These products can stay on the site without ad spend. (Source 2023.)

Build Order for Paid Traffic

1 min
  1. Bottom-of-funnel Google Shopping for buying searches.
  2. Facebook dynamic product ad retargeting (ads that show the exact products a visitor viewed) for visitors who left.
  3. CTR and quality score optimization to lower CPCs. CTR (click-through rate) is the share of people who click your ad. Quality score is Google's rating of how good your ad is.
  4. Email and lifetime value to raise revenue per customer. Lifetime value is the total money one customer spends over time.

Each step adds to the previous one.

Funnel Stages (TOFU, MOFU, BOFU)

3 min
  • A funnel is the path a customer takes from first interest to purchase.
  • TOFU is top of funnel. MOFU is middle of funnel. BOFU is bottom of funnel.
  • Your strategy and ads must match the audience's funnel stage.
  • A message aimed at a later stage than the visitor is in will not convert.
  • A bottom-of-funnel offer shown to a top-of-funnel audience is not profitable.
  • The searcher still needs answers before they consider buying.
  • Send these visitors first to informational content that answers their questions.
  • Example, TOFU (surfing): a searcher types "Do you need to be strong to go surfing?" This is top of funnel.
  • An ad pushing a surfboard to that query is unprofitable.
  • A top-of-funnel ad answering common beginner questions works better. It sends people to a landing page (the page an ad leads to) that captures the email address.
  • That approach is profitable within a few weeks, with the sale coming some weeks later.
  • Example, BOFU (surfing): a searcher types "surfboard for beginner." This is bottom of funnel.
  • An ad saying "buy the best surfboard for beginners," sent to a landing page explaining why it suits beginners, can produce a sale within a few days.
  • Start paid traffic at the bottom of the funnel.
  • Make campaigns profitable with people already ready to buy. Only then move up to awareness audiences.
  • If a business cannot convert the small group of buy-now searchers, it should not spend large sums on awareness audiences.
  • Do not try to convince people who are not looking for the product to buy it.
  • Spending on this audience wastes budget and lowers results.
  • Expand the funnel later. Only do it after bottom-of-funnel campaigns run well.
  • The source treats the rest of the funnel, labeled as 80%, as a later step. It is not something to build first.
  • Audience size grows as you move up the funnel.
  • Searches for a product to buy are fewer than searches for the product category. Those are fewer than searches for a symptom or problem.
  • Plan budget and messaging with this size gap in mind.
  • Paid traffic lets you reach a user at any stage with a message that fits that stage.
  • Match each channel to one funnel stage. Do not ask every ad for an immediate sale.
  • Build bottom-up. Start with Shopping campaigns to capture immediate sales.
  • Then add upper-funnel channels (earlier stages) that build brand trust over time.
  • Plan the sequence backwards from the sale, so each earlier channel feeds the next.
  • Starting with Facebook TOFU ads (top-of-funnel ads) is possible for products people do not search for, such as new unique products.
  • But it is harder. These buyers need more touchpoints (contacts with your brand).
  • Influencer content often works best as the first exposure. Retargeting and an after-visit funnel come after it.
  • For low-volume, high-ticket B2B products (sales between businesses, at high prices), search capture still comes first. Buyers who search are commercially minded.
  • Then extend the audience up the funnel with display ads (banner ads on websites), Facebook or LinkedIn ads. Aim them at professional groups and industry news readers.
  • Most potential buyers are not actively searching, which is why this step is needed.
  • B2B ads with small, specific audiences are harder than B2C ads (sales to consumers).
  • The reason: the number of people at each funnel stage is small.
  • Social ads can still work when almost every business can use the product.
  • Then retargeting audiences become large enough.

Meta (Facebook and Instagram)

2 min
  • Meta is the company behind Facebook and Instagram.
  • Facebook is still valid for direct response ads (ads that ask for an action right away).
  • It works especially for cold traffic (people who have never heard of you) and retargeting.
  • But it has become more expensive.
  • When pixel data is limited, use interest-based targeting (targeting people by their interests) and strong creatives (ad images and videos). A pixel is a tracking code on your site.
  • Google search and the Facebook feed feed each other.
  • Facebook ads can drive branded search on Google (people later search your brand name).
  • Facebook dynamic retargeting moved up to the second most important ad type for e-commerce stores. It is second after Google Shopping.
  • This happened once the focus shifted to bottom-of-funnel audiences.
  • It had earlier been ranked third, next to Google search retargeting.
  • Warm audiences are people who already acted on your site or social accounts.
  • Building them lowers ad costs and raises conversion rates (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Advertising to warm visitors is cheaper than cold prospecting (reaching new people).
  • Shopping ads bring shoppers to the product page.
  • Then remarketing and trust-building emails bring them back.
  • Set up each step to feed the next.
  • Aim for 3 to 4 winning audiences per product.
  • Single audiences can die after a few weeks or months.
  • Several winners let you scale each one separately.
  • Later, you can build lookalike audiences (new people who look like your best buyers) from them.
  • For high-ticket products costing $1,000 to $3,000, a large social following matters less. (2025)
  • Showing up in front of people already searching to buy matters more.
  • Social media marketing is a weak main sales channel for this kind of store.
  • Facebook paid social is one of the Shopify traffic sources listed under the core traffic methods.

Older order advice (2020): across Google results, the TikTok channel was not tested. The team put its effort elsewhere.

  • Google Shopping and search came first.
  • Facebook and Instagram remarketing came next.
  • YouTube, Discovery and display were later expansions.

TikTok

1 min
  • Start with organic content (free posts, not ads) to find a winning product.
  • Only spend on Spark Ads (TikTok ads that boost your own post) after organic results prove that a product and hook (the opening that grabs attention) convert.
  • A newer account often performs better than an older one.
  • An existing account with a large follower base still has value.
  • An account with about 10,000 followers can apply for the Creator Fund (TikTok's payment fund for creators).
  • It can also use a playlist linked from the profile and reach more viewers.
  • When switching an existing account to a different product, expect slow results for one to two weeks.
  • Keep posting without skipping days. The algorithm (the platform's automatic ranking system) usually picks the account back up.
  • Start the age range at 18. Never target anyone under 18, because they cannot complete a purchase.
  • Examples: women 18 to 35 for a female-oriented product, or men 18 to 45 for a male-oriented product that tested well.
  • TikTok ad accounts differ in performance. Some show high CPMs (cost per 1,000 views) for the same video.
  • If a new account shows poor results despite good creatives and landing pages, the account itself can be the reason.
  • Older advice, 2020: TikTok was not tested in that period. The effort went to Google, Facebook and Instagram first.
  • TikTok ads can be one of the untested channels in the roughly 10% testing share of a larger budget (see Experimentation Budgets).

Pinterest

3 min

Pinterest: Fit and Audience

  • Pinterest has more than 400 million active users.
  • Most users come for inspiration. This suits products tied to home decor, fashion, beauty and health.
  • Roughly 25% of the time users spend on Pinterest is spent on shopping.
  • Buying intent is high compared with platforms where people only browse.
  • The US accounts for about 50 to 60% of the Pinterest audience.
  • The source states that around 55% of US users earn more than $100,000 per year.
  • The average order value (the average amount spent per order) on Pinterest is above $50.
  • The source presents this as higher than most other ad platforms.
  • Pinterest is less saturated (less crowded) than Facebook, TikTok or Google.
  • Many advertisers have not yet tested it. This can reduce competition for ad space.
  • Products that often get banned on Facebook or TikTok can usually run on Pinterest.
  • This includes traditional dropshipping products.
  • Pinterest ads are cheaper in the UK and Europe than in the US.
  • The UK and Europe still have a solid market.
  • The US is more competitive because it has the largest user base.
  • Account restrictions can usually be lifted within one to two days, or even a few hours.
  • Personal support from Pinterest is available for account problems.
  • Create the account with a US VPN. Then you can run ads without the VPN.
  • Backup: Pinterest and Snapchat brought in some sales while Google and Meta accounts were blocked.
  • Keep alternative channels ready as a backup when a main ad account is suspended.

Pinterest: Testing and Budget

  • Pinterest Ads is a low-cost platform for specific niches.
  • You can test it with a daily budget of about $5.
  • Results usually take around one week. No sales may appear in the first seven days.
  • Results take longer than on TikTok or Facebook, typically six to seven days.
  • Not every product works on Pinterest. Only test specific products.
  • A $5 daily budget for seven days costs about $35.
  • In one case, CPM for a US audience dropped from $15 at the start to $2 after seven days.
  • The CPC was about 2 cents.
  • On Pinterest, higher budgets do not help early on, because the platform needs time to learn the audience.
  • In the speaker's tests, the most profitable products ran at $5 per day. Some campaigns at $100 per day were also tested.
  • Pinterest ads can deliver very low CPMs, so the platform is cheap to test on.
  • Early traffic quality is usually weaker, because the algorithm is still learning the audience.

Pinterest: Organic and Free Traffic

  • Pinterest works best as a free traffic source for stores that already have an audience.
  • Those visitors are the easiest to convert into quick wins.
  • Stores without an audience can still get traffic through a viral pin (a pin that gets shared widely). That outcome is less predictable.
  • Pinterest users come for inspiration, discovery, new products and how-to content.
  • Build your store content for that purpose.
  • Many users save products to a wish list (a saved list to buy later). So pinned products can convert after a delay.
  • Pinterest referral traffic (visitors sent from Pinterest) on Black Friday and Cyber Monday produced 2x to 3x the e-commerce revenue of normal days.
  • This was with the same traffic volume. It points to high buying intent on peak shopping days.
  • Pinterest traffic spikes of 100 to 500 visits per hour can happen regularly when content is shared well.
  • According to the speaker's experience, virality is easier to reach on Pinterest than on most other channels.

Pinterest: Setup Procedure

  1. Search pinterest.com/source/ with your store domain (your web address). This shows who has already pinned your products. Then reach out to those users.
  2. Check that the Pin It button (a button that lets visitors save your photos to Pinterest) and Rich Pins (pins that show live product details like price) are turned on in the store. Shopify and BigCommerce do this automatically.
  3. Share your pins and your Pinterest profile everywhere your audience already is. This includes your website, email and other social networks.
  4. Build a strong profile. Use boards (collections of pins) for products, inspiration, resources, behind-the-scenes and testimonials (customer reviews). These should support the store.
  5. Create good-looking vertical images (tall images) for your content and products.
  6. Use group boards (shared boards with several contributors). Find them, get access, post your content, drive traffic, then retarget (show ads again to) those visitors.

Bing, Yahoo, X, Marketplaces and Other Channels

4 min

Bing and Yahoo (two search engines, sites where people search, like Google)

  • Lenient policies: Bing and Yahoo run Gemini ad networks (systems that place paid ads on many websites). Their rules, called policies, are often more lenient, meaning looser, than Google's about which products can be advertised. Check their policies when Google rejects a niche (a narrow group of products). They are worth testing as additional paid channels (extra places where you pay for ads).
  • Small share: Bing and other search engines are much smaller than Google and should not form a significant part of the marketing effort (all the work to attract buyers).
  • Taboola (case): Taboola is a native ad network (ads that look like articles on other sites). Its ads were tested at about $10K monthly revenue (sales per month) and did not work, because the seller lacked good information on how to run that channel. Focusing back on Facebook ads brought a large increase instead.

X (Twitter, a social network)

  • Not recommended: Twitter ads are not recommended, since they produced poor results in the presenter's own testing.
  • Narrow fit: X advertising is considered worthwhile mainly for fashion and jewelry in the US, and to a lesser extent the UK. It is not a good channel for large spend, where Facebook, Google and TikTok (a short-video app) are the main platforms.
  • Bot traffic (2024): Advertising on X did not work for the author, and the traffic (visits to your site) appeared to be mostly bots (fake visitors made by software). Do not expect it to convert (turn visitors into buyers).

YouTube

  • Alone rarely works: YouTube ads alone rarely work for drop shipping (selling products that a supplier ships to the buyer, so you hold no stock) with a small budget. The platform is better suited to established brands with a strong budget and studio-quality creatives (professional-looking ad images and videos).
  • Journey role: YouTube video ads can build brand awareness early (how many people know your brand) in a multi-touch journey (a path where a customer meets your brand several times before buying) (see Multi-Channel Journeys).
  • Expansion order: YouTube ads are the first expansion step (your first move to grow) once a store is above $50,000 in monthly sales (see Expansion Triggers).

Marketplaces and classifieds (marketplaces are big shopping sites where many sellers list items; classified ads are small listings on sites like Craigslist)

  • Amazon and eBay: For a dropship store (a store that sells products a supplier ships), Amazon and eBay are not recommended as sales channels. Pinterest (a visual idea site) and Instagram (a photo and video app) are considered worth selling on alongside the store.
  • Free marketplace posts (2023): Free posts in marketplaces such as Facebook and Craigslist can bring the first sales and cash before any paid advertising starts. Paid campaigns can follow once the store has a few sales and optimized product pages (product pages improved to sell better).
  • Classifieds, zero-budget start: A zero-budget start (starting with no ad spend) is possible with classified ads, but reinvest the first sales into paid Google Ads (ads you pay for on Google) at about $1,000 per month once they come in.
  • Classifieds, city choice: Do not post the same classified ad in every city, because such ads get flagged (marked as suspicious by the site). Post in the classified section of a few large, high-traffic cities (cities with many visitors) such as New York (Manhattan) and Los Angeles.

Blocked niches and outreach

  • Direct placements: When Google and Meta (the company behind Facebook and Instagram) ads are blocked for a niche, buy placements directly (spots where your ad or link appears) on busy niche forums (online discussion boards for one topic), Facebook groups that sell cover image space, or niche blogs, either through affiliate deals (a partner earns a cut of each sale) or paid ads. Link these placements to strong content pages, not only product pages, to build authority (trust and expertise others see in you).
  • Snapchat restriction (2025-2026): Snapchat's Spotlight (a feed of short videos) and Stories (a feed of posts) sharing was restricted for users under 16, who can no longer share outside mutual-friend groups (groups where people follow each other). Brands targeting younger audiences (groups of people you reach) on Snapchat need to account for this reduced sharing reach (how far posts travel).

Local platforms

  • Later experiment: Test local shopping platforms (sites that show nearby shops or products) or comparison engines (sites that compare prices) only after the store is profitable and the owner has built up cash reserves (money saved aside). Before that point, they are a later experiment, not a launch channel (the first channel you open).
  • Not ruled out: Local shopping platforms are not ruled out for good. They can perform well in some markets (groups of buyers in one area), so they are a valid option once the core business generates profit.

New sources

  • Market research: New and unique traffic sources (where your visitors come from) are found through market research (studying your buyers and rivals) in your specific industry, not copied from others (see Platform Choice and Channel Fit).

Channel Expansion Triggers

2 min

  • Google first: Expansion beyond Google only makes sense once the store is highly profitable and Google ads can no longer be scaled further (grown bigger).

  • Squeeze Google: Before adding new traffic channels, squeeze as much volume as possible out of Google, because splitting focus across channels too early dilutes results (makes them weaker).

  • Local platform trigger: Test local shopping platforms or comparison engines only after the store is profitable and cash reserves exist.

  • Profit trigger: Local shopping platforms become a valid option once the core business generates profit.

  • Mistake to avoid: Jumping to a new local channel while the core system (your main working setup) is not yet profitable or running splits attention and cash.

  • Do not switch while it works: Switching to unproven channels while the existing system works adds risk without a clear gain.

  • Do not launch many at once: Launching several traffic channels at once, such as Facebook ads and PLAs (Product Listing Ads, Google ads with product pictures and prices) together, spreads a beginner too thin and usually causes trouble. Build a foundation with one channel at a time and expand step by step.

  • Paid search first: Establish profitable paid search (ads on search engines like Google) first, then add SEO (search engine optimization, getting free visits from search results), social and content.

  • Do not rely only on paid: Do not rely entirely on paid ads for traffic, because paid advertising keeps getting more expensive. Add other channels so the business is less dependent on ad spend (money spent on ads).

  • Defensive advantage: Running several profitable traffic channels together, such as paid search, SEO, social, content and email, makes the business harder for new competitors to displace (push out), because each channel supports the others.

  • Mature business: A mature (established) business can run four or five profitable traffic streams.

  • Expansion order, store above $50,000 per month: Once a store is above $50,000 per month:

    1. Add YouTube ads to drive traffic (still under Google).
    2. Add Dream 100 outreach (contacting a list of 100 key partners or sites in your niche) and influencer partnerships (deals with people who have online audiences).
    3. Add ad platforms that fit your specific industry.

  • Testing share: At larger ad budgets, about 10% of the monthly ad budget can go to untested channels, while about 90% stays on proven campaigns (see Experimentation Budgets).

  • Keep learning the basics first: Learn each traffic channel in two passes, starting with a short platform overview before detailed material (see Experimentation Budgets).

Traffic Ownership Types

2 min

Controlled (paid) traffic

  • Definition: Controlled traffic is traffic you can direct anywhere, including solo ads (paying an email list owner to send your offer), banner ads (image ads on websites), native ads (ads made to look like the site's own content), pay-per-click (you pay each time someone clicks) on Facebook, Google and Yahoo, and affiliate (a partner earns a cut of each sale) or joint venture (a partner deal where you share profit) traffic. You pay again every time you want more of it. This makes paid traffic powerful but expensive to scale.
  • Paid is controlled and measurable: If a business's website converts visitors into customers (turns visitors into buyers), paying to acquire those customers is a sound strategy, since paid traffic is controlled and measurable (you can track it with numbers).

Free and organic traffic (organic means free results from normal search)

  • Organic and free listings: The Google organic search and free product listing rows of the traffic table are free (see Channel Overview).
  • Free-only stores: A store running on free traffic alone is valid for products where ads do not convert.
  • Free marketplace posts: Free posts in marketplaces can bring first sales before paid advertising begins.
  • Free Pinterest traffic: Pinterest works best as free traffic for stores with an existing audience.

Owned audience and email

  • Email and audiences: Building an audience from people who already acted on your site or social accounts lowers ad costs and raises conversion rates (the share of visitors who buy).
  • Email capture: Lead generation (collecting names and emails of interested people) pages need an email capture form (a box where visitors leave their email), and retargeting (showing ads again to people who already visited) plus email nurture (sending helpful emails over time) the visitor.
  • Email in the journey: Users sign up for an email list to get a coupon (a discount code), which builds trust before the purchase.

Outreach and partnerships

  • Dream 100 and affiliates: Traditional funnels (the path from first visit to purchase) include Dream 100 outreach (see Platform Choice and Channel Fit).
  • Affiliate and own website: Affiliate marketing, Amazon presence, organic search and the own website are named as examples of profitable streams.
  • Influencers: Influencer content (posts by people with online audiences) often works best as the first exposure before retargeting and an after-visit funnel (a follow-up path for people who already visited) for unsearched products (products people do not search for yet) (see Funnel Stages).
  • Omnipresence: Being present wherever the customer spends time online within the niche (see Platform Choice and Channel Fit).

Experimentation Budgets

2 min
  • Test each channel with real spend: Not every traffic channel pays off. Some work well while others mostly waste ad budget. Test each channel with real spend and measure the results instead of assuming one works.
  • Ten percent rule: At larger ad budgets, about 10% of the monthly ad budget can go to untested channels such as TikTok ads, Performance Max (Google's automatic campaign that mixes several channels), a new banner placement, or content-driven ads (ads that promote useful content), while about 90% stays on proven campaigns. For example, a $1,000 monthly budget splits into $900 proven and $100 testing.
  • Pinterest test budget: A daily budget (the most you spend per day) of about $5 for about seven days, roughly $35 in total (see Pinterest).
  • Pinterest budget comparison: The most profitable products ran at $5 per day, while some tests at $100 per day did not perform better early on.
  • Audience testing: Aim for at least 3 to 4 winning audiences (groups that bring sales) per product, so each can be scaled separately.
  • Reinvest the first sales: Reinvest first classified-ad sales into paid Google Ads at about $1,000 per month.
  • Cash reserves: Test local shopping platforms only after the store is profitable and cash reserves are built up.
  • Keep the test running: Do not stop paid ads when a niche product is only getting clicks without sales. Keep spending while optimizing (improving) keywords (search words you bid on), bids (the price you offer per click) and product pages.
  • Time to learn on TikTok: When switching an existing account to a new product, expect one to two weeks of slow results.
  • Organic first: Use organic content to find a winning product before spending on Spark Ads (TikTok ads that promote your own posts).
  • Learning passes (2024): Learn each channel in two passes: first a short overview covering Google Ads, Microsoft Ads (Bing's ad system), Meta Ads (Facebook and Instagram ads) and SEO (search engine optimization), then the detailed how-to material. Understanding the basics first makes the detailed material easier to apply.
  • Scaling readiness: Once the message resonates (connects well) with the customer, reaching US$1k per day or even US$10k per day becomes a scaling problem rather than a mystery.

Multi-Channel Journeys

2 min

  • Channels influence each other: Paid channels influence each other, so a campaign on one platform can lift (increase) total site traffic from other sources. Judge channels as a connected system, not as isolated silos (separate parts that do not talk to each other).

  • Typical journey with paid traffic (follow these steps in this order):

    1. Google automatically places a user who watches videos on a topic (e.g. health benefits of a product category) into an interest targeting category (a group of people who share an interest, chosen by Google).
    2. Show that user a relevant YouTube video ad to build brand awareness.
    3. The user later searches for a buyer's guide (an article that helps people choose a product), sees the brand in a Google search ad and clicks, which lifts CTR (click-through rate, the share of people who see an ad and click it).
    4. The user signs up for the email list to get a coupon, which builds trust.
    5. The user searches for the product and clicks a Shopping ad (a Google ad with a product picture and price), where the prior touchpoints (earlier moments the user met the brand) make conversion more likely.

  • Cross-platform lift: Facebook ads can drive branded search (people typing your brand name) on Google, so both platforms feed each other.

  • Flywheel (a loop that feeds itself): Cold video ads (ads for people who do not know you yet) prospect (find) large audiences and build a warm pool (people who already showed interest) as a byproduct (side result) (every 50% video viewer). Bottom-of-funnel ads (ads aimed at people close to buying) harvest that warm pool with high ROAS (return on ad spend, sales earned per dollar spent on ads). The longer cold video runs, the larger the warm pool grows, and the better bottom-of-funnel performs, all without changing bottom-of-funnel creative (the ad images and videos). This is why ROAS climbs passively (on its own) over time.

  • Shopper chain: Shopping ads, then remarketing (showing ads again to people who already visited) and trust-building emails, each setup feeding the next (see Meta).

  • Multi-channel thinking: The largest and most successful advertisers transfer learnings from Google Ads into content marketing (sharing useful articles and videos to attract buyers), use Google Ads to amplify (boost) content, and use paid traffic to grow social media and SEO. Plan each paid channel with its effect on the other channels in mind.

  • Four channels with four stages: Free organic search, free Shopping listings, paid Shopping ads and remarketing each cover a different stage of the buyer journey (the steps from first search to purchase).

  • Measurement: Use Google Analytics as the default measurement tool (a free tool that counts visitors and sales).

Advertising Principles

3 min

Foundation before tactics

  • Strategy first: Paid traffic results depend on a solid strategic foundation, and tactics (specific moves) only add value on top of it. Collecting tips without that foundation makes campaigns weaker, not stronger.
  • Tip versus strategy: A tip is a single action, while a strategy states the conditions under which that action works. For example, retargeting (showing ads again to people who already visited) is a tip that performs well in e-commerce (selling products online), but its strategic condition is that the first audience was targeted correctly.
  • Tips on a broken base: Relying on blog tips and tricks without a strong foundation leaves paid traffic performing worse, because the tips get applied to a broken base.

Cost and margin

  • Margins are thin: High-ticket dropshipping (selling expensive products that a supplier ships) has relatively modest margins (profit kept per sale), so the cost to acquire each customer must be controlled carefully. Each paid click has a direct cost, which is why selective targeting (choosing carefully who sees your ads) matters more in this model. Expect profitability to suffer if you buy every available click.
  • Business model matters: Paid clicks cost money and more spend usually brings more traffic, so business model matters as much as ad skill. A business model is how a business earns money. A business that can spend more to acquire a customer, due to a higher cart value (how much one order is worth) or a funnel (the path from visit to purchase) with lead magnets (free or cheap offers that collect contact details), can outbid (offer more than) competitors and appear in better positions more often.
  • Case, two surfing models: Store A sells a single surfboard for $800 and pays $100 to acquire a customer, advertising commercial terms (words that show buying intent) to people who are still asking whether they are strong enough to surf. Store B starts from the bottom of the funnel, sells surfboard packages with wax (a grip for the board) and wetsuit (a warm rubber suit for surfing) for $1,500, can afford $200 per customer acquisition, and offers a top-of-funnel (ads aimed at people just discovering) ebook (an electronic book) for $5 to collect leads (people who gave their contact details) who are later converted. Store B's higher cart value lets it outbid Store A and show more often in better positions, and the leads it collects feed more money into bottom-of-funnel ads.
  • Reading high click costs: If clicks look expensive in your niche, competitors may have found a better business model or funnel. Treat high click costs as a signal to examine how rivals fund acquisition, for example through higher cart values or cheap lead magnets.
  • Scaling: Once the message resonates, scaling (growing sales) is a question of execution rather than a mystery (see Experimentation Budgets).

Relevance and intent

  • Warm cheaper than cold: Advertising to warm visitors is cheaper than cold prospecting (finding people who do not know you yet).
  • Traffic quality: Traffic quality matters more than traffic volume (the number of visitors) (see Shopping and Search Intent).
  • Do not chase non-searchers: Spending on people who are not looking for the product wastes budget (see Funnel Stages).

Investment logic

  • Acquiring customers is an investment: If a business's website converts visitors into customers, paying to acquire those customers is a sound strategy, since paid traffic is controlled and measurable.

Channel-level caution

  • Not every channel pays off: Test each channel with real spend and measure results (see Experimentation Budgets).
  • Do not overspend on Shopping for undercut products (products priced below competitors): (see Shopping and Search Intent).

Lead Generation

1 min
  • Purpose: Lead generation targets top-of-funnel searchers (people at the first stage who search) who are not ready to buy yet. The goal is to build brand familiarity (how well people know the brand) and nurture them over time, so the brand is already trusted when they reach the buying stage, ideally before competitors do.
  • Payback timing: Lead generation is an investment that pays back over months, not on the first sale. Hot audiences (people ready to buy, such as those who search product names, brand names or SKU numbers, which are product code numbers) buy fastest, but lead generation targets people who are not ready to buy yet.
  • Three prerequisites: Lead generation with search ads depends on three elements: a content page (a page of useful information) with valuable content, retargeting set up for visitors of that page, and an email capture form on the page. Without follow-up retargeting and email, the visitor is not nurtured (kept interested over time) and the traffic produces no sales.
  • Lead magnets in the model: A top-of-funnel ebook for $5 collects leads who are later converted, and those leads feed more money into bottom-of-funnel ads (see Advertising Principles).
  • Email in the journey: A user who signs up for the email list to get a coupon builds trust before buying (see Multi-Channel Journeys).
