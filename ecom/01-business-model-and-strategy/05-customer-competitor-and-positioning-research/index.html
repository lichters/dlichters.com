Customer, competitor and positioning research

Section 1.5 · 5 of 23 in this chapter · 15 min read

In short

  • Pick one narrow buyer group before you write anything. Trying to reach everybody makes your message fail.
  • Buyers pass through five stages before they buy, and most do not notice them. Reach them early, not only at the moment of sale.
  • Know what makes you different from competitors, meaning your clear reason to be chosen, before you spend money on ads.
  • Sell the result customers want, not only the product. Answer the four questions in order first.

Customer avatars and target audience

2 min
  • A customer avatar is a written profile of one typical buyer. The target audience is the group of people you aim your business at.
  • The target audience definition has two goals.
    • First: understand not only what people do, but why they do it and what they need.
    • Second: communicate in a way they understand and connect with, so they take the action you want.
  • A precisely defined audience makes your message more relevant. It also helps you select better products.
  • A precisely defined audience improves user experience (how easy and pleasant a visit to your store feels), conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) and retention (how many customers come back).
  • Trying to serve everybody dilutes the message.
  • A broad audience makes marketing fail. Narrow the audience to a specific segment (one clear group with shared needs) before you write any messaging.
  • Customer avatar work is a foundational marketing task. It is basic groundwork for all marketing.
  • If you already have current profiles, review them only for accuracy.
  • If you have never done it, complete the full sheet.
  • There is no fixed number of customer avatars that fits every business.
  • The right count depends on the market, the niche (a small, specific part of a market) and the product category.
  • Decide the count separately for each one.
  • Answer these four questions in order before building any funnel (the path a buyer walks from first contact to purchase):
    1. Who is my dream client: the specific person I most want to work with, including their traits, goals, passions and income level?
    2. Where do they spend time online: which sites, groups, newsletters, blogs and platforms do they use?
    3. What bait will attract them and repel everyone else?
    4. What result do I want to deliver to them? A business sells results, not products.
  • Market and design a product where the customer journey (the steps a buyer takes from noticing a need to buying) is understood.
  • Sell a product for the real reason people buy it, such as the feeling of control or the desire for a hobby.

Customer awareness pyramid

4 min

Structure and stages

  • The customer awareness pyramid shows the five stages a buyer passes through before purchasing.
  • The five stages are: completely unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, and ninja aware.
  • The pyramid represents the whole market for a product.
  • Each higher stage contains fewer people. The bottom stage is the largest. The top stage is the smallest.
  • Customer awareness follows a five-stage pyramid: completely unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware and ninja aware.
  • Each buyer passes through these stages before purchasing. Usually they do not notice the steps.
  • The five stages in practice:
    • Completely unaware: the person does not know they have a problem. They feel the problem a product solves without knowing it. They form the largest part of the potential market.
    • Problem aware: the person knows the problem but not the solution. They start searching for a fix. The segment is still large, but smaller than the completely unaware stage.
    • Solution aware: the person knows which type of solution fits. They have not chosen a product type.
    • Product aware: the person has narrowed down to a product type. They compare variants.
    • Ninja aware: the person knows the exact product they want. They only decide where to buy it.
  • Ninja aware buyers make up roughly 10% or less of the total market at any given time.
  • Each stage is a smaller slice of the pyramid than the one below it.
  • If the best product is sold by only one seller, the ninja aware buyer has no choice of retailer (the shop they buy from).
  • If several sellers offer it, as with reselling (selling other companies' products), the buyer still chooses a retailer at the final stage.

Problem first

  • E-commerce sells solutions to problems or desires, not products alone.
  • The buyer first has a problem. Then they turn it into a want or need. Only then do they look for the product.
  • Marketing must therefore start from the problem the product solves.
  • Example of a beginner surfer:
    • First, the person wants to surf. Then they realise a surfboard is required. This is problem aware.
    • Next, they weigh buying against renting. They decide to buy because they plan to surf for years. This is solution aware.
    • They then compare board types for beginners. They choose one based on budget and reviews. This is product aware.
    • Finally, they pick a seller and pay. This is ninja aware.

Passing through the stages

  • Every buyer passes through all awareness stages.
  • This happens mostly subconsciously, as a natural process of learning before buying.
  • Ignoring this process lowers the conversion rate.
  • The time a customer takes from first noticing a need to buying varies widely. It can be a few days or several months.
  • Low-cost, simple products usually have short journeys.
  • Expensive or complex products, such as a $3,000 kitchen appliance, usually need longer consideration.

Mapping the pyramid to the funnel

  • The traditional sales funnel corresponds to the awareness pyramid turned upside down.
  • Top of funnel means completely unaware or problem aware.
  • Middle of funnel means solution aware or product aware.
  • Bottom of funnel means ninja aware, ready to buy.
  • The awareness pyramid is an inverted sales funnel, meaning the pyramid flipped upside down.
  • Completely unaware is the top of the funnel.
  • Solution aware sits mostly in the middle.
  • Product aware and ninja aware form the bottom, where buyers are closest to purchasing.
  • The two entries above place product aware buyers differently.
  • puts them in the middle of the funnel. puts them at the bottom with ninja aware.
  • Use the middle placement for a three-part view: top, middle and bottom.
  • Use the bottom placement when the question is which buyers are closest to purchasing.
  • The awareness pyramid is a framework for the customer journey. It also applies beyond copywriting (writing sales words).
  • It can guide email marketing, retargeting (showing ads again to people who already visited) and the type of content published for the business.

Where growth comes from

  • Many e-commerce businesses focus only on the bottom of the funnel (product aware and ninja aware).
  • That segment is small at any time and cannot grow.
  • The business stops scaling (growing its sales) once it has used up the available traffic there.
  • The market is far larger at the completely unaware, problem aware and solution aware stages.
  • It is much smaller at the product aware and ninja aware stages.
  • Scaling therefore requires reaching people earlier in the journey.
  • Be present at every stage of the pyramid.
  • Do not sit only at the bottom of the funnel, where competitors already hold the buyer's attention.

Order of work

  • Before building traffic (visitors to your store), work through the Know part first. It is the first of several work parts.
  • Understand the ideal customer, the competitors, the financial numbers and the business goals.
  • Then move on to Convert, Educate, Promote, Develop and Consolidate, in that order.

Market segmentation and niches

1 min
  • Market segmentation means splitting an audience into groups called segments.
  • Each segment shares common needs and characteristics.
  • Each segment reacts similarly to a given marketing message, so each segment can receive a tailored message (one made for it).
  • Market segmentation means grouping people who share characteristics, so you can serve them with a message fitted to them.
  • In a crowded market, narrowing the audience can shrink competition.
  • So a product already on the market can still win by targeting (choosing who sees your ads) a new segment.
  • Segmenting the audience is a way to find a less competitive position.
  • This works even for a product that already exists elsewhere.
  • Testing one specific audience can be worth doing before a broad approach, when platforms push toward broad targeting.
  • Any product can sell when the audience is narrowly defined.
  • The ad copy, creative (ad images and videos), landing page (a single page built for one offer) and emails must all address the same pain point (the problem that hurts the buyer).
  • Choosing a specific audience and angle (the main reason your message speaks to them) matters more than the product itself.
  • About 10% of a broad audience may recognise dropshipping (you sell, and a supplier ships straight to the customer).
  • A large audience of 100 million therefore still leaves about 90 million potential buyers.
  • Focus on service quality and product quality to win these buyers.
  • A market niche is a group of prospects (people who could become buyers) with well-defined, shared requirements.
  • Examples of such requirements: delivery to a job site, measured quantities or delivery within hours.
  • Find niches by listing the specific needs of a group. Then check whether your strengths meet them.
  • Use your previous industry experience to spot niches others have missed.

Identifying competitors

1 min
  • If you resell other brands, your closest competitors sell the exact same brands and products.
  • Second-level competitors sell the same type of product, even under other brands. List them too.
  • If you sell your own product, competitors are those selling a similar competing product.
  • This includes popular look-alike products sold only on Amazon.
  • Resellers should leave out Amazon, eBay and similar marketplaces (big sites where many sellers list items).
  • Resellers should focus on standalone websites that appear in the same search results as you.
  • A competitor is a store that sells for several brands. It has no own manufacturing and no physical location (no warehouse, retail store or showroom).
  • Two to three such online-only competitors with over 20 brands each is enough to call a niche drop-ship friendly (suitable for drop-shipping).
  • They do not all have to be in the exact niche.
  • Competitor sites that describe their products as factory direct (sold straight from the maker) are most likely drop-shipping from warehouses.
  • Treat that wording as a strong sign of drop-shipping.
  • Large retailers such as Wayfair (a big online home-goods shop) mostly drop ship the products they sell.
  • They also private label many of them, meaning they sell under their own brand name.
  • They hold little stock themselves.
  • Treat such a retailer as a future competitor. Do not treat it as a company to sell to, or a model to copy directly.

Competitor analysis sheet

2 min
  • A competitor analysis sheet uses one row per competitor, with these columns in this order.
  • Column terms in plain words:
    • USP: unique selling proposition, the clear reason a customer should pick you.
    • Proof points: evidence such as reviews, test results or awards.
    • Bonuses/premiums: free extras added to an offer.
    • Risk reversal: a guarantee that takes the buying risk off the customer, such as a refund.
    • Hook: the first line that grabs attention.
    • Primary marketing promise: the main result the marketing promises.
    • Features/deliverables: what the customer actually gets.
    • Price & terms: the price plus the conditions of buying.
    • Traffic sources: where visitors come from.
    • Email marketing: sending planned emails to subscribers.
    • Content: guides, articles, videos and similar material the competitor publishes.

  • The competitor analysis sheet is a living document. It keeps changing as you learn.
  • Review it at least once a year. Ideally, review it every six months.
  • Also review it whenever a new competitor appears or something new is learned.
  • The competitor sheet can be shared with a marketing team or employees.
  • Then they can quickly see what competitors are doing.
  • Keeping the sheet current is cheap and useful for the whole team.
  • Competitor analysis is an ongoing task, not a one-time check.
  • Review the top three competitors regularly to see what they add, change or drop.
  • Store-level competitor analysis: compare the store's product page with the top two to three competitor stores found by searching the product in Google.
  • Look at what the customer would see as a reason to buy elsewhere.
  • Shoppers often compare your store with other sellers of the same products.
  • The purpose of a competitor review is to find what the top competitors skip or do poorly.
  • Make those gaps the focus of your own store, so buyers have a clear reason to pick you.
  • Once traffic sources are mapped across all competitors, check which channels no competitor uses.
  • Such channels can reveal an open opportunity.
  • Record gaps in the market as carefully as the channels competitors already use.
  • If a competitor's USP or email marketing cannot be found, customers probably cannot find it either.
  • Treat it as effectively absent from the market.
  • Use this to judge which gaps in competitor offerings could be an opportunity.

Competing against large players

2 min
  • Entering a market with a big competitor selling the same product carries risk.
  • The big competitor already has trust, resources and scale (size and reach).
  • Look for differences the competitor does not use: a country, a marketing angle or a platform.
  • For example, check traffic in a specific country with SimilarWeb (a tool that estimates how many visitors a site gets).
  • When a big brand already sells the same product, do not compete head-on (directly, in the same way) with the same market and angle.
  • Look for an unused country. Check traffic via Similarweb.
  • Or look for a different marketing angle.
  • Or look for a platform the brand does not use, such as Pinterest (a picture-sharing site).
  • A niche-specialist store (a shop focused on one narrow area) can compete with large generalists such as Wayfair, which sells over a million products.
  • Build the store for one specific buyer type.
  • Study competitor weaknesses.
  • Position yourself as the authority in that niche.
  • Choose a product category where the market has known problems, such as trust or quality issues.
  • Solve them publicly.
  • Being open about formulas, sourcing (where products come from) and testing is a differentiator (a feature that sets you apart) in such categories.
  • Most competitors avoid these topics.
  • Competing with Amazon works through specialist expertise, niche focus, buying guides, product bundles, free bonus gifts and direct relationships with brands.
  • Amazon sells everything. But it offers less specific advice, fewer special bundles and less industry-specific content.
  • Do not try to beat Amazon on price.
  • Its algorithms (automatic computer rules) track the lowest price and drop their own price further.
  • Once the price falls, Amazon rarely raises it again.
  • Before entering a niche, check whether the top competitors run strong sites with guides, videos and reviews.
  • If they do, plan content of the same quality or better from day one.
  • Do not launch a basic store instead.
  • A store that copies the default supplier descriptions (the text the company that ships your products provides) and a few basic images will rarely beat a competitor with richer content.
  • Google will not rank (show higher in search results) the bare minimum above established high-quality sites.

Value, USP and differentiation

2 min

  • Many conversion problems come from a weak understanding of the market.

  • They rarely come from the store's design.

  • A business must know where it fits in its market.

  • It must also know why customers should pick it over rivals.

  • Once it knows this, most marketing decisions get easier.

  • A business is fundamentally about solving real problems for other people.

  • Products are the means of doing that.

  • Growing a large e-commerce business requires communicating clearly how the business and its products solve problems that real people actually experience.

  • Answers about the market and the competition feed copywriting, on-site conversion work, ad copy, email content and retargeting ads.

  • A clear picture of competitors makes all of these tasks easier.

  • Before choosing marketing actions, a store owner should be able to explain, in a few lines:

    • why customers would buy from them rather than from competitors, and
    • what makes the business distinct.

  • Understand the ideal customer first. Then the competitors. Only then the point of difference.

  • The competitor analysis is a starting point for every later decision.

  • Turn its findings into action items, such as a new USP, a new email sequence (a planned series of emails) or a new content plan.

  • The purpose is to find where your business can differ from the others.

  • Define your business differentiator before scaling.

  • A differentiator is what sets you apart from competitors. It includes your products and the business mission (the purpose of your business).

  • It determines where you fit in the market, so it is the most important input for later planning.

  • Frame the business around the result you give customers, rather than the product you sell.

  • When the result is clear and valuable enough, price stops being the main barrier to buying.

  • Identify the highest-level result you could deliver, even if most customers first buy smaller pieces.

  • Alex Homosi's value equation (a simple formula for how much a customer values an offer). The author's name is spelled this way in the source. It is probably Alex Hormozi, so check the spelling before you quote it.

    value = (increase perceived likelihood of achieving the dream outcome, or getting away from pain) combined with (reduce time, effort and sacrifice)

  • The dream outcome (the best result the customer wants) and perceived success probability (how likely the customer thinks they are to succeed) go up.

  • Time, effort and sacrifice go down, because those are scarce resources people try to conserve.

Price, quality and service strengths

1 min
  • Every business has three areas of market strength: price, quality and service.
  • In a competitive market, a company generally cannot be the best in all three at once.
  • So decide which combination you truly offer before you choose prospects.
  • Match your strengths to the segments that value those strengths most.
  • Avoid segments whose priorities you cannot meet.
  • A good offer aimed at buyers who rank a different factor first still wastes effort.
  • Classify a feature, such as on-time delivery, by what the business sells.
  • For a packaging supplier, on-time delivery is part of service.
  • For an overnight carrier (a company that delivers packages overnight), on-time delivery is the product's quality. Service then covers tracking, billing and courtesy.
  • The category decides which buyers will value it.

Qualifying prospects

1 min
  • Sort every prospect into one of four groups.
  • Spend most of your time only on the first group.
    1. Ready to buy: needs, wants and can afford the product.
    2. Needs and can afford it but does not want it.
    3. Needs and wants it but cannot afford it.
    4. Needs, wants and can afford it but prefers another supplier.
  • Time and resources are limited.
  • So every call to a low-probability prospect costs the chance to reach a likely buyer.
  • Qualifying out most of the market early (ruling people out early) is the normal way to work. It is not a missed sale.
  • Select customers who purchase online independently.
  • Select customers who accept standard delivery timelines without requesting constant updates.
  • Select customers who are satisfied with the completed transaction.
  • Avoid haggling customers (people who push hard on price), chronic complainers and those seeking continuous contact.
  • They demand excessive personal attention.
  • That conflicts with a self-managing business (one that runs with little hands-on work).

From segment to message

1 min
  • Before touching any ad platform, understand your market, your audience and your customers.
  • Ads run without that groundwork waste money and time on any platform.
  • Platforms include Facebook, Google, YouTube, Amazon and Pinterest.
  • Paid ads should not show a buy-now offer to everyone.
  • Most people in a market are not ready to purchase today.
  • The rough benchmark is that under 10% of searchers (people who search online) are ready to buy now.
  • Segment the audience by readiness.
  • Treat each segment with a different message.
  • The sales funnel runs in five steps: Awareness, Interest, Learn, Shop, Buy.
  • Each stage needs its own kind of message.
  • First make people aware of the solution. Then give them interest. Then let them learn and compare. Then let them shop, and finally buy.
  • Digital marketing and sales funnel models vary. This five-stage sequence is the common baseline.
  • Process for each client, packaged as a repeatable product (the same steps every time), so it can be rolled out for every client and scaled:
    1. Know the market.
    2. Choose a segment.
    3. Craft the message.
    4. Prepare the creative.
    5. Prepare the structure, which means the store or the product-specific landing page.
