Product photography and image editing

Section 12.19 · 19 of 26 in this chapter · 31 min read

In short

  • Check the licence (the permission rules for reuse) before you use any image.
  • Never use Alibaba pictures or competitor photos on product pages.
  • Use stock or supplier photos while a product is untested (not yet proven to sell). Take your own photos once it sells.
  • Start from a clean, sharp, front-facing photo. A weak photo cannot be fixed later.
  • Make a square 1080 × 1080 pixel image with the product centred.
More key points
  • Use one plain light-grey background (hex E3E3E3) for every product photo, so the set looks the same.
  • Check every cut-out edge for leftover bits and thin dark lines before you publish.
  • Show the product in real use, with real people, and give each product page more than one photo.
  • Use Canva for quick, cheap edits. Use Photoshop for precise cut-outs. Pure white is still required for product listing ads.

Sourcing and licensing product images

4 min
  • Search in this order: Pexels, Pixabay, Google Images, Alibaba, AliExpress. Pexels and Pixabay are free stock photo sites (libraries of ready-made photos).
  • If none of these works, search for a very similar product.
  • Or research hashtags (topic tags such as #homedecor) on Instagram and use raw UGC-style images. UGC means user-generated content: real-looking photos from customers, not studio shots.
  • Stock photo sites suit homepage images (the front page of your store).
  • Product pages need the actual product photo. Use filtered Google image results (results narrowed down by search settings) or supplier images there.
  • Stock sites cost about $1 to $2 per image.
  • Canva's free stock photos work well for popular niches. A niche is one narrow market, such as pet beds.
  • For niches with few stock photos, such as mechanical or basic furniture products, download photos from a stock photo website.
  • Then upload them to Canva. Canva is an online design tool.
  • Google Images, licensed use (2023 method):
    1. Search the product on Google Images.
    2. Click Tools.
    3. Click Usage rights (the reuse permissions) and pick a Creative Commons option. Creative Commons is a free licence that says how a photo may be reused.
  • Google Images, licensed use (2019, older method):
    1. Search for the product on Google Images.
    2. Click Tools below the search bar.
    3. Open the usage rights menu.
    4. Filter for images labelled for reuse, and use only those.
  • Google Images results are often small, and their licensing is unclear.
  • Check the dimensions (width and height in pixels) of every result before you use it.
  • Do not use Alibaba pictures on product pages. Alibaba is a large supplier marketplace where factories sell to shops.
  • Most Alibaba listings do not match the product you sell. The images are also not licensed for resale (permission to sell them to buyers).
  • Replace watermarked supplier photos too. A watermark is a logo or text laid over a picture.
  • AliExpress and CJ Dropshipping photos are often good enough for home decor ads (ads for home items). AliExpress is a global shopping site with many sellers. CJ Dropshipping is a supplier platform.
  • So a photographer is not needed to start testing these products. Testing means checking whether people want a product before you invest in it.
  • For early fashion tests, use supplier photos from AliExpress as ad creatives (the images or video used in ads).
  • Do this instead of paying for a photo shoot.
  • Keep production cost low until the product has validated sales (proof that people buy it).
  • Once you scale (grow sales and ad spending), avoid showing raw AliExpress images on a product page.
  • Use a sizing guide built in your brand colours instead of a generic supplier image. Branding is your store's look: colours, logo and style.
  • Generic supplier photos from marketplace-style listings look unbranded and reduce trust.
  • Replacing them with Canva-edited images that match the store's branding improves credibility.
  • Use royalty-free images (photos you can use without paying each time), or photos supplied by the brand that you have permission to use.
  • Do not use competitors' product images on the store or in ads. A competitor is a rival store.
  • Competitor photos can be modified for uniqueness.
  • AI tools can transform original photos into unique variations.
  • Images can be sourced from competitor websites if they are not protected.
  • If a site blocks saving images, take a screenshot (a picture of your screen) on a Mac.
  • Or use an image enhancement tool.
  • An image enhancement tool is a website that upscales (enlarges and sharpens) low-resolution product images.
  • It can be used for free after you create an account.
  • "Stock Images (Free)" was named as a free stock photo source in the December 2020 coaching call.
  • This source names the resource, but does not name the provider or describe its licence.
  • Until a supplier is approved, and its images can be used, use placeholders (temporary stand-ins).
  • Placeholders can be AI-generated demo product photos or licensed stock photos.
  • This avoids using supplier images without permission.
  • Stock product photos with a single angle are fine for demo products.
  • Use real photos once products are approved and sold.
  • Supplier product photos that look like 3D renders (computer-made pictures) are a weak choice.
  • If you must use them, disclose (say openly) that they are renders. Then ask customers for real photos.
  • Keep watermarks very subtle and hard to see.
  • Do not set a watermarked image as the primary image. The primary image is the main first picture.
  • Do not set it for a product variant either. A variant is a version of a product, such as one colour.
  • Google Merchant Center is Google's product feed for shopping ads. It rejects listings whose primary image has a watermark.
  • Keep watermarked images on the product page.

Own photos versus supplier and stock images

1 min
  • Taking your own product photos is usually not worth the cost. Matching supplier-quality images requires a professional photo shoot (a paid session with a photographer).
  • Use supplier-approved images when available.
  • Keep production cost low until a product has validated sales.
  • Invest in photo shoots and edited videos once a product shows success.
  • Generic supplier footage (raw video clips from the supplier) on Google performs poorly.
  • Shooting your own photos of received samples (the test items from the supplier) is a practical starting point when no professional production shoot is available.
  • Images and video must look professional.
  • Summary: stock and supplier images apply while a product is untested.
  • Shoot your own photos once sales validate the product.
  • A low-budget home setup is covered below.

Low-budget and home photo setup

1 min
  • Budget smartphone setup (prices in US dollars).
  • A used coil light, bought second-hand for about $20, works as the key light. The key light is the main light on the product.
  • Cheap LED ring lights from Amazon at $10 to $15 serve as additional or fill light. A fill light is a softer second light that lightens shadows.
  • A white card plus a few sheets of paper from a stationery shop cost about $7 in total.
  • Add a sturdy stand with a spirit level (a bubble tool that shows if something is flat).
  • Add an adjustable tripod (a three-legged camera stand).
  • Add a cheap smartphone clamp adapter (a clip that holds a phone on a stand).
  • Home photo procedure (Photoshop costs about £10 per month). Photoshop is Adobe's photo editing software.
    1. Photograph the product on an ordinary surface such as a bed or a table.
    2. Cut the product out of the background in Photoshop. This is called a cutout.
    3. Place the cutout on cleaner or different backgrounds for the ad.

Starting from a clean source photo

2 min
  • Before generating a Shopping image with AI, start with a clean source photo. A Shopping image is the picture shown in Google Shopping ads.
  • A clean source photo is front-facing (facing the camera), high resolution, with the product clearly the focus. Resolution means how many pixels (tiny dots) the picture has.
  • Input quality determines output quality: what goes in decides what comes out.
  • So replace a weak source photo first.
  • Delete photos that are too soft or pixelated (made of visible blocks), even if they look usable at a glance.
  • They will look worse once enlarged.
  • Discard shots that are too low in resolution for the intended use.
  • Camera resolution matters.
  • If product images are pixelated, edit them with an AI image tool.
  • Or hire a Fiverr freelancer (a person hired on the Fiverr site) for about $5 to $10.
  • Ask them to retouch the background and make the product stand out.
  • Never enlarge a small image to fit a larger banner space.
  • It becomes pixelated and unprofessional.
  • The image should be at least the size of the area where it is displayed.
  • Resize an image before cropping it (cutting the edges off).
  • Reduce width or height to the target first.
  • Crop only the side you do not need if one dimension is still too large.
  • Before cutting out a product, check the original shot.
  • A straight-on, centred angle and a clean, well-lit photo give a better result.
  • Odd angles or low-quality sources are harder to cut out.
  • Product photos should be shot straight on at a suitable angle.
  • An odd angle can look awkward even after a clean cutout.
  • A perfectly head-on, dead-centre shot can look flat on a product page.
  • Choose a better angle during the shoot.
  • Background removal cannot fix composition (how the picture is arranged).
  • Products that already sit on a clean, separated background need little or no cutout work.
  • A white product on a white backdrop is hard to cut out because the edges blend in.
  • Shoot light-coloured products on a plain grey or contrasting backdrop.
  • Good lighting and correct setup matter more than heavy editing.
  • Good light lets the phone capture the most detail.
  • Blurred or dark images can be partly rescued, but not fully corrected.

Photo shoot setup: backdrop and surface

1 min
  • Use a matte white or neutral backdrop. Matte means it does not shine.
  • The backdrop must be monochromatic (one colour family only), with no blue or green tones.
  • Colours bleed into the product: colour from the background spills onto it.
  • Matte surfaces avoid harsh reflections (bright shine spots).
  • Darker backdrops such as black or grey also work if they are monochromatic.
  • Set up the surface:
    1. Lay white paper over a flat surface such as a chest of drawers. Let it curve upward into the wall in a cove shape (a smooth curve with no corner line), with no visible horizon line (the line where floor meets wall).
    2. Tape it to the wall and floor.
    3. Make it large enough to frame the product from several angles.

Photo shoot setup: light

1 min
  • Diffuse hard light (soften it) before it reaches the product.
  • A direct source creates harsh glare and shine.
  • An umbrella-style diffuser (a white cover that softens light) in front of the light softens it into cloud-like light.
  • Or hang a white pillowcase in front of a ring light. It works the same way.
  • Natural light works best, ideally from large windows on an overcast day (a cloudy sky).
  • Clouds filter sunlight into soft light.
  • When natural light is unavailable, use artificial lights with a diffuser.
  • Adjust the key light's distance before adding diffusion.
  • Moving it changes how strong and harsh the light is.
  • The diffuser then works on the right level.
  • Reduce shadows with a fill light:
    1. Place a second light or a white reflector card (a white card that bounces light back) on the opposite side of the key light.
    2. Move the fill light farther away so it only softens the shadow edges.
    3. Check the result by watching how the shadow lightens when the card is introduced.
  • Watch for shadows the photographer casts on the product.
  • Keep the camera's position on the side opposite the key light, so it does not block the light.
  • For apparel shoots (photos of clothing), check that light falls evenly on the product.
  • Lighting that falls on elbow pads or blocks the face makes the subject hard to cut out cleanly.
  • Re-shoot with more even light rather than trying to fix it in Photoshop.

Photo shoot setup: camera and phone

1 min
  • Use a camera with at least 1080p resolution. 1080p is a standard HD picture size.
  • Most phones support it.
  • Use the basic camera app.
  • Avoid manual settings such as ISO (how sensitive the sensor is to light) or aperture (the lens opening).
  • Turn on the 3x3 grid overlay, the faint lines that split the screen into nine squares.
  • Use the grid to keep the product centred and level.
  • Tap the subject to set focus (make it sharp).
  • Smartphones show a level guide when tilted.
  • Use it together with the 3x3 grid to align a straight product shot so both sides are symmetrical.
  • Do not use the telephoto or zoom lens on a smartphone for product shots. Image quality is poor.
  • Use the standard main camera, and move the phone closer for close-ups.
  • Shoot wider than the final crop (the frame you keep), so the image can be cut to a square later.
  • Keep the phone away from shutter buttons on the stand.
  • An accidental tap can fill the camera roll (the phone's photo library) with duplicates.
  • Position the stand where no physical buttons sit under the lens or near your hands.

Photo shoot setup: angles and details

1 min
  • Shoot a product from several angles:
    1. Take a dead-level straight-on shot at product height.
    2. Take more angles from the sides, front and back.
    3. Take a high-angle shot from above.
    4. Take a flat shot from below. For drink cans, this makes them look powerful.
    5. Take a top-down angled shot.
    6. Keep the camera fixed and rotate the product to capture other sides, if it has several good angles.
  • Shoot a wide variety of angles, since not every shot will be usable.
  • Choose the best few for cutout.
  • Keep the others for social use without editing.
  • For apparel, capture several angles such as front, side, bent-arm and full-body frames.
  • Accessories or equipment should not cover the model's face.
  • Only the best frame of each angle needs a full cutout.
  • Photograph product features: take close-ups of buttons, controls, bases, lids and other distinctive details.
  • Show functional features, such as a product floating in water.
  • Shoot close-ups at minimum focus distance (the closest the camera can focus).
  • Move the phone back slightly until the focus is sharp.
  • A product image must show the entire product in its natural state.
  • It must also highlight its unique features.
  • Pick sharp, well-lit shots.
  • Include a close-up of the most distinctive feature for each product.
  • Capture close-ups of fabric, weight and detail.
  • This helps buyers understand quality before purchase.

Lightroom corrections

1 min
  • Division of work: Lightroom (Adobe's photo app for colour and light) handles quick crops and simple exposure adjustments.
  • Photoshop handles masking (hiding parts of a picture), cutouts and retouching (small repairs).
  • Basic light correction order:
    1. Exposure: brighten dark photos.
    2. Contrast: sharpen or soften the difference between light and dark areas.
    3. Highlights down: reduce overly bright white areas.
    4. Shadows down: add depth.
    5. Check Whites and Blacks for the tonal range (the spread from darkest to brightest).
    6. Use the crop tool and choose the 1:1 square ratio for product images.
  • Colour Mixer (HSL panel; HSL means hue, saturation, luminance).
  • Select a colour channel such as yellow, and increase its saturation (colour strength) to make it pop.
  • Or desaturate a channel such as purple, to remove an unwanted colour cast (a colour tint over the photo).
  • Keep the product's true colours.
  • Adjust colour only to correct a colour cast or to match the real item.
  • Heavy shifts make the product look different from what the customer receives.
  • Judge exposure and contrast by eye on the screen, not by fixed values.
  • Move each slider until the image looks right.
  • Then check the result side by side with the original.
  • Use a simple Lightroom crop for photos that only need framing.
  • Once a cutout is saved from Photoshop, the file returns to Lightroom.

Square canvas and image size

1 min
  • Standard product image size is square at 1080 × 1080 pixels.
  • Crop to a square with the 1:1 ratio (equal width and height).
  • Save at this size for consistency across listings.
  • Non-square product images create uneven gaps in the image layout.
  • Crop all product images to the same square ratio before uploading.
  • Set up a square canvas in Photoshop:
    1. Hold the Selection tool icon in the toolbar to reveal the Artboard tool (a tool that makes a fixed frame).
    2. Draw a square artboard and resize it to the size you need.
    3. Fit the product photo inside it, so the subject sits roughly centred.
  • Draw the square artboard with the Artboard tool, hidden under the selection tool group (the set of tools behind one icon).
  • Then scale it with Ctrl+T (the transform shortcut for resizing) while holding Shift to keep the ratio.

Background removal in Photoshop: full workflows

3 min
  • Cutout workflow, Photoshop (eight steps):
    1. Draw a square artboard with the Artboard tool (hidden under the selection tool group). Then scale it with Ctrl+T while holding Shift to keep the ratio.
    2. Click Select Subject in the top bar. This is Photoshop's automatic selection of the product. Then check the result.
    3. Add missing areas with the brush (plus), or remove extra areas with the brush held in minus mode.
    4. Deselect with Ctrl+D. Then refine openings and edges by brush or lasso (a freehand selection tool).
    5. Add a layer mask with Select and Mask. A layer mask is a hidden layer that hides the background without deleting it. Select and Mask is the panel for refining edges. Use the left toolbar to clean up the cutout first.
    6. Smooth, feather and Shift Edge in the right-side panel last. Feather softens the edge. Shift Edge moves the edge inward or outward.
    7. Click OK with Output To set to Layer Mask, so the original photo is kept.
    8. Retouch spots with the Spot Healing Brush (removes small marks) and the Clone Stamp (copies one area over another). Then save with Ctrl+S so the file returns to Lightroom.
  • Cutout procedure, Photoshop (six steps):
    1. Click Select Subject in the top menu bar.
    2. Check the result, and add or subtract missing areas with the Quick Selection tool. Use the plus sign to add, hold Shift for minus, or Alt for minus in the lasso.
    3. Open Select and Mask.
    4. Refine edges with the Refine Edge brush for hair, and the brush for gaps.
    5. Use Smooth, Feather, Contrast and Shift Edge in the right-hand panel.
    6. Under Output To, choose Layer Mask, not Selection. Selection would delete the background. Then click OK.
  • Cutout checklist, Photoshop (eight steps):
    1. Cut out the subject (Select Subject, then refine).
    2. Refine edges with the brush, lasso, and Refine Edge for hair.
    3. Smooth the edge subtly.
    4. Feather only if the background contrast is low.
    5. Apply Shift Edge (about 30 to 40) to remove dark fringes (dark edge lines).
    6. Output as Layer Mask.
    7. Remove spots with Spot Healing Brush, and blemishes or stitching with Clone Stamp.
    8. Add an E3E3E3 fill layer behind the subject. E3E3E3 is a light grey colour code. Resize the subject as needed.
  • Select Subject, then Layer Mask:
    • Check the edges after Select Subject.
    • Add missing areas with a Layer Mask brush set to hard brush hardness (100%).
    • Use the Polygonal Lasso tool for straight edges.
    • Use Select and Mask to smooth edges and shift the edge inward slightly. Then click Layer Mask.
  • If a selection is active when you click Add Layer Mask, the mask is built from that selection.
  • The background is hidden automatically.
  • Use this right after Select Subject for a non-destructive cutout in one step. Non-destructive means the original stays untouched.
  • Use a layer mask rather than deleting pixels.
  • The mask is non-destructive: the original is preserved.
  • The mask can be deleted to restore the original.
  • It can be edited later by clicking it.
  • A layer-mask cutout can be reversed or reworked.
  • Drag the mask onto the trash icon and confirm to restore the original photo.
  • Or click the mask thumbnail in the Layers panel to reopen Select and Mask.
  • In Select and Mask, use the brush in minus mode to remove leftover background.
  • Check the result before clicking OK.
  • Always edit through layers rather than painting directly on the photo.
  • Adjustment layers (edits that change colour or light without touching the original) and layer masks keep the original intact.

Halos, edge fixes and colour differences

1 min
  • A thin dark or blue halo after cutout comes from the original background colour.
  • Fix it with Shift Edge inward.
  • Then remove the remaining colour tint with a Hue/Saturation adjustment layer (a layer that changes colour strength).
  • Reduce saturation on the affected area only.
  • For minor colour differences between garments and the background, use a Hue/Saturation adjustment layer.
  • Clip it over only the affected area. Clipping means attaching the layer so it changes just that spot.
  • This reduces colour cast.
  • For small differences, it is often not worth the extra layering.
  • Resize a cutout subject inside its frame:
    1. Select the subject's layer in the Layers panel.
    2. Press Ctrl+T to open free transform (a box you can drag to resize).
    3. Hold Shift to keep the aspect ratio (the width-to-height shape). Hold Alt to scale outward from the centre.
    4. Drag to the target size, then press Enter to confirm.

Retouching in Photoshop

1 min
  • Spot Healing Brush (a Photoshop tool that copies nearby pixels over a flaw) removes small spots, hair and blemishes (skin marks).
    • Use short sharp clicks instead of dragging.
    • Keep the brush small.
    • Use some feather (a soft fade at the edge of the brush) so the repair blends in.
    • Dragging across high-contrast areas (places where light and dark meet sharply) can smear the repair.
  • Clone Stamp (a tool that paints a copy of one area onto another) fixes stitching (sewn seams) or larger blemishes that the Spot Healing Brush cannot fix.
    • Hold Alt (a keyboard key) and click to sample a clean source area.
    • Release Alt, then paint over the defect.
    • Clone from an area with the same light direction. Lighting changes make the copy look too light or too dark.
  • Remove overlaid text or a white background from a lifestyle shot (a photo of the product being used in real life):
    1. Use Select Subject (a Photoshop command that picks the main object automatically) to cut out only the subject, for example an arm. Refine it in Select and Mask (the Photoshop panel for cleaning up selection edges).
    2. Add a layer mask (a hidden cover on a layer that hides pixels without deleting them) so the subject is isolated without deleting pixels.
    3. Add a grey fill layer (Edit > Fill, Colour E3E3E3) behind the subject, so the overlaid text and white areas disappear.
  • When a photo has text or other objects on it, cut out only the usable part of the subject. Place it on the plain grey background, so the text disappears.

Background fill and backdrop colour

1 min
  • Standard product backdrop: a neutral light grey, hex E3E3E3 (a colour code written in letters and numbers). It equals RGB 227, 227, 227 (the same colour as three numbers for red, green and blue).
    • In Photoshop, add a new layer.
    • Choose Edit > Fill, set Contents to Color, and enter the value.
    • Place the filled layer below the product photo, not above it.
  • Use one consistent light-grey background (E3E3E3 in the example) across all product images, so the set looks uniform.
    • Apply the same fill to every cutout (a product cut away from its original background).
    • Check it visually after placing the subject.
  • Off-white (a soft white that is not pure white) looks more attractive than pure white for product photography.
    • Add a new layer under the product.
    • Use Edit > Fill and set the colour to off-white.
  • An off-white or slightly grey backdrop hides cut-out edges better than pure white.
    • Choose the final background colour before cutting out the product, so the edges are planned for it.
  • Pure white is still the standard for product listing ads (ads that show a product picture, name and price). Keep standard white-background photos for them.
  • A light-grey background that matches the website's colour scheme is a practical default. Cutouts then fit the store's look.
  • Product photos for a store with a light-grey colour scheme can use a plain light-grey background that matches the theme. Other colours are possible in Canva (an online design tool), but light grey suits that palette.

Shadows

1 min
  • Add a simple shadow in Photoshop:
    1. Draw an ellipse (a flat oval or circle) with the Ellipse tool. Set fill to black and stroke (outline) to none.
    2. Convert the layer to a smart object (a layer that keeps its settings, so you can change them later).
    3. Apply Filter > Blur > Gaussian Blur (a filter that softens the edges evenly).
    4. Position it under the product.
    • A thick, tight blur gives a shadow close to the floor.
    • Blur more and move the shape further back for a shadow that looks farther away.
    • Side shadows: copy the layer, use Alt or Option (keyboard keys) to clip it to the image layer (attach it so it only shows over the image), and transform it with Command+T (resize or rotate).
  • Refine a shadow with a layer mask.
    • Add a mask to the shadow layer.
    • Paint away parts with a black brush, or use the Gradient tool (a tool that fades from one colour to another) to fade the shadow along the left edge.
    • Tune it until it looks convincing.
  • Canva background removal also removes the shadow. Add shadows back using Elements > Shadows (Elements is Canva's library of shapes and effects).
  • PhotoRoom's AI Instant Backgrounds (an automatic feature that places the product on a ready-made background) places the product on a pre-made background with shadows.

Generative Fill and AI-based background work

1 min
  • Remove a background with Generative Fill (Photoshop's AI tool that creates new image content in a selected area):
    1. Use the Lasso tool (a tool for drawing a freehand outline) to draw around the product. Keep the shadow inside the selection if you want to keep it.
    2. Press Ctrl+Shift (Command+Shift on Mac) to invert the selection (swap the selected and unselected parts) to the background.
    3. Right-click and choose Generative Fill, then click Generate to create a clean background.
    4. Pick one of the generated options.
  • Canva's Magic Media tool (Canva's AI image maker and editor) handles most product image creation and editing needs.
  • Canva's AI erase tool (a tool that removes unwanted objects with AI) is a basic option.
  • Use AI-generated background images for social media posts and email campaigns, and occasionally for product pages.
    • Keep standard white-background photos for product listing ads.
  • Before a Shopping image (a product picture used in Google Shopping ads) is generated with AI, check the source photo. Replace a weak source first.

Canva background removal and edits

2 min
  • Canva background removal: open the uploaded image, choose Edit photo, then select Background remover. Canva's Magic Grab (a tool that lifts an object off the page) lifts the object off the page.
  • Canva one-click removal, with refinement:
    1. Open the image and click Edit photo.
    2. Click Remove background and let Canva process it.
    3. Check the result. For a clean look, choose a plain light-grey background, which matches a website colour scheme.
    4. If parts were cut out wrongly, refine after the fact. Click Adjust, then Configure, then use Remove and Restore (tools that erase or bring back parts of the cutout).
    5. Use the brush-size control and zoom in to erase leftover lumps with Erase, or restore cut-out areas with Restore.
    6. Check edges for thin black lines and remove them manually.
  • Refinement tools are found under Adjust > Configure, which offers Remove and Restore. Brush size can be changed, and the area can be zoomed in for finer work.
  • Canva's background removal may leave thin dark lines around the edges of a cutout. Erase them manually with the Erase tool at high zoom. Otherwise the image looks unpolished on the product page.
  • Canva's auto cutout (automatic cut-out) does not always get edges right.
    • Check every edge.
    • Remove leftover fragments before exporting (saving the file for use).
    • Hair, small gaps and white fringes (thin light edges) are common problem areas.
  • Canva's one-click removal is fast. It works well when speed matters more than precision.
    • It gives less control than Photoshop.
    • Blemishes are hard to remove.
    • Shadows are harder to handle.
    • Edges may not be clean enough for a premium product page.
  • Canva's refinement is rougher ("a ball park", meaning roughly right) than Photoshop's precise edge tools.
  • For a precise cutout, such as clothing with blemishes or detailed edges, use Photoshop. Use the selection tool, brushes, and edge refinement such as Shift Edge (a Photoshop setting that shrinks or grows a selection's edge).
  • Canva colour adjustment: open Edit photo > Adjust > Color. Pick individual colours that Canva detects, and change saturation (how strong a colour is) or vibrance (which boosts muted colours).
    • Canva's contrast control (the gap between light and dark areas) is less precise than Lightroom's (Adobe's photo editor).
  • Canva can make a simple change to a supplier image (a product photo from the seller). Cut the product out and change the background colour. This is enough to make a generic image look distinct when no major edit is needed.
  • Edit supplier product photos so their backgrounds match the colour scheme of the landing page (the page a visitor arrives on). Use Canva or Photoshop to create a branded look.
  • Canva's Magic Grab, Background remover and AI erase tools form the cheaper beginner option, with basic colour correction.

Making supplier images look unique

1 min
  • Procedure:
    1. Take the supplier product image.
    2. Remove the background in Canva.
    3. Place the product on a new background.
    4. Adjust the colouring slightly, so it looks like a different image.
    5. Add your brand name to the image before publishing.
  • Modify competitor photos (photos from rival sellers) for uniqueness. AI tools can transform original photos into unique variations.
  • Replace generic supplier photos with Canva-edited images that match the brand.
  • Edit backgrounds to match the landing page colour scheme for a branded look.
  • Use a sizing guide (a chart showing product sizes) built in your brand colours, instead of a generic supplier image.

Product page image set

2 min
  • Every product page needs more than one photo. A single photo makes a purchase unlikely.
    • If the supplier has only one, ask for more, search the internet, or create variations.
    • Variations can be: the product alone, the brand logo, and the available colour options.
  • Upload the clean white contoured studio images first. "Contoured" means cut out to follow the product's shape.
    • Also add at least two styled images in the product media section.
    • Use both studio and lifestyle images, with people wearing the product.
  • Order product images for a listing (the set of pictures on one product page):
    1. Place the main straight-on product shot first, as customers expect.
    2. Add images of the product from different angles.
    3. Add close-up images showing material or mechanism (how it works).
    4. Add lifestyle images showing the product in use. These can optionally be embedded in the description instead of the gallery.
    • Upload all images that are relevant to the purchase decision.
  • Image set order, Source D 2026:
    1. Product hero image (the main big picture): the product on a pure white background, generated with the hero image prompt.
    2. Value proposition headline image (the main promise of the product, in big text): an image with a large caption based on the value proposition headline.
    3. Homepage photos.
    4. Product photos for the product page.
    • Every image headline, bullet and visual suggestion must reflect real customer language and the emotional desire of the target buyer.
  • A homepage hero image is valuable space. It can be a tight crop (a close zoom) of one of the product photos. Use a strong product photo for it.
  • Show the product's dimensions and benefit callouts (short labels that point out a benefit) on the images.
  • Apparel product set example (clothing):
    • An indoor morning-light living room scene.
    • An early-morning urban coffee room scene.
    • A mirror selfie.
    • A zoomed hero image.
    • A close-up of the fleece layer.
    • Each scene should show the product in a different everyday setting.
  • Use high-quality product imagery and close-ups showing fabric, weight and detail.
  • For a product that solves a problem, show it in use in the main product images.
    • Also make a short how-to-use video, such as a person using it while sitting or lying down.
    • Where the product suits presents, also offer it as a gift option.

Lifestyle images and the emotional brief

3 min
  • Product photos should create emotion and desire. Show the life the buyer wants, not only the item. Plan each image around what the target consumer aspires to (hopes to be or have).
  • Lifestyle photos can sell an aspirational version (an ideal, dreamed-of version) of a product's use. This works even when most buyers do not actually do that activity. Choose scenes that match the dream of the target consumer.
  • Show the product in use, such as a parent using a baby product on a picnic or a traveller using a bag. This demonstrates outdoor, travel and everyday uses.
  • Models showing the product in use, including opening the package and comfort, give buyers a clear picture.
    • Use real people, not only the product on its own.
  • Product photos can include recognisable local scenery, such as mountains behind the model, and local products on the table.
    • These cues signal that the brand is local, not shipped from abroad.
    • This helps build trust.
  • Use original photos and models instead of supplier stock or AliExpress images (a large online marketplace).
    • Original content can be reused in Facebook ads.
    • It makes the product look more trustworthy.
  • Benefit-led product images (one image per benefit): each image shows the product solving a specific problem, with a short callout.
    • Example: a camera bag photographed with a laptop inside, labelled "fits 15-inch laptops".
    • Example: a kitchen knife set photographed on a cutting board, labelled "razor-sharp blades for daily use".
  • Product images and GIFs (short looping animations) that show exactly how the product is used are strong selling assets. They can convert buyers (make visitors buy) even when the store is simple.
  • The product image should show the product in its intended setting, and look like it does when set up.
    • A plain product shot grabs less attention than a product in use.
    • Use the strongest image available.
  • Photo styles can be set by describing the camera look: a film photo, an iPhone-style snapshot, or a studio shot by a professional photographer.
    • Asking a chat assistant (an AI chatbot) which style the target consumer prefers is a valid way to choose.
  • A candid hard flash style (a casual, direct, strong camera-flash look) fits a trendy, authentic look.
    • It resonates with consumers from short video platforms such as TikTok.
    • It pushes existing iPhone-style photos towards a stronger, more distinctive look.
  • Model clothing can be changed to brand colours, such as dark purple or a pink sweater. This makes the photos reinforce the brand palette (the brand's set of colours).
    • Adjust clothing in the prompt (the written instruction to the AI), instead of accepting the default colours.
  • Apparel case (a real clothing brand example):
    • Starting point: an apparel brand's website used eight product images. These were five on the product page, one homepage hero, one fleece interior detail, and one everyday-wear shot. The existing photos were already candid phone-style shots. The goal was to replace all eight with a stronger brand look.
    • Actions:
      • A chat assistant generated eight scene prompts.
      • The first image run showed the same woman in every photo, so the prompts were edited to describe a different woman each time.
      • The hard flash style prompt was added, and product photos were attached as reference (example pictures given to the AI).
      • A night scene was changed to daylight, and clothing was set to brand colours such as dark purple and pink.
      • All eight images were generated and placed on the site.
    • Result: the before and after comparison was judged a large improvement. The photos looked good on mobile. No conversion figures were reported.
  • Discount sticker on product photos:
    1. Take the product photos from the supplier or AliExpress listing.
    2. Open Canva and search for a Black Friday sticker template (a ready-made design).
    3. Upload the sticker, remove its extra layers if needed, and place it on the product photo.
    4. Resize the sticker and fit the text, for example "60% off" or "50% off".
    5. Put the sticker on all product images, for example in the top-right corner, to improve click-through rate (the share of people who click).
  • For home decor and furniture ads on Pinterest, the visual quality of the product images is the main driver of performance (the main cause of results).
    • Invest in strong product imagery before testing campaigns.

Social and Pinterest imagery

1 min
  • Pin images (pictures posted on Pinterest) should show the product in use, and the result or feeling it creates.
    • Do not rely only on a product shot on a white background.
    • Lifestyle scenes such as a summer picnic engage the Pinterest audience more than plain product photos.
  • Dropshippers (sellers who ship products from a supplier, without holding stock) with only supplier photos should create their own images.
    • Show people using the product and getting the desired benefit.
    • Story-based images are the main way to make dropshipped products stand out on Pinterest.
  • Raw user-style images (plain, unpolished photos that look like customers took them) found through Instagram hashtag research (finding popular tags) produced a conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) of about 8% to 9%. This was in one test with a weighted hula hoop.
    • Real-looking UGC-style photos (UGC means user-generated content, photos by real customers) can outperform polished shots.
  • The same Instagram hashtag research produced a conversion rate of 8-9% for one product.
