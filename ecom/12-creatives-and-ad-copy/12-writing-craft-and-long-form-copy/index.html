Writing craft and long-form copy

Section 12.12 · 12 of 26 in this chapter · 59 min read

In short

  • Write for one reader. Use short, plain words, the way you would talk to a friend.
  • Lead with what the reader gains. Do not start with your factory, team or size.
  • Keep every important claim in each ad. A reader may see only one ad, so do not split the proof across several.
  • Check every text before you publish. Use a readability score (a 1-to-100 number that shows how easy text is to read).
  • Cut any line that does not sell. Clever lines, slogans and filler pull attention away from the offer.
More key points
  • Have every article edited, even if you wrote it yourself. A native English editor (someone who speaks English as a first language) checks the quality.
  • Proofread every ad and script before it goes live. Spelling mistakes make your brand look careless.
  • Write a little every day. Skill comes from practice and from spotting patterns.
  • Start with the reader's situation. The first lines decide whether anyone keeps reading.
  • Keep one big idea. Cut anything that does not support it.
  • Every piece of writing should ask the reader for one action.
  • An ad's job is to earn the click. The sale happens later on the website.
  • Write like you are talking to one person, in short, simple sentences.
  • Show the benefit on the first page, or the reader leaves.
  • Test single-word swaps. One word can change response by about 20 percent.
  • Answer every doubt a reader might have, or they will stop reading at that doubt.
  • Always write a P.S. at the end. Many readers look there first.
  • The envelope must earn the open. Most people decide in seconds.
  • Admit your offer's real weak points and say who it is not for. Readers trust honest sellers more.
  • Whatever the envelope promises, deliver it inside.

Voice and stance

4 min

  • Write ad copy like you are telling a friend about a great product you just bought.

  • Do not sound pushy.

  • Let the customer decide. Do not try to win them over.

  • Blog copy should sound like a friend giving a recommendation.

  • Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs.

  • Leave plenty of white space (empty space around the text that helps the eye rest).

  • Images help readers stay engaged.

  • Use plain, direct language, the way a good salesperson talks face to face.

  • Avoid literary style, slogans and attention tricks. Polished writing draws attention away from the offer and makes readers resist.

  • Before you keep a line, ask: would this help a salesperson sell the product face to face?

  • If not, cut it. Clever wordplay, slogans and show-style copy pull attention away from the offer.

  • Inform and persuade. Do not entertain.

  • Ads target people making a serious spending decision. Readers come for information, not fun.

  • Avoid a transactional tone (a tone that only talks about the sale).

  • Lead with what the customer gains. Show how the brand adds value. Buyers reject brands that feel only transactional.

  • Humour and blunt tone suit products with a casual, direct brand voice.

  • They can backfire on premium products. Match the tone to the price and the audience.

  • For a serious product, an informational and scientific tone works better than entertainment.

  • Celebrity or funny articles brought more traffic (visitors to the page). They brought far fewer relevant buyers than direct informational articles.

  • Write for new prospects only. A prospect is someone who may buy but has not yet.

  • Existing customers have already decided. They seldom read ads. Spend space on them only if you can do it in the headline.

  • Include every important claim in each ad. A reader sees an ad once and may need a different argument than the one in the headline.

  • Do not spread claims across a series of ads and hope readers connect them.

  • Long, complete copy sells more than short copy for products that need thought.

  • The reader decides in one reading, so they need every important claim. A full story is more persuasive than one slogan or short claim.

  • Clever, cute or award-seeking ads that do not persuade the buyer waste budget.

  • Before approving an ad, ask: does it move a specific reader to a specific action?

  • Clarity comes first. If the reader does not understand the message, no proof or style will persuade them.

  • Messy or misread ads can also harm the business over the long term.

  • Build connection through a personal story related to the reader's situation.

  • Use it when you start in an industry, to give context. Show relatable parts of yourself or the brand. This is the base of the Know, Like, Trust phase (when the buyer comes to know, like and trust you).

  • Make stories about the listener, not the seller. Every listener relates a story to their own life.

  • Give them a situation they recognise.

  • Tell the stories you least want to share. These are emotional, vulnerable or embarrassing ones.

  • Readers identify strongly with shared experiences. These stories tend to get the highest response.

  • Procedure for finding the personal stories that sell your product:

    1. Write a list of ten personal moments that were embarrassing, painful or hard to talk about.
    2. Mark the ones you would least want to share publicly.
    3. For each marked story, note the feeling a buyer would have in a similar situation.
    4. Choose the story whose feeling matches the feeling your product addresses.
    5. Write it in the first person, focusing on what happened to you and what you learned.
    6. End by linking the lesson from the story to one step the reader can take.

  • Show the rough edges of your product. Perfection can make a message feel unbelievable.

  • Name honest weaknesses and frame them as acceptable trade-offs (you give something up to get something). This builds trust.

  • Educate before you sell when the audience does not know the problem yet.

  • Explain the problem and its consequences before you offer the product.

  • Trust signals make the claim believable. Examples: before-and-after photos, money-back promises, and real customer proof.

  • Brand guideline document for writers, in five sections:

    1. Resource: what the business sells, why it sells it, who the company is, and why the reader should care.
    2. Guidelines: style rules such as short words, short sentences and short paragraphs, written casually as if speaking face to face.
    3. Key factors: required items per article, such as a minimum word count, three links to external sites, and using the keyword (the search word the page is built around) a set number of times, plus SEO basics (search engine optimisation, getting found on Google) that editors check.
    4. Ground rules: topics writers must avoid, for example health claims that a product cures a disease, which readers and regulators (government bodies that make the rules) will not accept.
    5. Examples of articles that follow the guidelines.

Voice guide

2 min

A voice guide is a short rulebook for how your ads sound. Use this prompt to write it, exactly as written:

Given high-performing ads and a diagnosed awareness stage, extract the consistent tone and voice across them so every future ad maintains the same character. Write a guide covering the single core reason this voice works for that specific audience stage, the sentence mechanics that create the voice (length, rhythm, contractions, repetition, subordination), recurring word choices and registers that work or break the spell, whether the stance is peer-level or authoritative, how the winning ads open (and what opening patterns dominate), and a one-line test any sentence can pass or fail.
  • Take every rule from your own winning ads. Do not use generic copywriting advice.
  • Quote short parts of the ads as proof for each rule.

The voice guide (the rulebook) has six required sections, in this order:

# Section Required content
0 PRIORITY RULE The single core insight about why this voice works for the identified awareness stage (how much the buyer already knows), in 3-4 sentences. Everything else defers to it.
1 SENTENCE-LEVEL RULES Mechanics of sentence length, subordination (a small clause that hangs on a main sentence), declarative tone (plain statements), contractions (short forms like "don't"), repetition and rhythm, with banned-to-rewritten example pairs pulled from real ads.
2 VOCABULARY Recurring word registers (styles of language), and words that would break the spell.
3 NARRATIVE STANCE Whether the voice is authoritative (the expert speaks down to you) and outward, or peer-level (an equal speaks to you) and confessional (sharing a private story). State it as a fixed rule with its failure mode (inverting it).
4 HOOK RULES Recurring opening moves (the first line that grabs attention) and entry-point variety. Ban any opener formula that repeats across ads.
5 THE TEST A one-line gut check applied to every sentence, e.g. "Would a slick copywriter write this? If yes, rewrite it."

Specificity and proof

2 min
  • Lead with the reader's outcome. Not the company's factory, staff, size or equipment.
  • Most readers filter every message by what it does for them. Describing the business rarely moves anyone to buy.
  • Use mental-movie copy: swap vague words for specific, visual ones so the reader can see the product in use.
  • Build each sentence in steps: a vague action, then a clearer action, then a specific place and object, then a sensory (sense-based) or step-by-step detail.
  • Example of the ladder: "Set the water heater to 60 degrees, step into the shower, and feel the first warm rush on your neck." Write new lines the same way for your product.
  • Describe the result, not just the features. Use positive visual adjectives.
  • Every word that does not create an image lowers the chance the reader forms a picture of the benefit.
  • Use colourful language, metaphors (one thing called another), similes (one thing like another) and poignant (moving) stories to break out of generic marketing.
  • Balance gusto with substance. Avoid flowery language for its own sake, so the marketing does not feel like marketing.
  • Concrete examples persuade more than statistics. Examples trigger self-demonstration, where the reader imagines using the product.
  • Lead with stories and examples. Add statistics as support, in the amount your product needs.
  • Recommended balance by product type:
Product type Recommended balance
Beer Mostly emotional imagery of people and good times, minimal statistics
Cars Lead with examples, add statistics on performance, safety or efficiency for the buyers who care
Laser printers Mostly statistics: paper capacity, resolution, ink life, monthly duty cycle (how much work it can handle per month)
Landscaping services Examples about beauty, neighbour perception and convenience
Gym memberships Photos of attractive members, before-and-after results and testimonials (customer quotes); statistics only as a secondary detail
  • A rhetorical question is a statement dressed as a question. It makes the reader think about the claim.
  • Research on persuasion is mixed. Still, rhetorical questions reliably help memory when used to stress a point, not to argue.
  • Use "imagine", "what if" and "think about". These words stop the scan and pull the reader into the outcome.
  • Examples: "Imagine finishing your run and still feeling like you could go another mile." and "What if your kitchen clean-up took five minutes instead of forty?"
  • Use specific words. Proper names, concrete nouns (things you can touch) and precise verbs beat vague terms like "someone" or "something".
  • Specific words cut the work the reader must do to understand you.
  • Limit adjectives (describing words), especially when describing a product.
  • Write the mechanism section (how the product works) in a conversational tone.
  • Use "you" and "your" often. Write as if talking to one person. This works better than formal product text.
  • Short sentences and short paragraphs help here too.
  • The same copy rules work for every format. Ads, emails and web pages follow the same structure and best practices.
  • Copy strategy target. A strong target combines clarity, implementability (can be carried out), grounding in reality and a set timeframe. Model statement:
Using this [email/campaign/sales page/webinar/ad], I will persuade my audience that [named feature] works better than their existing solution for [named goal]. My proof: tangible data, documented examples, and resonance with xyz emotional drivers.

Readability

2 min

Note: the Source B advice below is from 2021-2022 and counts as older advice. Where the 2021-2022 advice and the Source D or Source C material differ, both values are shown, with the context where each applies.

  • Readability is measured with the Flesch Reading Ease score. It runs from 1 to 100. Higher means easier.
  • Microsoft Word shows this score in its readability statistics.
  • Check every ad, sales letter (a long sales text), email and landing page (the page a visitor arrives on) before you publish.
  • Reading Ease score to school grade level:
Flesch score School grade level
0-30 College graduate
30-50 College
50-60 10th to 12th grade
60-70 8th and 9th grade
70-80 7th grade
80-90 6th grade
90-100 5th grade
  • Readability targets for ad copy (Flesch Reading Ease framework):
  • Average sentence length of about 11 words.
  • Mention people about 14 times per 100 words. People means words such as he, she or a name.
  • 70 to 80 percent of words should have one syllable (one beat, like "cat").
  • Flesch-Kincaid (a score that gives a school grade) target, from 2021-2022, older advice: a score of 6 or 7, which means roughly sixth or seventh grade.
  • Read against the table above, a grade of 6-7 sits at a Flesch Reading Ease of about 70-90.
  • Sample scores: "John loves Mary" scores 92. "John has a profound affection for Mary" scores 67. A long, wordy version with many hard words scores 32.
  • Each added complexity lowers the score. When a score is too low, simplify words and sentences.
  • Readability checkers: the Flesch-Kincaid Readability Score, the Hemingway App (a tool that flags hard text), Grammarly (a writing checker) and Readability.
  • The Flesch-Kincaid score is built into Microsoft Word. Hemingway or Grammarly serve the same purpose on a Mac.
  • Grammarly also finds spelling and grammar errors, and sentences that could be shortened. Its premium (paid) version gives more features.
  • Other readability formulas exist. They are for special cases:
  • The Fog Index (a formula that estimates the years of schooling a reader needs).
  • The SMOG formula from 1969 (estimates grade level from long words).
  • The Forcast formula (built for technical forms and manuals).
  • A single score such as Flesch is enough for most ad copy. Use the others only when a specific audience or document type needs them.
  • Ads that are read only rarely need to be simpler. Most readers skim and do not work hard to decode a message.
  • Write for quick scanning.
  • Write for an average reader with average or lower reading comprehension, not for the most educated person. Most visitors do not have a high command of the language.

Simplicity and plain language

2 min

  • Explain it so a six-year-old can understand. If you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it well enough.

  • Revise first drafts to simplify and clarify the message. Simple is good. Simplistic (talking down to people) is bad.

  • You may break the rule to prove a point, but use this with caution.

  • Do not oversimplify until the copy sounds condescending. Explain in everyday language, and respect the reader's intelligence.

  • Lead by translation: turn complex ideas into everyday language. Clarity matters most.

  • Grammar matters, but not that much. Marketing copy gets the point across. Core function often beats form.

  • Casual is fine if the audience is casual. Simplicity matters more than perfect grammar. Formal words put distance between you and the reader.

  • Use short, simple words instead of formal substitutes.

  • Plain words like "tired" or "hungry" beat inflated synonyms. Pompous phrasing reads as complicated and slows persuasion.

  • Use short sentences. Long sentences are harder to make sense of. Short ones are easier to read and get read more often.

  • Express one thought in each sentence. Let the next sentence carry the next thought.

  • Aim for about 11 words per sentence. Vary the length so the copy sounds natural.

  • Write the way you would speak to one person sitting next to you. Use plain, direct language. Avoid stiff, formal phrasing.

  • Use your target customer's vocabulary. Technical or formal language fits only when the audience is an expert group, such as scientists or engineers.

  • For general consumers, avoid supplier and manufacturer jargon (industry terms). Explain terms in simple words.

  • Use plain, to-the-point wording. Skip flashy or flowery language. Short sentences that get to the point keep the text readable.

  • Remove flowery language and fluff. Fluff means any words or sentences that add no value.

  • Check each sentence while editing. Ask: does it make sense, and is it needed? Brain-dump drafts (rough first drafts written fast) are fine, but they must be edited.

  • Format and simplify a product description in this order:

    1. Increase the font size to at least 18pt (18 points, a unit of letter size), keeping the same font.
    2. Break long paragraphs into shorter ones of about two lines each.
    3. Shorten overlong sentences and rework the structure where needed, so sentences are short.
    4. Remove fluff, flowery language and unneeded adjectives.
    5. Replace jargon with plain words.
    6. Test the result with a readability tool such as Flesch-Kincaid, Hemingway or Grammarly, and aim for a score of 6 or 7.

  • Example: a dense product description was set at about 12pt, had very long paragraphs and four-line sentences. Only two changes were made: the font went to 18pt, and paragraphs were split into blocks of about two lines. No words changed. The text became noticeably easier to read. This shows that formatting alone changes readability. Sentences of four lines still need rewriting.

  • After setting font size and paragraph length, break long product copy further with bullet points and short headings, so the page is easier to scan.

  • Keep every fact and claim the same while you do this.

  • Sentences that still run across several lines need a separate rewrite into shorter sentences.

Length and layout

2 min
  • Use a minimum font size of 18pt for web copy: product pages, long sales pages and content pages.
  • Never go below 18pt. 20pt can be used on text-only sales pages. Most traffic now comes from mobile devices (phones and tablets), so larger text is needed.
  • Use one font family (one letter style) throughout a piece of copy. Mixing several fonts in a small section makes the text hard to read.
  • Keep paragraphs to two lines or fewer most of the time. Occasional longer paragraphs are fine.
  • Short paragraphs create white space. White space helps the eye separate parts of the page and scan with less strain. This makes the reader more likely to keep reading.
  • Dense blocks of text, as found in printed books, are harder to read on screens.
  • Limit regular paragraphs in direct-response text (copy that asks for action right away) to about four or five short sentences, so the page reads quickly.
  • Copy length should match what is needed to communicate the message. It should not follow a fixed word count.
  • Long copy works when it has quality. Short copy works for simple products that need only a few strong points. Length is about quality, not quantity. This applies to conversion copy (copy that gets a visitor to buy or act), not SEO copy.
  • Length rules by context. The sources differ, so apply each value where it fits:
Context Value Source
Paragraphs, general Two lines or fewer most of the time
Paragraphs, ad copy One to two lines maximum
Paragraphs, direct response Four or five short sentences maximum
Thought-command section One or two paragraphs, with short sentences and short lines
Story section Five or six paragraphs if engaging
Considered purchase Long, complete copy
Simple product Short copy with a few strong points
  • Ad length depends on three things:
  • How much copy builds desire to its full strength.
  • How much copy makes the buyer feel comfortable and flattered, and lets them see the product fit into their life.
  • How much copy overcomes existing beliefs and makes the claims believable.
  • Format ad copy in short, simple sentences. Use paragraphs of one to two lines maximum.
  • Make the call to action (CTA, the line that asks for the next step) stand out and stay visible. Use only one CTA per ad.

Banned patterns and checklist

2 min

Checklist prompt. Apply it before you finish copy. Use the prompt exactly as written:

Compile a checklist of forbidden patterns and language that a writer applies before finishing copy. This includes structural problems (stacking false contrasts, using generic opening formulas, starting with rhetorical devices), the parallel-repetition trap where consecutive lines repeat the same structure to fake emphasis instead of escalating, dead transition phrases, hedging language that weakens conviction, and formatting crutches like excessive emoji or punctuation.

The banned list has five required sections, in this order:

  1. Banned structural patterns. These include false-contrast stacks (lines like "It's not X. It's not Y. It's Z."), rhetorical section-openers (a question or device that opens a section), and generic "Imagine if" openers. Each comes with a bad example and a fix.
  2. The parallel repetition stack (highest priority). This means repeating the same opener or skeleton (the same sentence shape) across lines to fake emphasis. Define it with 5-6 banned examples and explain why it fails. It is mechanical and signals copy written by a machine (AI). Replace it with the Escalation Principle.
    • The Escalation Principle: in ad copy, every line must add new information or raise the stakes.
    • No line may echo or repeat the structure of the line before it.
    • Repeating the same opener across lines creates mechanical, AI-like copy. It signals fake emphasis, not real momentum.
  3. Banned transition and filler phrases. Dead connectives (empty linking phrases) such as "Let's dive in," "Here's the thing," "At the end of the day," and "But wait, there's more."
  4. Banned qualifiers and hedging (soft words that weaken a claim). Words such as "might," "could potentially," "often" and "usually" are replaced with absolute statements plus proof.
  5. Banned formatting crutches. These are emoji overload, exclamation overuse and dense paragraph blocks. Each has its own rule.
  • Repetition for emphasis (Source C view). Anaphora means repeating a word at the start of lines. Epistrophe means repeating it at the end. Both drive home points.
  • Examples: "I have a dream…I have a dream…" (MLK) and "We shall fight…we shall fight…" (Churchill).
  • Use them to break copy into digestible bits, make related information rhythmic, and repeat core arguments.
  • Repetition, reconciled. The Source D rule and the Source C anaphora advice seem to conflict.
  • Apply the Escalation test to every repeated line. If the repetition adds new content or raises the stakes, it is anaphora and allowed.
  • If it only copies the skeleton of the line before it, it is the banned parallel stack.
  • Rhetorical questions, reconciled. A rhetorical question used to stress a point helps memory.
  • Do not open a section or a piece of copy with a rhetorical device. Do not use a rhetorical section-opener.
  • Avoid FAQ sections (question-and-answer blocks) in sales letters. They break the story flow, repeat what was already said, and remove the seller's control of the conversation.
  • Avoid "Imagine if" openers and generic opening formulas.

Editing and proofreading

2 min
  • Have every article edited, even if you wrote it yourself.
  • Hire native English editors. "Native" means English is their first language.
  • They cost about $20 to $25 per hour.
  • Let the editor help you pick and vet writers. "Vet" means check their quality.
  • The editor uses their judgement of good writing.
  • Then write the headline (the big title) and the meta description (a short summary shown in search results).
  • Create a custom image, made just for this article.
  • Upload the article with its image and alt tag (a short text that describes the image for screen readers and search engines).
  • Proofread scripts (the planned words for an ad) and angle sheets (sheets that list each selling angle, meaning each way of framing the sales message) before publishing.
  • Proofreading means checking for spelling and grammar mistakes.
  • Example: a template contained the spelling errors "Emarrassed", "prperly", "malting" and "healty".
  • Errors in ad copy (the written words of an ad) make the brand look careless.
  • Errors reduce trust before the viewer reaches the offer.
  • Reread the finished copy a few days after writing it.
  • At that point the wording looks fresh.
  • Check the balance of reinforcement (repeating a point to make it stick).
  • A new angle can slide into repetition. That is easier to spot at a distance.
  • Cut any passage that brings no new angle.
  • Read every sentence during editing.
  • Ask two questions for each one: does it make sense, and is it needed?
  • Hire a copywriter (someone who writes words meant to sell) if your own copywriting skills are weak.
  • Practise regularly to improve conversion rates (the share of visitors who buy or take the action you want).
  • This matters for ads and landing pages (the page a visitor reaches after clicking an ad).
  • Copywriting is the most important part of Facebook ads.
  • Reason: the image stops people from scrolling.
  • Print your drafts (ad variations, email sections, product angles) at a small size.
  • Spread them on a table or floor.
  • Group them into piles by theme.
  • Lay the pieces out in the order you want, and check the flow (how each piece leads to the next).
  • Type the final order into your digital file.
  • Return to the printed pieces whenever the sequence stops working.

Drafting process

2 min

  • When learning copywriting, aim to be better than you were before.

  • Do not aim to become the best copywriter in the world.

  • Each new piece of copy should be a little stronger than the last.

  • Writing copy feels hard at first. That is normal for beginners.

  • Keep writing, even when early results are weak.

  • The difficulty decreases with repeated practice.

  • Practice procedure:

    1. Write copy for your products on a regular, ongoing basis.
    2. Test different approaches (different angles, styles or messages) across different times and different areas you write copy for.
    3. Keep applying what you learn consistently in your business, and keep improving over time.

  • Build copywriting skill with a daily routine.

  • Read one piece of sales copy actively, with a pen.

  • Write one page of copy. It may be thrown away, or it may be a hand-copied page from a strong writer.

  • Generate one idea per day by asking "what if".

  • Repetition and pattern recognition (spotting the same structures again and again) are the main drivers of skill.

  • Active reading means reading with a purpose.

  • Look for what the writer is trying to communicate beneath the text.

  • Mark patterns.

  • Note new lines that can inspire original copy.

  • Passive reading only absorbs the words.

  • Direct-response copy (writing that gets an immediate action) is a demanding school for copywriters.

  • A single ad must make the whole sale.

  • It gets no help from displays or salespeople.

  • Techniques that work under these conditions carry over well to general advertising.

  • From the second sentence onward, each sentence has one job.

  • That job is to force the reader to read the next sentence.

  • The ad is a chain of sentences.

  • The chain moves the reader from headline to product to purchase.

  • Direct-response copy must provoke action.

  • First move the prospect (a possible buyer) emotionally.

  • Only then persuade them intellectually, with logic and facts.

  • Do the emotional work in the opening.

  • The reader gives an ad only seconds before deciding whether to keep reading.

  • A strong idea is big enough to stir interest.

  • It is easy to understand.

  • It is immediately convincing.

  • It is clearly useful to the reader.

  • Believability matters as much as size.

  • Build the copy as a sequence of checks on the reader's questions.

  • See the Ten-Question Sequence under Sales letters.

  • Same approach everywhere: the principle of consistency across channels (channels are the places you reach buyers, such as ads, emails and websites).

  • The same structure and best practices apply to ads, emails and websites.

Slippery-slide flow

4 min
  • Slippery-slide flow means copy that pulls the reader down, line by line.
  • Each sentence has one job: make the reader read the next.
  • The first line exists only to make the reader go on to the second line.
  • Test it by asking whether it makes leaving the page almost impossible.
  • Do not open with your name, credentials (qualifications or proof of expertise) or company history.
  • The exception: the piece is a parody (a joke copy of something), or the audience already knows you.
  • Start with the reader's situation.
  • Or start with a question that opens a loop (a question the reader wants answered).
  • Strong first-line pattern, in three steps:
    • Name a specific person or situation.
    • State a concrete change or loss.
    • Leave the outcome open so the reader must continue.
  • Examples: "Maya found out her dog had been eating the wrong food for two years on the day the vet called." and "Nobody on the boat knew the forecast had changed until the wind hit the sails."
  • Opening with "my name is" or "I've been an expert for years" wastes the most valuable seconds of attention.
  • Replace it with a line about what matters to the reader.
  • Case: a long-running sales letter (one that ran for years) opened with a credentials paragraph.
  • That paragraph said the author was a certified specialist with 15 years of experience.
  • The seller removed that opening line.
  • Conversion rose by 22%.
  • The team then tested a new opener (opening line) built around a question about a hidden problem.
  • That lifted conversion by another 20%.
  • The lesson: the opening should focus on the reader's world, not on the author.
  • Desire-line pattern (a line that names what the reader wants): use "I'm going to" plus the feeling or outcome for the reader.
  • Never use "I want you to" about the seller.
  • Examples: "I'm going to make your Saturday mornings feel unhurried again." and "I'm going to have your kitchen smelling like fresh bread by the time your guests arrive."
  • Every line must add new information or raise the stakes.
  • Stakes means what the reader stands to gain or lose.
  • Never echo the structure of the previous line.
  • In longer copy, the lead-in (the short opening section) creates curiosity.
  • It prepares the reader for the body.
  • The body sells by showing benefits and building desire.
  • It keeps going until the reader wants to click through to the landing page.
  • The call to action (the request to act) comes last.
  • It can be an offer or a "learn more" click.
  • The right choice depends on how aware the audience already is.
  • For the interest stage (the part that makes people care), open with a problem the ideal customer can relate to.
  • Then agitate it (make the problem feel worse).
  • Explain why it happens.
  • The stronger the agitation, the more the reader feels the need for a solution.
  • Create a character who resembles the ideal customer.
  • Tell their problem story, with photos to make it engaging.
  • Readers who see themselves in the story become warm (friendly and trusting).
  • They do this without realising they are being sold to.
  • In a long-form editorial (a long, article-style text), end every section with a hook (a line that pulls the reader on).
  • The hook pulls the reader into the next part.
  • Readers who stop mid-story never reach the product pitch.
  • So the hook matters as much as the headline.
  • Short-paragraph trick: ask a question or make a quick statement.
  • Then answer it in a short follow-up line.
  • Example form: a short question, a one-line answer such as "I thought so.", then a short lead-in such as "Now let me explain…"
  • Open-loop (a promise that rewards the reader for reading on) is a pledge to reveal information later.
  • Examples: telling the reader that within a moment you will demonstrate precisely how the statements just made hold up completely.
  • Or that if they stay with you, you will show how to obtain the advantages you already promised.
  • Launch a fresh loop before the earlier one is resolved, so the reader keeps going.
  • Pattern interrupt (something that breaks the usual pattern): open by showing something the reader has not seen in other ads.
  • Examples: an unusual image, document or opening line.
  • Its job is to grab attention.
  • Check what competitors (rival sellers) use as pattern interrupts.
  • Avoid repeating them.
  • Payoff lines (short lines that follow a headline) give the reader slightly more detail on the headline's topic.
  • They do not reveal the key information.
  • A payoff holds attention while the copy moves toward the big promise.
  • Reach the big promise as soon as possible.
  • The lead (the opening part of the copy) should contain one big idea.
  • That idea can be summarised in a single sentence.
  • This is the copy's "soul" or "golden thread" (the one idea that ties everything together).
  • Everything in the headline, lead and body must tie back to that idea.
  • Remove content that deviates from it.
  • The soul can be vague in the first draft. It gets sharper during writing.
  • Express the reader's situation in plain steps.
  • Keep one thought per sentence.
  • Each sentence moves the reader forward.

Opening lines and lead length

2 min
  • Lead length. Sources differ. Use the one that fits the format:
    • The opening few hundred words decide whether the rest is read.
    • In a long-form sales letter (a long written sales message), that is roughly 100 to 600 words.
    • A weak lead loses the prospect, no matter how strong the later sections are.
    • The lead is the densest part of the copy, meaning the most packed with key points.
    • It typically covers roughly the first 500 words.
    • In long-form letters, that ranges from 2 to 7 pages.
    • If the proof (evidence) for the lead's claims has not started by page 7, the lead is likely too long.
    • Shorten it.
  • In long-form sales copy, roughly 80% of the emotional impact comes from the first 20% of the text.
  • So the opening deserves the most drafting and testing effort.
  • Revising the rest rarely fixes a weak opening.
  • Opening test procedure:
    1. Read only the opening of the sales copy, without the rest.
    2. Ask whether it excites the reader and creates anticipation to keep reading.
    3. If it does not, rewrite the opening before editing anything else. A weak opening usually means the whole promotion will underperform.
  • Two identical sales letters can differ only in their opening lead.
  • Such letters can produce response rates (the share of readers who reply or buy) that differ by a factor of two or three.
  • Example: 0.5% against double or triple that.
  • Test whole leads rather than small details, to find the largest gains.
  • Dominant-emotion leads: once a control winner (the version that already won, used as the baseline) is found for a rollout (full launch), write up to six cover tests.
  • Each cover test has a different lead type. Lead types include benefit-led and dominant-emotion leads.
  • Run heads-up A/B splits (two versions tested head to head) between the strongest results.
  • In recent heads-up splits of this kind, dominant-emotion (indirect) leads won more often than benefit leads.
  • Do not list every feature and benefit in one piece of copy, hoping one lands. (A feature is what the product has. A benefit is what it does for the buyer.)
  • That dilutes the message and splits the reader's attention.
  • Keep one main benefit and one core emotion per lead.
  • Cut the rest.

Sales letters

6 min

Structure

  • Use four sections to structure a long-form direct-response sales letter, in this order: (1) headline, (2) lead, (3) body copy, (4) offer.

  • Keep the order.

  • Rearranging the sections makes the copy harder to follow.

  • It loses the attention of busy, skeptical readers.

  • Write the lead in this order:

    1. Capture attention, via a pattern interrupt or the headline.
    2. Expand on the headline with a payoff that gives more detail without giving away the offer.
    3. Establish credibility with a single line, such as a quote from an authority or a track record.
    4. State the one big promise as early as possible.
    5. Open a loop that promises information revealed later in the copy.

  • Credibility (proof you can be trusted) in the lead can be a single line.

  • Examples: a quote from an authority figure, or a credential at the start of the letter. A credential is proof of skill, such as a title or degree.

  • Full credibility is saved for the body.

  • The quote or credential should make the claim seem inevitable or predictable.

  • The lead does not need to reveal the product or offer.

  • In many long-form letters, the product appears only in the final pages of the lead.

  • That happens after the guru (an expert figure) or seller is introduced.

  • The lead's job is to build hype (excitement) around an opportunity, or to illustrate a severe problem.

  • The body then proves the points.

  • Body copy: repeat this sequence for each claim:

    • (1) address an objection (a doubt the reader has),
    • (2) make a claim that answers that objection,
    • (3) give proof,
    • (4) describe the benefit.

  • Keep to this template for body copy. Do not use the emotion-based approach used elsewhere.

  • Find objections (doubts a buyer has) by generating them from the four core emotions and their alter egos (a second version of each emotion).

  • The four core emotions are New/Only, Easy/Anybody, Safe/Predictable and Big/Fast.

  • For each one, ask what a skeptical reader would question.

  • Examples: "Why should I buy from you?", "What if I don't have money?", "How do I know this will work?", "How fast will I see money?".

  • Common objections include "Why should I act now?", "Who are you?", "How does it work?", "Is this risky?", "How much does it cost?" and "What if it doesn't work?"

Body sections (patterns)

  • Guru or credibility section: follows the lead.
  • It marks the move from lead to body.
  • It answers "Who are you?" and "Why should I listen to you?"
  • It does this by showing photos with credible people, appearances on TV or news networks, or a track record of success or accurate predictions.
  • Case studies (detailed success stories) and testimonials (customer praise) are usually saved for the offer.
  • Discovery story: explains how the seller found the opportunity.
  • It answers "How do you know about this?" and conveys Only (the feeling that the offer is exclusive).
  • Use a secret source, an elite network (a small, exclusive group) or a chance event.
  • These show the information is not available to the general public.
  • Support it with proof that the relationship exists.
  • Key insight: the single piece of information the reader must believe for the opportunity to seem real.
  • Back it with "signs" that the event is coming.
  • Example: stocks of related companies quietly rising because insiders (people inside the company) are buying.
  • These signs convey Safe and Predictable.
  • Layman's explanation: describes how the opportunity works in simple words anyone could repeat.
  • Think of two people talking at a bar.
  • It answers "How does it work?"
  • It should convey the New or Easy emotion.
  • Example: three simple steps plus a patented (legally protected) or new technology.
  • Technical explanation: uses dense jargon (special expert words) and scientific detail.
  • It reassures the reader that the seller understands the mechanism (how it works inside).
  • The reader does not need to follow it.
  • It conveys Safe and Predictable through expertise rather than understanding.
  • "Better Than" section: compares the offer to the most popular item currently in the market.
  • Example: FAANG stocks (big tech company shares) or Bitcoin.
  • The comparison shows the offer outperforms it.
  • Back the claim with a quote or figure from a news source.
  • Also use a chart or comparison, and a second proof point (another piece of evidence).
  • The section should convey Big, optionally paired with Safe.
  • "Last Time Something Like This Happened" section: uses a historical case (a past event).
  • Use a subhead such as "The last time something like this happened was over 25 years ago!"
  • Tell the story.
  • Say that this time the opportunity is much bigger.
  • The historical pattern conveys Predictability (the feeling that the outcome can be foreseen).
  • A case study with a famous name can make the pattern strong.
  • Example: Gary Halbert's "Water Ad" example. Gary Halbert was a famous copywriter.

Ten-question sequence

Structure the letter around the reader's questions, in this order:

  1. How is this different from everything else I've seen? State the new opportunity. Make sure it is truly unique. Without uniqueness, the idea is not worth testing.
  2. What's in it for me? Open with a big promise tied to the reader's key desires. The reader must see the benefit within seconds.
  3. How do I know this is real? Add early proof, such as historical charts, testimonials or third-party quotes (quotes from people outside your company). Wrap it in a story.
  4. What's holding me back? Reveal the real reason past solutions failed. Find it by reverse-engineering the answer to step 1 (working backwards from it), so the reader is not blamed.
  5. Who or what is to blame? Name a common enemy that the audience already distrusts or resents. Draw it from their existing beliefs.
  6. Why now? Create urgency (a feeling that you must act soon) with an either-or choice. Add a real deadline or event that raises the stakes.
  7. Why should I trust you? Establish credibility through an "I've been in your shoes" storyline (you faced the same problem), a Robin Hood storyline (you stand up for ordinary people against the powerful) or an expert storyline (you are the expert).
  8. How does it work? Explain the new mechanism logically. Anchor it in a belief the reader already holds. Wrap it in a story with credible sources.
  9. How can I get started? Present a no-brainer offer (an offer so good that saying no seems silly). Show a large gap between stacked value (all the extras added up) and the final price. Add bonuses (free extras) and a guarantee (a promise to refund or fix it if it fails).
  10. What do I have to lose? Close with a push-pull: let the prospect choose freely among options, then remind them what they lose by doing nothing.

Offer and close

  • Use the P.S. (a short extra note at the end) to restate the offer, repeat the contact details and push for action.
  • Readers often read the P.S. before the body copy.
  • So it should contain the strongest closing line.
  • If a P.S. is used after the sign-off (the closing words of a letter, such as "Best regards"), summarise the offer briefly, like an abbreviated lead.
  • Close with one strong, one-time-only offer.
  • Sales-letter build procedure (lift note and opening). A lift note is a short note that makes the reader want more.
    1. Write a lift note that asks a question which inverts the promise (turns it around). Then support it with a single-sentence story that validates it (proves it true).
    2. Open the sales letter with one statement that implies the core promise.
    3. Support the statement with a short bulleted list of facts.
    4. Validate the statement (prove it) with your own result.
    5. Close with one strong, one-time-only offer.

Advertorials and native-style long-form

2 min

  • An advertorial (an article-style ad) is a pre-sell page (a page that sells before the main product page).

  • It looks like editorial content, meaning ordinary articles.

  • It leads to a product page.

  • It often takes the form of a story, a listicle (an article in list form) or a "how I did this" narrative.

  • It works as a psychological bridge. Visitors get drawn into the story before they see the price.

  • So they are less likely to bounce (leave straight away) from a product page.

  • It is time-consuming to produce.

  • So it is most worth testing for problem-solving products.

  • Test with an advertorial right away if the product suits the format.

  • Do this rather than testing only a product page.

  • The goal is to avoid wasting test budget (the money spent on tests) on a weak funnel (the path from first click to sale).

  • The trade-off is more production time.

  • Teams that prefer fast testing may skip this step.

  • Advertorial research and writing method:

    1. Find advertorials that already sell in your niche (a small, specific market) or an adjacent niche (a closely related market), using search on YouTube and similar platforms.
    2. Copy the full advertorial text and paste it into a Google Sheet (an online spreadsheet).
    3. Go through it paragraph by paragraph. Note what each paragraph says and what job it does in the sequence.
    4. Rewrite the text for your own product, adapting the same paragraph roles, and then test it.

  • Editorial page steps, with a 70/30 split: about 70% information and story, and the final 30% for the product pitch.

    1. Plan the page around the 70/30 rule.
    2. Write the headline first, making it benefit-driven and attention-grabbing with a bold claim, curiosity or news angle.
    3. Interest: open with a problem the ideal customer can relate to, then agitate it and explain why it happens.
    4. Desire: explain why other solutions fail, present your product as a new approach, and add social proof (evidence that other people trust it) with customer reviews, photos, results over time and data.
    5. Action: introduce the product and place a clear call to action that leads to the product page.
    6. Add photos throughout the text to keep the reader engaged.

  • Native-style long-form ad pattern: a large primary text that reads like an article. (Native-style means it looks like normal content on the platform.)

  • Structure, in order:

    1. a bold headline
    2. a first-person story of a small symptom that grew slowly
    3. an ordinary check-up that found nothing alarming
    4. a turning point where the person tried the product
    5. then the product as the answer
    6. and a disclaimer (a required note of limits) at the end

  • It works because the story earns attention at the top of the funnel (the first step, where people first meet you).

  • The disclaimer keeps it compliant (following the rules).

  • Two new examples: "I Kept Losing Track of My Running Times for Two Years. A Coach Found the Cause in One Session." and "My Desk Routine Had Stopped Working by Spring. A Physiotherapist Showed Me What I Was Missing."

Direct mail and envelope techniques

1 min
  • Mail-order ads (ads sent by post or response form) must make the full sale in a single run.
  • So they pack the greatest number of intensification devices (tricks that raise pressure to buy) into one long ad.
  • They are often frozen (kept unchanged) and repeated after three to six months.
  • National advertising (ads seen across a whole country) keeps the product in front of the public constantly.
  • So it runs far more often.
  • Its product lifespans (how long a product stays on sale) are much longer.
  • Catalog-style direct mail (printed mail that looks like a shop catalogue).
  • Open the first paragraph of each product ad with several very short sentences.
  • Make each one two to four words.
  • They pull the eye down the page.
  • They keep readers moving toward the offer.
  • Use the same opening rhythm across product ads.
  • That keeps the reader moving through the page.
  • Operator note (a note for the person running the ads) on the rhythm rule: the repeated opening rhythm applies across separate product ads.
  • Inside one ad, every consecutive line must still escalate (rise in strength).
  • It must not echo the line before it.

Core principle: one reader, one action

2 min
  • Direct-response copy (sales writing built to make a reader take one specific action right away; copy means written words that sell) follows one formula: name the reader's problem, show the product as the answer, and end with one clear instruction.
  • It works because every sentence pushes toward that one action, instead of just giving information.
  • Example in the same pattern: Tired of cracked phone screens after one drop? This case absorbs the impact and ships in 48 hours. Tap to see the protection.
  • Each piece of copy must aim at exactly one action, chosen by its place in the funnel (the path a buyer takes from first contact to purchase).
    • On a product page, the action is adding to cart (putting the item in the shopping basket) and buying.
    • In an ad, the action is clicking through to the website.
    • In an email, the action is visiting the website.
  • Adding a second competing action weakens the first.
  • Ads that try to make the viewer buy directly fail when the ad does not allow purchase on the spot.
  • The viewer must first visit the site. The ad should only earn the click, and the sale happens later on the site.
  • Ads do not sell the product. They sell the click.
  • A few lines of text cannot sell a product effectively.
  • So the ad only needs to arouse enough curiosity (spark interest) that the person wants to visit the page.
  • Words in emails, search text ads (short written ads shown in Google search results) and social ads all count as copy.
  • They must be written with one defined action in mind. This includes Google search text ads and Facebook ads.
  • The purpose of ecommerce (selling online) copy is to move the reader to the action the business wants.
  • In most cases, that action is buying the product.
  • Judge every headline and text block by one question: does it push toward that purchase?
  • Today's online copywriting methods largely come from direct-response text marketing, developed from the early 1900s through the 1950s and 1960s.
  • Before TV and the internet, mail and newspaper ads sold almost everything through text alone.
  • That forced writers to become very skilled.

Voice and reader focus

1 min
  • The word "you" matters more than any other word in a sales letter.
  • So focus on what the reader wants, not on the seller.
  • People are interested in their own goals, so speak to them directly.
  • Copy written as one person speaking to one reader, using I, you and me, feels personal.
  • It converts (gets more people to buy) better than copy written as if addressing a crowd.
  • A byline (the name printed under the text) with a real person's name strengthens this feel.
  • Personal first-person invitation (pattern; first-person means written as "I" or "me"): rewrite a crowd-directed corporate line as a note from one person.
  • That person would like to meet the reader again and show the new product.
    • Impersonal form: We invite all customers to visit our booth.
    • Personal form: You may remember me from the last show. I would love to see you at the next one and show you the new machine.
  • The personal version creates warmth and a direct invitation.
  • Honesty shows through the copy.
  • Hidden flaws or evasive claims (vague answers that dodge the question) are noticed by readers.
  • A candid tone builds trust. It also makes the buying environment (the feeling that surrounds the purchase) feel real.
  • Creative and personal writing shows through the copy.
  • Readers sense the personality of the writer.
  • So write in the voice of your company, not in someone else's voice.
  • Logic does not have to be strictly correct to work.
  • It only has to convey the intended feeling clearly.
  • A reassuring phrase about returns can sound odd when read literally, and still be effective.
  • It signals a prompt (quick) and caring company.
  • The goal of all copy is to get the reader to exchange their hard-earned money for the product or service.
  • So every choice in the copy serves that exchange.

Buying environment and layout

1 min
  • The first paragraphs of an ad set the buying environment.
  • Layout (how the page is arranged) and copy tone must match the price and nature of the product.
    • A premium product (high-priced, top quality) needs a refined, confident look and tone.
    • A bargain needs a busy, deal-focused look and tone.
  • In print, the copywriter (the person who writes the words) controls the buying environment completely.
  • They do this through layout and word choice.
  • A store must spend thousands to set this up. Print does not.
  • So use this control on purpose, instead of leaving the mood to chance.

Word choice and emotion

3 min
  • Single words carry emotional associations.
  • So word choice can change the reader's feeling, even when the facts stay the same.
  • Choose the more evocative word (the one that stirs feeling) when it is accurate.
    • Example: cottage instead of motel office for a small building.
  • Do not change the facts to suit a mood.
  • Word-level emotion choice (pattern): when the facts allow it, choose the more evocative word.
    • Example: cottage instead of motel office for a small building.
    • Keep the facts the same.
  • Test single-word swaps on high-traffic claims (main promises that many people see).
  • One swapped word can change response measurably.
  • Test case: in a mail order offer (sales sent by post), replacing repair with fix raised response by about 20 percent in the original test.
  • Test single-word swaps on high-traffic claims.
  • Small wording changes can move response measurably.
  • Readers respond more to vivid, emotional description than to a dry specification list (a plain list of technical details).
  • People enjoy emotion at any time.
  • But they must be pushed to study facts.
  • So put life and occasionally a light touch into the copy. This stirs the reader out of indifference.
  • Replace long lists of points with a few vivid images the reader can see.
  • Long sequences of firstly, secondly and thirdly put readers to sleep.
  • They also lose the attention you have built.
  • Sensory description (pattern; describing through the senses of sight, sound and touch): describe the product through a few concrete sights, sounds or textures the reader already knows.
  • Do this instead of listing specifications.
  • The reader pictures the product in his own life.
  • That makes the benefit feel real and memorable.
    • Example one: The lamp throws a warm, even pool of light across the desk, and the dial turns without a flicker.
    • Example two: The jacket feels heavy and soft in the hand, and it closes with a sound that tells you the zip will hold through a wet walk home.
  • Build a word picture (a mental image built in the reader's head):
    1. Pick a familiar object or scene the reader already pictures.
    2. Add one point of interest at a time, in logical order. Start with the framework (the basic shape), then add the parts that matter.
    3. Stop when the reader can build the complete picture himself.
  • Use sensory language (words that appeal to the senses) and big-impact phrases.
  • They let the reader see and feel the benefit.
  • Example: describe a cold, dark office late at night when selling a fast computer.
  • Build a card file of vivid phrases from advertising, novels and television.
  • When stuck, use reference works of descriptive words, such as thesauruses (books of word alternatives).
  • Sell an emotional story first, then back it up with logic.
  • Buyers decide on emotion and then justify with logic.
  • Cold, purely factual letters rarely work.
  • So the letter needs an enthusiastic voice to compensate for being ink on paper (a printed letter with no sound or pictures).
  • Boredom is the most serious sin in marketing copy.
  • So find something to be excited about, even for dull products.
  • Excitement can come from the feeling of owning the product.
  • It can come from the enjoyment of the money or time it saves.
  • It can also come from exaggerated and passionate language that the reader will still find moderate.
  • Passion exercise (a writing practice): write a letter that tries to persuade someone to do something risky but exciting.
  • Use vivid sensory detail, bold promises, and the removal of each objection (a reason the reader says no) as you go.
  • Then carry that energy into the real offer.
  • Example for a hardware retailer (a shop that sells tools and fixtures): Picture the first morning your shop opens with the new displays up. Your customers walk in and see exactly what you promised them. Here is how to get that morning ready in ten days.

Readability and simplicity

1 min
  • Write sales copy in very simple language.
  • Keep sentences and words basic.
  • Introduce one new idea at a time.
  • Simple wording makes it easier for a reader on a phone to follow the argument.
  • It also helps them reach the offer.
  • Make copy readable with short, punchy sentences.
  • Sentence fragments (incomplete sentences, like "Fast. Simple.") are fine.
  • Keep paragraphs to three or four sentences.
  • Use everyday language and no jargon (words only experts know).
  • Writing that sounds like speech moves the reader along.
  • Grammar rules matter less than momentum (forward movement).
  • Write at a simple reading level.
  • The target audience is often less literate or less patient than the writer assumes.
  • This trap is called the sophistication trap (writing for readers who are more skilled or patient than they are).
  • Assuming sophisticated readers costs money.
  • The simpler approach is safer.
  • Even TV commercials are pitched at about a sixth-grade reading level (about the level of an 11- or 12-year-old).
  • Reduce the perceived effort of your offer.
  • Do this by stating the time it takes in plain terms, such as 15 minutes a day.
  • A small, specific time commitment makes the reader more willing to start.

Opening and first page

1 min
  • Answer the first two questions on the first page: what is new, and what is in it for the reader.
  • Readers often browse on phones and look for a reason to leave.
  • If the prospect (possible buyer) does not see their benefit quickly, they will leave and return to what they were doing.
  • Structure the first paragraph as an extended headline (a long opening that works like a headline).
  • Its job is to sell the reader on reading the letter.
  • Only then sell the offer.
  • Cut any preamble (opening words before the real point).
  • Examples: apologies for not writing a personal letter, or long explanations of why the mail matters.
  • Write the headline so that it makes the reader feel the letter is welcome.

Core belief and planning

2 min
  • To find the core of a promotion (the main idea of a sales push), ask one question: what is the single most important thing the reader must believe?
  • Write the answer as one sentence before drafting anything.
  • It works as the mission that every later section must support.
  • Planning sequence for a promotion idea:
    1. Write the one belief for the promotion idea.
    2. Answer the first seven questions with a couple of lines each.
    3. Map out the whole promotion. This takes about 5 to 10 minutes.
    4. Do the research the promotion needs.
    5. Write each section in order, starting with the sections the map has already defined.
  • Full sales letter sequence, in order:
    1. Research the buyer thoroughly with the ten questions, and connect with their priorities.
    2. Use the offer yourself or talk to its users. Then list every feature with its benefit, sorted by importance.
      • A feature is what the product has. A benefit is what it does for the buyer.
    3. List every objection and identify the flaws in the offer. Then decide how to admit them.
    4. Plan delivery: postage class (the mail price level), envelope design, and whether it is a letter, a postcard or a self-mailer (a folded mail piece with no envelope).
    5. Write the headline that makes the reader feel the letter is welcome.
    6. Build the reading path with subheads (short titles between sections), bold text and short paragraphs. This lets skimmers (readers who scan only parts) get the gist.
    7. Minimise price impact with comparisons, value stacking (listing extras to show total worth) and payment framing (how the price is presented, such as per month).
    8. Use the proven persuasion techniques: scarcity (limited supply) or intimidation, ROI (return on investment, how much the buyer earns back), ego, a strong guarantee and a story.
    9. Write a first draft without editing or worrying about length.
    10. Rewrite for strategy: add repetition, a yes sequence (small agreements that lead to a bigger one) and page-ending teasers (short lines that make the reader turn the page).
    11. Rewrite for style with short sentences, big-impact words and personality.
    12. Answer every question and objection.
    13. Create urgency with a deadline, a premium (a bonus gift) or limited availability.
    14. Add a P.S. (postscript, a short note at the end) that restates the offer.
    15. Run the checklist.
    16. Add graphic devices (visual tools such as boxes, arrows and images), and build the layout with the copywriter involved.
    17. Rewrite for passion and edit aggressively for clarity.
    18. Compare the draft with strong examples.
    19. Pretest it aloud and with real customers, at no cost.
    20. Prepare the final version. Mock it up (make a sample of the real piece), cool off for a day, get second opinions, do a final review, then print and mail.

Flow and the slippery slide

3 min
  • The first seven questions should be answered within the first third of the letter.
  • By then, the reader should be emotionally sold.
  • Only after the emotional sale is complete should the copy turn to logic.
  • Once the reader understands how the mechanism (the way the product works) delivers the result, they are pre-sold (already convinced before the offer).
  • They are now ready for the offer.
  • Present the offer only then. The reader has already accepted that the new opportunity is the key to what they want.
  • Story formula (pattern): [Background fact] and [supporting context], BUT [conflict or problem], THEREFORE [result or conclusion].
    • Use and to set up background and momentum.
    • Use but to create tension.
    • Use therefore to deliver the conclusion.
    • Why it works: stories are how people make sense of information. The but creates the tension that keeps the reader reading.
    • So wrap proof and quotes in this structure instead of listing them.
    • Example 1: Reorders had been climbing for two years, and the team celebrated, BUT the newest customers had stopped coming back after month two, THEREFORE we rebuilt the onboarding sequence around month-two results.
    • Example 2: Our waitlist grew every week and the signups looked healthy, BUT more than half of those people never bought, THEREFORE the first purchase offer now appears on the confirmation page.
  • Each sentence should only have to earn the next sentence.
  • The reader moves through the copy like a train gathering speed.
  • Once they are moving, the next lines become easier to read.
  • That is why short opening sentences work.
  • Slippery slide rule (a rule that keeps readers sliding down the page): every element and sentence must pull the reader forward so strongly that they keep reading to the end.
  • Once a reader gets past about 25 percent of an ad, they are likely to read the whole ad.
  • So the goal is to get them past that point.
  • Seed of curiosity (pattern): end a paragraph with a very short line.
  • The line promises that the next paragraph holds something worth reading.
  • Readers keep going through slower sections.
  • Use it sparingly, because overuse makes copy feel manipulative (tricky and pushy).
    • Examples: The catch is hidden in paragraph four. Most buyers miss the one detail that changes everything, so keep reading.
    • Most buyers miss the detail that changes everything. Keep reading.
    • The cheapest fix takes less than a minute. Here is why it works.
  • Hidden payoff (pattern): state a real benefit near the top of the ad.
  • Say it will be revealed later.
  • So the reader has to read the full copy to get it.
  • The payoff should be real, and it should arrive at the end.
    • Examples: Your refund question gets answered in the final section, after the warranty details.
    • The cheapest way to run this setup is explained at the bottom of the page.
    • The fastest way to cut your phone bill is in the last paragraph, and most readers skip it.
    • The one setting that doubles battery life is explained after the list of features.
  • Never end a page with a complete sentence. It gives the reader permission to stop.
  • End each page mid-sentence, or at an exciting phrase.
  • Add teaser copy (a short hook) at the bottom of the page. It works like a headline for the next page.
  • Copy flows like a one-way chain: each block must anticipate the question raised by the one before it.
  • A reader who stops at any question will not reach the order request (the ask to buy).
  • So check each transition (the link between two blocks).
  • Create a double readership path.
  • Impulsive readers (people who act on quick impressions) skim headlines, subheads, photos with captions and boxed short paragraphs.
  • Analytical readers read every word. They want facts, figures, charts and statistics.
  • Write the skim path so it carries the full offer.
  • Then use it as signposts (guide markers) along the full text for analytical readers.
  • Repetition in one letter (pattern): restate the core promise in six different forms:
    • a plain statement
    • an example
    • a short true story
    • a testimonial (a customer's praise)
    • a quote from an expert or customer
    • a numbered summary
  • Repetition is one of the two ways people accept new ideas. The other is shock.
  • So vary the wording on each pass.

Length

2 min
  • Copy length rule: copy should be long enough to create the buying environment, build interest, relate to the buyer's needs and make the full case.
  • It should be no longer than that.
  • Judge length by whether the reader takes the requested action, not by word count.
  • Higher-priced products need more copy to justify the price and create the need.
  • A low price that looks like a clear value needs less copy.
  • A low price that seems too cheap to be credible needs more copy to build trust.
  • Short copy works only when the photos, the price and the medium (the way the message reaches people, such as print or a catalog) already do the environment-building work.
  • Example: a catalog with strong photography and low prices.
  • Otherwise write more copy.
  • Readers read long copy when they care intensely about the subject.
  • Interest drops once the reader has mastered the topic.
  • So long copy pays off mainly for a target reader who is still learning and deeply involved.
  • The more unusual a product, the more copy it needs.
  • That copy relates the product to the user, builds the buying environment and explains its new features.
  • Mail order suits unusual products that need that much copy. Most retail settings do not allow it.
  • Long copy works when it targets qualified buyers (people who are ready and able to buy).
  • Most readers will glance and discard, and that is acceptable.
  • Write for the buyer, not for the non-buyer.
  • Add pages until the buyer has every fact needed to act.
  • Some letters grew from one or two pages to many, and got better results with each addition.
  • Research cited by the author found the following about industrial ads (ads aimed at businesses, not consumers) with extensive text.
  • Readership drops sharply for copy under about 50 words.
  • It drops much less between 50 and 500 words.
  • Do not shorten copy just to fit a format or page limit.
  • Write to tell the whole story.

Drafting process

1 min
  • Practice produces the largest gains in copywriting.
  • Write every day in small forms: letters, postcards, articles for local papers and ads.
  • Volume builds skill faster than study alone.
  • A first draft is expected to be weak.
  • Do not judge the draft by how it reads.
  • The first draft is an emotional release of everything you want to say.
  • The real quality comes from editing.
  • When drafting, get the copy out first, and ignore spelling and grammar.
  • Switch to logic when editing.
  • Write with the intuitive, emotional side of the mind, and edit with the analytical side.
  • Write the first version as a print direct response ad, even when the final product will be sold on the internet, in a catalog or on TV.
  • The print version reveals the core selling points.
  • Those points can then be used in every other medium.
  • Use a checklist as a mid-course correction in the writing process.
  • Scan the draft against every formula, technique and strategy in the playbook (your set of proven methods).
  • Drafts drift from good practice as they are revised.

Editing

1 min
  • In editing, refine copy so that it expresses exactly what you intend with the fewest words.
  • You can rearrange sentences, cut words that add little, substitute stronger words, or add words for clarity.
  • Keep the same emotional feel.
  • Edit aggressively to clarify, not only to shorten.
  • Remove any word or phrase that does not advance, strengthen or reinforce the core sales story.
  • Edit the draft in several passes, setting it aside for hours between passes.
  • The shorter version usually follows naturally from clearer wording.
  • Compare your draft with several strong examples, not just one.
  • Check whether your text flows as smoothly, speaks to the audience as well, is structurally sound, is as easy to understand, and moves the reader to act as effectively.

Objections, admissions and proof

1 min
  • List every objection (a doubt or reason to say no) a prospect (a person you want to sell to) could have. Write each one on its own card. Then answer each objection in your copy, because a letter that leaves an objection unanswered lets the reader stop at that doubt.
  • Damaging admission (pattern): a repeatable method. Openly state the real drawbacks of your offer and who it is not for. Then show why the trade-off is still worth it for the right buyer. This raises credibility (trust) because readers trust a seller who admits weak points.
    • Example: This is not the cheapest option, and it takes two weeks to ship. If you need it tomorrow, a local store is the better choice, but if you want a tested product that lasts five years, this one is built for you.
    • Second example: We do not offer same-day installation or phone support after 6 pm. If you can plan a week ahead and want a repair done right the first time, you will be glad you waited.

Closing

1 min
  • Write a P.S. (short note added after the signature, "postscript") after every sales letter, because many readers look at the end first. A P.S. that restates the offer or promise acts as a second headline (the main title that grabs attention). Do not consider the letter finished until a P.S. is written.

Envelopes: attention and first impression

3 min
  • Assume recipients sort their mail over a wastebasket and decide in seconds. In business settings an assistant may screen the envelopes first. The envelope must therefore earn the open before the letter is read.
  • Never be half-way in envelope design. If the envelope reveals that the mail is business or sales material, use the whole face as a billboard (a large, eye-catching design) with teaser copy (a short line that makes people curious) and a strong image. Choose one look: the disguised look or the billboard look. Do not mix both.
  • Whatever the envelope promises must be delivered inside, such as a Personal label followed by something personal in the mailing. Unfulfilled envelope promises destroy the credibility of everything enclosed. Fulfilled promises increase it.
  • Sneak-up envelope (pattern): a plain white envelope with no business name, only an address, a person's name as return address (the sender name in the corner), no teaser text, individual or ink-jet addressing (the address printed one at a time by a printer) and a live stamp (a real stamp, not a printed one). It looks like personal mail. The author estimated about a 98 percent chance of delivery and opening with first-class postage (the faster, more expensive mail service). Use it when prospects are cold and you want the envelope read before it is judged.
  • Avoid gummed white address labels (sticky paper labels), which look like junk mail. Individually addressed or ink-jet addressed envelopes produce better response. The author paid home workers to hand-address envelopes, because testing showed the lift justified the cost.
  • A mailing to doctors used the individual doctor's name and address as return address, with no company name. It pulled up to 300 percent better than the same letter in envelopes showing the company. The author reports this from his own tests with a professional audience.
  • Hybrid envelope (tool): combine personalisation (using the reader's own name or details) with teaser copy. For example, use a window envelope (an envelope with a clear plastic window) showing a personalised letter headline with the recipient's name and city. Print teaser text on the front and testimonials (customer praise) on the back. Personalised headlines, body copy and enclosures are now easy to produce with printing technology.
  • Use an attention-getter (a catchy line on the envelope) only with a clear purpose. On third-class mail (the cheapest bulk mail service) a catch-phrase pulls readers into the envelope. On first-class mail, a plain corner card usually pulls better because it looks like a personal letter. Test both versions against each other.
  • If you use a catch-phrase or attention-getter on the outside of an envelope, send the mailing third class to save about half the postage. Otherwise the postage cost of a first-class letter is wasted on an envelope that already looks like an advertisement.
  • Envelope picture (pattern): place a picture linked to the recipient's work or organization on the envelope. Replace the recipient's name line with a care-of line (a line that sends the letter to a company, not a person) for his company. A picture of a person he recognizes from his trade flatters him and draws favorable attention before he reads the letter.
    • Example one: a photo of a shop owner from a trade magazine, addressed as care of the owner's store and street address.
    • Example two: a photo of a plant manager from an industry newsletter, addressed care of the plant's name and city.
  • When a mailing carries a named person's name as the return address, enclose a note the size of a small notepad, signed by that person. The note says the information helped them and asks the reader to go through everything enclosed. This note is the promise the envelope makes, delivered inside, so the reader trusts the rest of the mailing.

Postage and delivery

1 min
  • Third-class bulk mail can have a non-delivery rate (the share of letters that never arrive) of 10 to 30 percent, according to older postal audits (official mail checks). The author believed it was worse. Bulk mail is only justified when the economics cannot support first-class postage. Non-delivery can wreck a campaign without the sender ever knowing.
  • Use first-class postage with a real stamp instead of a meter imprint (a printed mark from a postage machine) to improve delivery odds. Postal workers are least likely to discard mail they believe the recipient expects. Automated stamp-affixing machines (machines that stick stamps on for you) are available for mailers who cannot hand-stamp.
  • Stamps and small postage details (historical tests, US mail of the 1930s):
    • Test stamp colour and denomination (the stamp's face value) before a large mailing. Two red 2-cent stamps pulled more replies than one 4-cent stamp. One red 2-cent stamp pulled better than two green 1-cent stamps. "Pulled" means "brought in".
    • A brown 1.5-cent stamp looked like a 4-cent stamp and pulled better than a green 1-cent stamp. It was no better than two half-cent stamps. Postage stamps also pulled better than metered postage or postal indicia (printed postage marks) in the same tests.
    • Printing the 1-cent metered postage in black ribbon (ink from a typewriter-style ribbon) may outpull a 2-cent stamp or meter in some post offices. Small details such as ink colour and postage type are worth testing, but only after the core letter already works.
  • Fold, indents, postage and stamp colour are minor details. They matter only after the opening, description, argument and close are working. Adding them to a weak letter will not produce results.

Gatekeepers

1 min
  • A gatekeeper (a person who screens mail before the boss sees it, such as an assistant) screens mail for an executive. Send the sales package this way:
    1. Place the sealed sales package inside a plain kraft envelope (a brown paper envelope).
    2. Address the kraft envelope personally to the decision-maker (the person who says yes or no), with no company name on the front.
    3. Put the sender's personal name in the return-address corner.
    4. Send it by overnight courier (a delivery service that arrives the next day), placed inside the courier company's outer envelope. This makes the piece look like a personal, urgent item rather than advertising.

Letter presentation and folds

1 min
  • Fold the letter so that only the salutation (the greeting, such as "Dear...") and first line show when the reader picks it up. Use a military-style fold (a fold like a folded army letter). This fold raised orders by as much as 10 percent in tests.
  • Indent (start the line further in from the margin) the paragraph that carries your most important point. In tests, indentation drew more attention than underlining or all-capital text.

Executive mailings and grabbers

1 min
  • For executive and business-owner mailings, use superior paper and an envelope with texture or a watermark (a faint pattern visible when held to light). Keep enclosures (extra items in the envelope) to a minimum. Place brochures and order forms in a second sealed package inside the main envelope. Prestige words such as exclusive, select, superior, charter member, prominent, worthwhile, yield and individual membership raise perceived status.
  • A grabber is a physical object attached to or sent with the letter to create curiosity, such as a coin, tea bag, miniature object or a trade-show invitation in a bottle. Pairing the grabber with a message that explains why the object was sent gives a reason to read the letter.
  • Cases: a seminar operator mailed a miniature trash can with peanuts. The letter was framed as a squirrel calling the prospect nuts for not responding. The point: not responding equals throwing a large sum of money away. Separately, a trade-show mailing sent a bottled treasure-map invitation with a compass, magnifier and highlighter. It produced a 75 percent increase in targeted traffic (visitors who fit the buyer profile) compared with prior years.
  • Consumer mail needs bold, colourful and attention-grabbing pieces. Give the reader something to do in every section, such as tear-off stamps, scratch-off cards, tokens or stickers. Colour is practically essential in consumer mailings, and colour photographs help.

Folders and multi-panel mailings

1 min
  • A folder works like a magazine advertisement and needs the same parts:
    • an illustration and headline to win attention,
    • an opening that arouses interest,
    • a clear description,
    • a logical argument,
    • a clincher (the final push that closes the sale),
    • and a coupon or card that makes ordering easy.
  • A circular (a single printed sheet) enclosed with a letter adds about 25 to 33 percent to the pulling power of almost any letter. The enclosure should carry the illustrated detail. The letter should carry the argument.
  • Layout of a multi-panel mailing folder (a folded sheet with several sections):
    1. Front panel: place a headline and illustration that win attention on their own.
    2. Opening pages: use a story or question that arouses interest in the offer.
    3. Center pages: describe the offer and present the logical argument with its key features.
    4. Last page: place proof, such as a letter from a third party who verifies the claims.
    5. Include a card or coupon that makes ordering a single action.

Further details

1 min

Build opening-line ideas from published books:

  1. Go to a physical bookstore and pick up novels from authors who write in your reader's genre (type of story).
  2. Write down the first line of each book. Note the hook (the idea that grabs attention), such as danger, a question, or a loss.
  3. Group the lines by hook type. See which types appear most often.
  4. Write several opening lines for your own product using the most common hook types. Then test the strongest ones against your current opener (the first line of your letter).
  • Sales copy (the written words that sell) is a core online marketing skill. It sells through words in print and in search results. Developing this skill can improve results more than most other changes.
