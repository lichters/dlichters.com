Copy frameworks and ad structures

Section 12.7 · 7 of 26 in this chapter · 42 min read

In short

  • Ad copy has one main job: get the reader to click. The sales pitch comes later, on the landing page.
  • Open with the reader's problem. The right people keep reading, and the wrong people skip the ad.
  • Keep each piece of copy to one idea, one feeling, one benefit and one call to action.
  • Build copy in order: first the customer's desire, then the headline last.
  • Put the call to action last, after the feeling and the logic.
More key points
  • Lead with the buyer's own nightly problem. The buyer should recognise it before reading any features.
  • Answer the common objection before the buyer raises it.
  • Tell stories with a clear hero, a problem, real stakes and a guide. Then end with a call to action.
  • Use future pacing and presupposition to make the result feel close and already true.
  • Use prompts, templates and tables exactly as written. Keep every number and id.
  • Each ad or sales text should do one job: one idea, one feeling, one action.
  • Use PAS: name the problem, make it feel worse, then offer your product as the fix.
  • Write the headline first, then check that it grabs attention.
  • End every text with one clear instruction for the reader, such as "Buy now."
  • Check every message with the value equation before it goes live.

Purpose of the copy and the Rule of One

2 min
  • The main goal of ad copy (the written words in an ad) is to get a click, not a direct sale. **
  • Most ecommerce ads (ads for online shops) send people to a landing page (the web page the ad links to).
  • The full sales pitch happens on that landing page. So the ad only has to make the reader click.
  • Starting with a problem statement (one sentence that names the reader's problem) sorts the right people from the wrong ones.
  • People who have the problem keep reading or click.
  • People who do not have the problem skip the ad.
  • This cuts wasted spend (ad money that buys no sales) and irrelevant clicks that cost money. **
  • Each piece of copy follows the Rule of One.
  • The Rule of One means: one central idea, one core emotion (the main feeling), one supporting story or fact, one main benefit, and one call to action.
  • Competing ideas weaken the emotional impact (the feeling the ad creates).
  • Competing ideas also split the reader's attention.
  • Judge every part of an ad by one question: does it get the reader to read the first sentence?
  • The parts are the headline (the big top line), subheadline (the smaller line under it), photo, caption (the text under an image), logo and layout (how everything is arranged).
  • A part that does not lead to the first sentence weakens the ad, even if it looks correct.
  • Putting the main sales pitch at the very beginning usually works worse than building reader interest first.
  • Lead with hooks (attention-grabbing opening lines) and story.
  • Deliver the pitch once the reader is engaged (interested and paying attention).
  • Direct response copy (copy built to get an immediate action, such as a buy or a click) works by making the customer feel understood.
  • The page shows the customer their problem first, then the solution.
  • The solution connects emotionally, so the customer feels the product was made for them.
  • Choose the copy approach by product type.
  • Use emotional copy for products with personal meaning, such as a gift that expresses gratitude toward family.
  • Use problem-solution copy for practical products that fix a specific issue.
  • Not every product needs a problem-first ad.
  • For hobby, identity (how someone sees themselves) or self-expression products, lead with the emotional need.
  • Emotional needs include comfort, control over a space or creative expression.

Copy build order: from desire to headline

3 min

Direct response build order (use in this sequence):

  1. Define the market desire: what the customer wants to reach or move away from.
    • Ask "why" again and again to find the underlying need and emotion (the deeper reason behind the want).
    • Examples of deeper reasons: shame about the home, fear of being judged, lost time.
  2. List the product's strongest features (the things the product has).
    • For each feature, write the reason it exists.
    • For each feature, write the concrete benefit it gives the user.
    • Benefits can be what it lets them do, how it saves time or effort, or how it makes them feel.
  3. Map each benefit to a desire from step 1, so every feature is tied to a goal.
  4. Build a quick persona (a made-up picture of one typical buyer).
    • Demographics are basic facts such as age, job and place. Fill what you can, not everything.
    • Psychographics are values and personality.
    • Include typical behavior.
    • Include needs and motivations: connection, control, freedom from pain, and conserving resources (saving money, time or energy).
    • Include feelings, such as frustrated, tired or ashamed.
    • Include goals, problems and pains.
    • Include the deepest fears.
  5. Draft headlines that combine desire, the persona's specific situation and a benefit.
    • Move from generic to specific.
    • For example, put a specific breed or pet type in the headline.
  6. Set market awareness (how much the customer knows: the problem, the solution or the product).
    • Set market sophistication (how many similar products the customer has already seen).
    • Then choose the headline style and mechanism (how the product works) to match.
  7. Apply psychological principles (proven ways to change how people think) and headline tactics to refine the copy.
    • Then write the body copy (the main text) and the call to action.
  • Work in this order: define the market's desire.
  • Then list features and turn each one into a benefit.
  • Then build a quick persona.
  • Then choose the headline using market awareness and sophistication.
  • Each step feeds the next, so the headline comes last.

Build copy in this fixed order:

  1. List the customer's main desires (what they want to get or get away from). Dig into the underlying tension (the inner pull or conflict behind each desire).
  2. List the product's unique features. For each one, write the concrete benefit it gives the customer.
  3. Build a persona covering demographics (location, occupation), psychographics (values, personality type), behavior, the context in which they need the product, and their underlying needs, motivations and feelings.
  4. Determine the customer's market awareness stage.
  5. Determine the market sophistication stage.
  6. Choose the psychological principles to apply. Then write the headline, body copy and ad script (the words for a video ad).

  • The value equation is a formula to check whether a message gives real value.
  • Value = (Dream Outcome multiplied by perceived Likelihood of achievement) divided by (Time plus Effort plus Sacrifice).
  • The dream outcome is the best result the customer wants.
  • Perceived likelihood is how likely the customer believes it is to work.
  • Sacrifice means what the customer gives up, such as money or comfort.
  • Copy should raise the dream outcome.
  • Copy should reduce the pain.
  • Copy should raise the perceived likelihood that it will work.
  • Copy should cut time, effort and sacrifice.
  • This is the Alex Hormozy value equation.

Sales copy order and body structure

5 min

Top-to-bottom order for a product sales copy (Source B method, older advice 2021-22):

  1. Primary promise: the main headline-level benefit.
  2. Painting a picture: a vivid description of the customer's situation or the result.
  3. Unique mechanism: why this product works differently.
  4. Remaining features and benefits.
  5. Power benefits: strong, emotional outcomes.
  6. Call to action at the end.
  • The call to action comes last.
  • It comes after the emotion and the logic (the feelings and reasons) have been delivered.

Body copy structure (general):

  • The headline opens the reader's attention.
  • The main body leads into the problem and how the product solves it.
  • A short segment builds the click and prepares the call to action.
  • The call to action asks the reader to act.
  • It builds on a belief the reader already holds, and moves them toward the belief you want.
  • Add these elements where they fit.
  • Problem-agitate-solution (name the problem, make it feel worse, then offer the fix) goes in the body.
  • Curiosity questions make people want the answer. Example: "What if you could come home to a [clean result] every day?"
  • Authority (proof of expertise). Example: "approved by [expert group] across [country]".
  • Similarity (you are like them). Example: "as [customer type] ourselves, we understand...".
  • Features and their benefits.
  • Social proof (signs that others trust it). Example: "Join thousands of happy customers".
  • Future pacing (picturing life after the purchase).
  • Limited-time offers or a free gift, when they are true.

Body copy opening:

  • Start by tapping into a belief or frustration the customer already holds.
  • Readers accept claims that match their own experience.
  • Common examples are a chore they dislike or a problem they already expect.
  • Example: "Keeping pet hair under control can be frustrating, especially when it returns as soon as you finish cleaning."

Body copy structure, step by step:

  1. Open with a belief or frustration the reader already holds.
  2. Agitate the problem (make it feel worse). Describe how hard it is to fix and how it returns.
  3. Pose a curiosity question that promises relief.
  4. Name the unique feature or mechanism as the solution.
  5. Put a number on the claim. Ideally compare it with competitors (other brands selling the same thing). Example: "twice as much as any other tool."
  6. Add an ease claim (a claim that it is simple or fast). Example: "in just a couple of swipes."
  7. Add a second feature and its benefit that removes time, effort or sacrifice.
  8. Add social proof.
  9. End with a call to action. You can add an offer, such as a free second unit.

Body copy template (fill in the brackets):

[Opening belief or frustration the reader already has about the problem]. [Agitation: how hard the problem is to fix and how it returns]. What if you could [desired outcome] every day? This is now possible thanks to [product name]. Its [unique feature or mechanism] [quantified comparison claim with competitors] in [short ease or speed claim]. It also has [second feature] that [removes a specific time, effort or sacrifice], so you can [outcome]. Join [number of customers or other social proof] who already [result]. [Call to action]: [offer, e.g. a free extra unit or a discount] when you order today.

Benefit stacking (adding several strong parts to one ad) and feature-to-benefit conversion:

  • These ad parts strengthen direct response ads when a brand lacks equity (brand equity is how well-known and trusted the brand already is).
  • An attention hook that highlights a fact or the problem being solved.
  • The big benefit or unique selling proposition (USP: the one thing that sets the product apart), which keeps viewers watching.
  • A compelling offer (a deal that is hard to turn down).
  • A clear call to action, such as "Shop now" or "Buy now".
  • Benefit-focused copy that states what the product does for the person, backed by features.
  • Social proof, since people copy what others do. This is a cognitive shortcut (a quick mental rule the brain uses).
  • Scarcity or urgency, such as limited time or limited stock. Scarce items feel more valuable.
  • Educational or entertaining content, since most Instagram and TikTok users want entertainment rather than a sales pitch.
  • Curiosity sparking (making people want to know more).
  • These elements do not all need to appear in every ad.
  • For each feature, write the concrete benefit it gives the customer.
  • Benefits include a cleaner home, fresher clothes, more comfort, lower cost or saved time.
  • Use the same angle and vocabulary (word choice) as the primary promise.
  • Use efficiency words like "quickly" and "effectively", so the copy flows from the headline.
  • Test different scenarios of the same product, such as morning use versus evening use.
  • Compare the results to see which one converts better (turns more viewers into buyers).

Short product ad pattern:

  • Open with a hook that names the problem.
  • Present the product as the answer.
  • List two to four concrete benefits. Put each on its own text line with a tick or icon (a small symbol).
  • Close with a time-limited offer (a deal that ends soon) and a clear call to action.
    • Example one: a hook about tired feet at the end of a shift, a stainless steel insole (the pad inside a shoe) presented as the fix, four benefit lines, then "Pairs ship free this week, tap below."
    • Example two: a hook about dull kitchen counters, a cleaning spray presented as the solution, three benefit lines, then "Second bottle half price until Sunday, order now."

Takeaway statement (a closing line that says who the product is not for):

  • Name the reader the product is not for, and the reason, before returning to the offer.
  • This raises trust and desire among true prospects (people who really could buy).
  • Formula: "This is not for [excluded reader] if [condition]. It is built for [target reader] who [need]."
    • Example: "This is not for weekend hobbyists who run once a month if you only need a basic pair. It is built for daily runners who log over 40 miles a week."
    • Example: "Skip this mixer if you bake twice a year. It is made for home bakers who knead dough every week."

Problem-first frameworks: PAS and its variants

7 min

Core PAS:

  • Problem-Agitate-Solution (PAS) is a three-step ad method: name the problem, make it hurt more, then offer the fix.
  • PAS is one of the best frameworks (ready-made structures) for moving people to action.
  • Its steps: outline the problem.
  • Make the emotional impact worse.
  • Offer the solution, then provide comfort.
  • Finish with a strong call to action.
  • First, name the problem.
  • Then make it feel worse. Spell out its consequences and how it feels.
  • Then present the product as the solution.
  • Finally, intensify the benefits by asking what each one means for the customer.
  • Use PAS in headlines, body copy and ad scripts.

Problem-Agitate-Solution steps, in order:

  1. State the problem the customer is already experiencing.
  2. Agitate it by making it worse. Describe how it feels, for example frustrating, embarrassing or anxious.
  3. Present the product as the solution.
  4. Name the features.
  5. Translate each feature into its benefit for the customer, such as a cleaner home, fresher clothes, more comfort, lower cost or saved time.
  6. Close with the outcome, such as sleeping easily or being comfortable hosting guests.

Pain-Agitate-Solve framework:

  1. Describe the problem in the hook. You can quote customers.
  2. Explain the problem in detail. Intensify it, or villainize it (make it the bad guy).
  3. Present the product as the solution to the problem.
  4. Describe how the product works.
  5. State the product benefits.
  6. Show the result and how it improves the customer's life.

Problem, agitate, solution pattern:

  • Name a problem. Make it feel worse by hitting the audience's pride or identity. Then present the product as the fix.
  • The agitation step is what makes the solution feel necessary.
    • Example one: "Your running shoes look fresh, but your insoles smell after two miles, and everyone at the gym notices before you do. Switch to a sole that dries in an hour."
    • Example two: "Your garden looks tired all summer because the soil is starving, and your neighbour's roses prove the difference. Feed the soil with this and watch the change in one month."

Problem, agitate, solution framework:

  • Name the problem. Make it feel worse. Then show the relief, followed by a call to action.
  • This creates urgency before the offer appears.
    • Example: "Still waking up with a stiff neck? Every night of bad sleep adds to the strain. Our adjustable pillow supports your neck in the position you actually sleep. Try it free for 30 nights."

Problem-agitate-solution steps for video, in order:

  1. Name the problem the viewer already feels.
  2. Make the problem feel worse by showing its consequences.
  3. Present the product as the solution.
  4. List the benefits and explain the mechanism that delivers them.
  5. Show the desired outcome the viewer gets after using the product.
  • Example for snowboard shorts (padded shorts for snowboarding): open on the rider's problem of falling often in the park and losing progress.
  • Make that pain feel worse by showing the time lost and being laughed at.
  • Present the thick absorbent padding (soft padding that soaks up impact) as the mechanism that moves the impact away from the body.
  • End on the desired outcome of riding jumps and progressing faster.

PAS script formula:

  • Open by showing the customer's problem.
  • Agitate it by showing how serious it is and why action is needed now.
  • Present the product as the solution, with its concrete benefits.
  • The viewer feels the pain before seeing the fix.
  • Example: a worker wakes up tired from a poor mattress and sees how poor sleep disrupts daily tasks. Then they see the mattress giving restful sleep and better daily productivity.

PAS copy pattern:

  • State the customer's problem and desired goal. Intensify the discomfort around it. Then present the product as the solution with its key benefits.
    • Pattern: "Cold feet are a real problem: [pain point with a credible statistic]. [Desired goal]. Our [product] is the [superlative] way to [outcome], designed to [benefit]."
    • Example: "Hot water bills are a real problem: your heater runs non-stop in winter. You want a warm home without the cost. Our smart thermostat is the most efficient way to keep rooms comfortable, designed to cut heating waste."
    • Example: "Tangled cables are a real problem: your desk looks chaotic and you lose time searching. You want a tidy workspace. Our cable organizer is the simplest way to clear clutter, designed to keep every cord in place."

Problem-Benefit-Solution variant of PAS (a version that skips the agitate step):

  • Instead of agitating the problem, state the problem next to the desired emotional goal.
  • Then present the product as the solution that delivers that goal.
  • The copy talks to the customer's need directly, rather than listing product specs (technical details).

Problem-then-solution copy formula:

  • Name the problem the buyer already has.
  • Make its cost or discomfort feel more serious.
  • Present the product as a fast or convenient fix with a concrete outcome.
  • The reader feels the problem first and sees the product as the relief.
    • Example for a sleep aid: "Waking up at 3 a.m. again? Stop relying on sleeping tablets that leave you groggy. Our drop-free formula helps you fall back asleep within minutes."
    • Example for a back-support cushion: "Sitting all day wrecks your lower back. Switch to a cushion built for long desk shifts and feel the ache ease from the first session."

Pain-point copy pattern:

  • Pick a pain point that many customers share.
  • Expand it to show how large the problem is and which further problems it causes.
  • Present the product as the solution. Describe the improvement in daily life.
  • The reader connects emotionally before seeing the product.
    • Example: "Back pain that follows you from the desk to the couch? Every week it steals more energy from work and evenings with family. This cushion relieves pressure so you can sit comfortably through the whole day."
    • Example: "Hair that frizzes the moment humidity hits? Every morning starts with a fight, and you end up hiding your hair under a hat. This serum keeps curls smooth from sunrise to dinner."

Agitation ladder:

  • Write three lines. They move from a physical problem, to a home or daily-life problem, to a social or emotional consequence.
  • Each line describes a concrete situation the customer lives through.
  • Each step raises the cost of doing nothing, so the solution feels necessary.
    • "My skin is itching all day. My bedsheets look dirty even after washing. I stopped inviting my partner's family over."
    • "The kids keep tracking mud through the hallway. The rug looks grey every morning. I feel embarrassed when the neighbours visit."

Avoid-negative-outcome row (a step that shows what the customer loses by waiting):

  • State what the customer loses by not buying, such as health risks, a dirty home and continued embarrassment.
  • Use it to create urgency after the outcome section.
  • Tie each consequence to the same problem used in the hook.
  • This keeps the message consistent from start to finish.

Problem-aware ad formula (the reader already knows the problem and has tried fixes):

  • Name the problem the reader already feels.

  • Then name the fixes they have already tried, or the costly or painful options.

  • Only then introduce the product as the way out.

  • The reader's frustration with old options makes the product feel like the logical next step.

    • "Tired of paying for laser sessions that fade after a month? Here is what can be done at home."
    • "Knee pain is not a reason to stop running, and painkillers only hide the pain. Here is the daily routine that works on the joint itself."

  • Naming a specific problem in the ad, such as smoker lines (fine lines around the mouth) or dark hollow under-eye areas, lets the viewer recognise their own issue at once.

  • State the solution right after the problem, so the link between them is obvious.

Universal pain point steps:

  1. Name a universal pain point that the target buyer shares, such as a boring routine (for example, "cardio is boring").
  2. Introduce the product directly as the fix for that pain.
  3. Promise that the product works for every level or type of user, for example "designed for everyone no matter your fitness level".

Pattern:

  • Use this pattern: [common frustration stated as a universal fact]. We fix that with [product].
  • It names a pain the reader already feels and positions the product as the answer.
    • "Cold coffee every morning is annoying. We fix that with a self-heating mug."
    • "Slow checkout on phones loses sales. We fix that with one-tap payment."

Segmented ad message formula:

  • Name a specific audience.
  • Describe their specific context or problem.
  • Give one sharp promise that solves it.
  • A narrow audience feels the message is written for them, which increases response.
    • Home cooks who keep nicking their fingers on the grater: a cut-resistant glove that keeps grip while slicing.
    • Commuters tired of arriving with soaked shoes: a foldable waterproof cover that fits in a work bag.

Statistic pattern (a number that describes a group):

  • Open with a statistic about a share of the audience that has a shared deficiency (a shared lack or problem).
  • List the symptoms it causes.
  • Explain the mechanism behind them (the cause).
  • Then present the product as the fix.
  • The reader recognises themselves, understands why the problem persists, and sees a solution that matches the cause.
  • Use only figures you can back with a source.
    • "[X]% of remote workers report stiff shoulders by mid-afternoon; long hours hunched over a laptop weaken the muscles that hold posture. [Product] supports those muscles with a short daily routine."
    • "[X]% of new runners quit within six months, often because worn-out shoes cause shin pain. [Product] adds cushioning where the shoe loses support first."

Underdog principle:

  • Ads that cast the customer as the underdog (someone who wants a change) tend to outperform stories built on an all-powerful villain.
  • They also tend to outperform stories about a brand that already has everything.
  • Readers tend to root for the person who is trying to improve.

Opening patterns (hooks and first lines)

1 min
  • Opening pattern for product copy: A hook is the first line that grabs attention. Name a frustration the buyer already feels at night. Then show the morning result. Then explain how the product works. The buyer recognises their own problem before reading any features.
    • "Lying awake at 3 a.m. again? Wake up with energy that lasts through the day."
    • "Stiff shoulders after a night on the sofa? A snug wrap that helps your body settle before bed."
  • Angle-based ad copy: An angle is one point of view on the problem. Contrast how a buyer feels after a good night with how they feel after a bad one. Then present the product as the bridge between the two. A gap between two states is easier to feel than a list of features.
    • "Rested mornings or groggy meetings? See what one better night can change."
    • "Tight neck, cold feet, no sleep? Notice the difference by morning."
  • Copy opening with the story or thought command: Copy is the written sales text. Start the copy right after the headline with a story or a thought command (see below). This is the first element of the copy. Choose the story if one exists. Otherwise use the thought commands. **

Objection handling

1 min
  • An objection is a reason the buyer says no. Handle the most common objection first, before the shopper raises it. For example, state that cheap knockoffs are never the same as the original product.
  • Pattern: [dismissal of cheap alternatives] + [short reason they fall short]. The objection is answered before the buyer voices it.
    • "Do not be fooled by cheap imitations, they are never built the same."
    • "Cheap lookalike bags skip the lining, so they are never the same."
  • Three-step pattern: [short label]: [step one], [step two], [step three], done. Three steps make the process look simple and quick.
    • "Easy start: pick your size, clip it on, go."
    • "Easy checkout: choose, pay, track."

Story and emotional frameworks

3 min

Hero's journey as an ad storyline (a story where the customer is the hero):

  • The customer is the hero with a goal.
  • The problem is the villain. It must be made to look bad.
  • Stakes show what happens if the hero fails.
  • Doubt in their own ability is shown.
  • A credible guide appears. The guide is the product, with proof of use or authority. The guide gives a path and the tools to win.
  • The customer is called to action.
  • The story ends with the hero winning, surviving and thriving.

Storytelling structure, in order:

  1. Give the hero a goal.
  2. Introduce an obstacle and make it the villain. For example, the constant hair on the bed.
  3. Show the stakes. What happens if they fail, such as illness, allergies or losing guests.
  4. Show the hero's self-doubt about reaching the goal.
  5. Introduce a guide with credibility and proof who shows the way.
  6. Call the reader to action.
  7. Give the reader the means to win.

Hook-story-offer:

  • Open with a hook. It names the reader's exact frustration and shows empathy.
  • Tell a short personal story that makes the struggle concrete.
  • End with an offer. The reader should feel understood before the offer appears.
    • Example: "Tired of finding fur on your clean clothes? I had the same problem and was embarrassed to invite friends over. Now I use a brush that clears it in minutes, and you can get it at a 20% discount this week."

Product story from the founder:

  • A product story can be told word for word from the founder's own experience. For example, the frustration that led to developing the product.
  • It attracts people who share that frustration. It makes them open when the product is presented. **

Story formula for health and science claims:

  • Formula: [Discovery or event] that [surprising result], which means [consequence for the reader].
  • Tell the story: who studied what, what they found and what it led to. Do not just list study results.
  • A story keeps the reader in an emotional frame. A bare list of results pushes them into analysis and lowers response.
    • A warehouse engineer timed every pick route for a month, found that one aisle layout doubled walking distance, and that change is why the new layout cuts shipping delays.
    • A soil scientist tested two fertilizer schedules across three seasons, found that one schedule kept yields steady when rain was scarce, and that is why the new schedule is built around dry weeks.

Teach, transform, transact:

  • Use email series, testimonials (customer quotes) that share results of results, or direct imagination triggers.
  • Draw the audience into an emotional state that closes the deal.
  • People buy on emotion, not facts and figures.

Formula with a blame shift:

  • Formula: If you've tried [common solution] and got [disappointing result], it's not your fault: [common solution] only ever addressed [surface cause], while the real blocker was [real cause]. [New opportunity] is built to remove that real blocker.
  • It removes blame from the reader. The blame moves to the old approaches. This makes the new mechanism look like the logical fix.
    • "If you've tried sleep trackers and still wake up tired, it's not your fault: they only count hours, while the real blocker is late-day light exposure, which our routine resets."
    • "If your ads keep getting clicks but no sales, it's not your fault: most tools only measure traffic, while the real gap is the checkout step where buyers drop off, which our tracker pinpoints."

Formula with an outside enemy:

  • Formula: [Frustrating outside force the audience already distrusts] is standing between you and [desire].
  • It names an enemy the reader already feels. This creates an us-versus-them feeling. It puts the reader on the seller's side.
    • "Big-box retailers that bury upsells in checkout are standing between you and a fair price on home repairs." (An upsell is an extra product offered at checkout.)
    • "Supplement brands that hide their exact doses are standing between you and results you can actually measure."

Status quo and contrast

1 min

The status quo is the way people do it now.

  • Pattern for contrasting the status quo: Start with "Normally you would". Then list two or three painful steps. Then name the cost or inconvenience of the current way. Then present the product as the shortcut. This makes the old way feel costly. It frames the product as the easy option. **
    • "Normally you would drive across town, wait in line and pay a fee just to get one key cut. This compact key cutter lets you do it from your kitchen table in two minutes."
    • "Normally you would call three installers, wait a week for a quote and pay a call-out fee just to hang one shelf. This kit lets you mount it yourself in ten minutes with just a drill."
  • Before-and-after framework:
    1. Show progress over time. Use a carousel ad (several images you swipe through in one ad) when possible.
    2. Do not label it "before and after". Use "with and without". This shows the quiet effect of your product.
  • Avoid negative ads. Avoid before-and-after images that dwell on problems. Tell readers what to do instead. Ads that show bright, desirable outcomes outperform those that show the gloomy side. (Note on use: the negative-impact lines below show the cost of inaction in one clear sentence. Then move to the bright outcome. Do not dwell on the problem.)

Future pacing, presupposition and negative impact

3 min

Future pacing (making the buyer feel the result before buying):

  • Future pacing places the buyer inside the outcome as if it has already happened. For example, a clean, hair-free bed. The reader feels the result before buying.
  • It works best once the problem, the solution and the audience are already clear.
  • Describe the customer already using the product and enjoying the result. They experience the outcome in their mind before buying.
  • Pattern: "Once your [product] arrives, you will instantly notice [result]."
    • "Once your standing desk arrives, you will instantly notice how much less your back aches by Friday."
    • "Once your new running shoes arrive, you will instantly feel how much lighter each morning run becomes."
  • Describe the moment after purchase as if the reader has already acted and is already enjoying the result. This makes the benefit feel real and near.
    • "Once your new kettle arrives, you will have coffee brewing in under a minute."
    • "Imagine walking into a home that smells fresh the moment you open the door."
  • Future pacing is also the last step of the thought-command pattern. The scene follows directly from the main promise, so the copy stays consistent. See the thought-command pattern below.

Emotional build in advance (procedure, in order):

  1. Take the audience through an emotional build. Use humour to lower their guard. Then use a vulnerable story to deepen the connection.
  2. Paint an aspirational picture of life after buying. Use concrete detail. Begin with the word "Imagine".
  3. Make the offer.
  4. Only if they have not bought: paint the pain of doing nothing. Show what their life looks like if nothing changes.
  5. Close with a simple question that asks whether their life would be acceptable if nothing changed.

Template for the aspirational picture:

Imagine waking up [TIMEFRAME] from now. [SPECIFIC PROBLEM SOLVED, e.g. your bills are paid / your back no longer hurts]. [CONCRETE DAILY MOMENT, e.g. you pick your kids up from school / you walk into the gym without dreading it]. [SOCIAL OR EMOTIONAL REACTION, e.g. friends comment on how well you look / your partner notices your energy]. [A SENSORY OR EMOTIONAL CLOSING DETAIL, e.g. you feel calm and proud of what you've achieved]. Imagine what that feels like.

Template for the pain of doing nothing:

I understand if you're still on the fence. It's natural to feel some fear about buying. Let's think for a moment about what happens if you don't.

Imagine waking up [TIMEFRAME] from now and [PAIN DETAIL, e.g. your credit card debt has grown from $10,000 to $15,000 / your health has gotten worse]. [DAILY CONSEQUENCE, e.g. you dread going to work / you miss moments that matter]. [RELATIONSHIP OR EMOTIONAL CONSEQUENCE].

I'm not saying this to be harsh. I'm saying it because there is a cost to doing nothing, and I don't want you to be in exactly the same place [TIMEFRAME] from now. If your life was exactly the same in [TIMEFRAME], would you be okay with that?

Presupposition (wording that assumes the answer is yes):

  • Word a sentence so it assumes the purchase or a fact is already true. The reader is not asked whether to accept it.
  • Pattern: "The last [product] you will ever need to buy" or "Have you tried the most effective [product] on the market?" The reader answers about their experience, not about the claim.
    • "The last kitchen timer you will ever need to buy."
    • "Have you noticed how quickly your pantry stays organized with this shelf?"
  • Assume the outcome rather than arguing for it. Phrases like "the last X you will ever need to buy" or "have you tried the most effective X" assume the reader has the benefit, or will. Avoid claiming a ranking the product cannot defend.
    • "The last password manager you will ever need to set up."
    • "Have you tried the sleep routine most nurses swear by?"

Negative impact (showing the cost of doing nothing):

  • Show what happens if the customer does not act. They fear the cost of inaction.
  • Pattern: "Stop [unwanted result] before it [bigger consequence]."
    • "Stop your kitchen from smelling of old grease before your guests notice."
    • "Stop the water stains from spreading before they ruin your hardwood floor."
  • Spell out the downside if the customer does nothing. For example, a home slowly overrun, or a problem that gets worse over time. Customers act more readily to avoid a clear, specific downside than to chase a vague upside.
    • "Every week you wait, the mould under the sink spreads a little further."

Feelings-first and identity-led copy

1 min
  • Lead with the feeling the buyer wants to have. For emotional products, this comes before the features. (See the product-type rule above.)

Thought commands and picture-led copy

1 min

A thought command is a short opening phrase that starts a mental picture.

  • Thought command pattern: Open with a command phrase such as "Imagine", "What if" or "How would you feel if". Then describe a concrete picture of life with the main promise delivered.
  • The phrase makes the reader start imagining the scene automatically.
  • The scene must follow directly from the main promise. This keeps the copy consistent.
    • "Imagine finishing your morning run and feeling your sore muscles loosen within minutes of stepping into your home sauna."
    • "What if your evenings felt lighter, with a short routine that leaves you calm enough to sleep through the night?"
  • Picture-of-daily-life pattern: Describe a simple, realistic sequence of the customer's day with the product. Go from the first action to the end result.
  • Each step must be easy to imagine. The reader can then picture using it at home.
    • "You wake up, walk to the kitchen and start your coffee machine. By the time the kettle boils, your cold plunge is ready, and you step in feeling awake."
    • "You finish work, open the app on your phone and start the massage chair. Ten minutes later you walk away from the desk with your shoulders loose."
  • Example of the sequence for an infrared sauna product: the customer rolls out of bed, switches the sauna on, makes a coffee while it heats, then relaxes inside for about 15 minutes, and comes out feeling great and ready for the day. The copy works by making the reader see this routine happening in their own home.

Sales ad sequences: hook, education, curiosity, product, proof, close

4 min

Proven ad structure (from hook to call to action):

  • Order: Hook, education, curiosity, product introduction, benefit demonstration, social proof, agitate-solution, call to action.
  • The hook names a surprising fact the audience has not heard.
  • Education explains why it matters.
  • Curiosity hints at a solution.
  • The product is shown solving the problem, with a visible benefit.
  • Social proof (other people's praise) and a strong call to action follow.
  • Each step moves the viewer one stage forward.
    • Example for a dog toothbrush brand: "Your puppy's breath could be a warning sign of hidden gum damage." Then explain that bacteria build up under the gumline. Hint at a simple daily fix. Show the brush in use. Add "Join 10,000 owners cleaning their dog's teeth at home". End with "Order your starter kit today."
  • Ad structure in order:
    • A compelling opening. For example, "Dog owners never ignore these three things in your dog's teeth".
    • Instructional content that explores the underlying concern, such as gingivitis (inflamed gums).
    • Engagement content that raises questions. For example, "What if I told you there was a solution?"
    • The commodity (the everyday product or service) and how it solves the problem. Show how it works.
    • Customer testimonials. For example, "Join thousands of dog owners seeing amazing results".
    • A powerful call to action.
  • Hero-style AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action): move the viewer through these four stages, in this order. Each stage prepares the next. The viewer moves from noticing the ad to buying.
    • Example: a bold opening question about cracked heels grabs attention. A short demonstration builds interest. A before-and-after result creates desire. A clear call to shop ends the video ad.

Means-end chain (the benefit of the benefit):

  1. Write the product's basic feature.
  2. Ask what the benefit of that feature is.
  3. Ask again what the benefit of that benefit is. Repeat until the answer is a final outcome, such as making money, saving time or enjoying family life.
  4. Build the copy around that final outcome. Show the positive result, not the mechanics.
  • Most people do not buy a product for its physical form. They buy the outcome it produces, such as cooking faster or reaching a place sooner.
  • Lead with the outcome. Treat the product's features as proof that the outcome is achievable.
  • Sales-transition sequence: a path that moves the buyer from knowing you to buying.
    1. Know: the buyer gets to know you through story and personal level.
    2. Like: show humour and share personal stories to find common ground.
    3. Trust: share successes and use video testimonials.
    4. Challenge: challenge their view of the problem. Offer an alternative solution. Create a "eureka moment" (a sudden moment of insight).
    5. Convert: sell insights, not solutions. Plant the idea of what results could mean. Sell the "benefit of the benefit".

    1. Write the headline. Check that it grabs the reader. (The headline is the big top line.)
    2. Write the subheadline. Check that it makes the reader want to read further. (The subheadline is the line under the headline.)
    3. Write the photo caption, since readers look at captions often.
    4. Write the first sentence. Keep it short enough to pull the reader into the second sentence.

Ad structure library

2 min

A structure library is a set of reusable ad outlines.

Prompt for building the structure toolkit:

From successful campaign scripts, identify the repeating beat sequences and build a reusable structure toolkit. Document whether the approach is to apply an exact brought structure, select from a skeleton menu, or make a case-by-case judgment call. Separate narrative attitude (which stays constant) from production format (which varies). List psychological moves that succeed with a worked example for each. Catalog the distinct structural patterns found in winners, explain how to combine them with voice and moves, and keep a log for patterns that don't fit the existing set.
  • A skeleton is a reusable outline of an ad.
  • Expect 3-5 distinct winning structures from your winning scripts when you build the Structure Library.
  • Derive them only from actual scripts. Do not invent structures that do not appear in the proven winners.
  • The Structure Library defines three modes for building ad scripts:
    • MODE 1: when given a specific brought structure to work from, rebuild exactly that structure. Do not force a stock skeleton onto it.
    • MODE 2: when given only an idea with no template, pick the best-fit skeleton from your proven winners.
    • MODE 3: when given a template that could go either way, flag the choice. Present both options: convert it to a proven skeleton, or keep its original shape. Let the decision-maker choose before writing.
    • Structures from outside the proven set are valid. Do not reject them automatically.
  • A brought structure is a template provided from outside your proven winning ads. This is different from a structure reverse-engineered from your own top performers.
    • In MODE 1, rebuild the brought structure exactly.
    • In MODE 3, decide whether to convert it to a proven skeleton or keep its original shape.
  • Pattern for winning ad copy: reframe the problem as not permanent. Name the mechanism that works. Add social proof with a number and a timeframe. Then close with a risk-free guarantee (a promise to refund with no cost to the buyer). Keep this sequence. Swap in your own problem, mechanism, proof and guarantee.
    • Example 1: "Stubborn hair thinning doesn't have to stay. Our scalp serum uses a peptide complex (a group of small protein pieces) to support follicles while you sleep, and thousands of customers report fuller hair within weeks. Try it with our 60-day money-back guarantee, no questions asked."
    • Example 2: "Puffy eyes don't have to be permanent. Our cooling gel-patch works on the swelling overnight, and over 5,000 reviewers say they see a difference by the second week. Risk-free: return it within 30 days for a full refund."

Ad elements and their order

1 min

A complete ad can contain these elements:

  • a strong hook (the first line that grabs attention)
  • a compelling offer (what the buyer gets for the money; optional)
  • a clear call to action (a plain instruction telling the reader what to do next, such as "Buy now")
  • benefit-focused copy (text about what the product does for the buyer)
  • social proof (signs that other people approve, such as reviews; optional)
  • scarcity (limited supply) or urgency (a reason to act now)
  • high-quality visuals (clear, professional pictures or video)
  • education or entertainment
  • curiosity (a gap that makes the reader want the answer)

Not every element is required in every ad.

Personal offer and founder story hooks

1 min

Personal-version offer. Offer a special, personally made version of the product. Present it as something the seller would keep for himself, and invite the reader to accept it as a mark of respect. This works because pride responds to being singled out. A personal gift feels more valuable than a standard offer.

  • Example one: I have had made for my own use a version with reinforced seams that most shops never offer; I would like you to try the first one before it reaches the market.
  • Example two: Our founder built this model for his own workshop, and we are letting a few long-standing customers buy it before it is listed.

Founder story. Tell the human story behind the product. Name who made it and the specific event that led to it, so the product becomes memorable. A story gives the reader something to care about beyond the specifications (the technical details, such as size and materials).

  • Example one: A fisherman who lost his boat in a storm designed a lightweight anchor that one person can carry and that stores under a seat.
  • Example two: A nurse who spent her nights repacking supplies for a rural clinic built a kit that fits in a backpack and can be restocked in minutes.

Sales letter structure

1 min

A sales letter is a written sales message. Six essentials, in this order:

  1. Opening: get attention by fitting into the reader's current thoughts. Build a point of contact (a link) with his interests, and make him want to keep reading.
  2. Description: picture the offer by first outlining its key features, then filling in the necessary details.
  3. Motive: show what the offer will do for the reader, such as comfort, pleasure or profit, rather than describing the offer itself.
  4. Proof or guarantee: give evidence that your claims are true, or build confidence with a money-back guarantee (you refund the price if the buyer is not happy).
  5. Snapper or penalty: a final push that shows the cost of waiting. Hold over the reader's head the loss of money, prestige or opportunity he faces if he does not act now.
  6. Close: tell the reader exactly what to do and how to do it, and make the action easy.

First sales letter. Use a first sales letter with three jobs: select readers who already care about the idea, make the first commitment feel easy, and remove the risk. In one historical series, the first letter targeted adults who lacked a college education and wanted culture, then offered a short daily reading habit and a free trial.

Face-to-face selling adapted to print

1 min

Sequence for print copy (words for paper ads and letters), adapted from face-to-face selling (selling in person):

  1. Set up the selling environment through layout, tone and the first paragraphs.
  2. Get the prospect's attention with the headline (the big title at the top).
  3. Introduce yourself and keep attention through the subheadline (the smaller line under the headline), the photo and the caption (the short text under a picture).
  4. Deliver the sales pitch in the body copy (the main text) while keeping the reader saying yes.
  5. Show the reader that you know the product and relate it to their needs.

Print ad copy sequence

1 min

Write the copy (the written words of the ad) in this order:

  1. Write the headline and check it grabs the reader.
  2. Write the subheadline and check it makes the reader want to read further.
  3. Write the photo caption, since captions are read often.
  4. Write the first sentence, short enough to pull the reader into the second sentence.
  5. Write each next sentence so it answers the question the reader is likely to have at that point.
  6. Build the body in blocks (separate parts of the main text):
    • hook and set the mood

Problem-agitate-solution (PAS)

2 min

Principle. Problem-agitate-solution (PAS) is a copy framework (a simple writing pattern). Surface the problem, make it feel worse, then present the product as the solution. It is especially useful for direct response video ads (ads that ask for an immediate purchase).

Structure. PAS works because it first makes the viewer feel the pain and then offers relief. This gives a clean structure for surfacing a customer's problem. The three parts are:

  1. Problem: surface the problem the customer experiences.
  2. Agitate: make the problem worse and attach an emotion (a feeling such as fear or frustration), so the viewer feels the pain.
  3. Solution: present the product as the answer to that problem, followed by features (what the product has) and benefits (what it does for the buyer).

Examples.

  • Example A, window-cleaning product: Streaks on your windows after every clean? Every wipe just smears the grime around and the glass looks worse than before. This squeegee with a rubber edge and a built-in drip tray leaves a streak-free finish in one pass.
  • Example B, back-pain cushion: Sitting at your desk all day leaves your lower back aching by 3 p.m., and each week it gets worse. This ergonomic cushion with a contoured memory-foam core takes the pressure off your spine so the afternoon slump is gone.

Example from snowboarding. The problem is repeated falls that cause pain, slow progress and embarrassment. The solution is thick padding that absorbs shock on hips and body. The desired outcome is progressing faster to jumps and becoming a better rider. This sequence is the structure of a video ad.

Video ad sequence. Use these seven steps:

  1. State the problem the viewer recognizes.
  2. Agitate it by attaching a feeling to it, such as tiredness, frustration or fear of falling behind.
  3. Present the product as the solution, stressing the instant benefit and low effort.
  4. Turn the benefit into a desired outcome.
  5. Amplify (make bigger) that outcome with what it says about the viewer, such as looking sharp or standing out.
  6. Add a social proof element (a review, a number of buyers, or another sign of approval).
  7. End with a call to action.

When to use it. PAS fits products that solve a real problem. For a nice-to-have product (useful, but not urgent) with no strong problem, skip the agitation and go straight to the solution. Make the benefit clearly better than the alternatives.

Ad layout sequence

1 min

A full ad layout follows this order:

  1. Hook (the first line that grabs attention).
  2. Educational statement (a fact that teaches the reader something).
  3. Curiosity line (a line that leaves a question open).
  4. Product introduction with the benefit.
  5. How to use the product, demonstrating how it solves the problem.
  6. Social proof with agitation of the problem.
  7. Call to action with social proof.

Demonstrating the product's use is important because it shows how the product solves the problem.

Examples.

  • Pet hairball supplement: Cat owners, never ignore these three signs of hairballs. Hairballs can mean digestion is off, and most owners miss them. Our daily chew helps your cat pass hair smoothly. Give one soft chew a day with food. Join thousands of cat owners seeing fewer hairballs. Learn more below.
  • Wrinkle serum: Skin lovers, never ignore these three signs of early fine lines. Fine lines form when skin loses moisture, and most people miss them until later. Our night serum restores hydration with one pump each evening. Our customers report smoother skin within two weeks. Shop now and join thousands of happy customers.

Problem-solving ad script

1 min

Scripting procedure. A script is the spoken or written text for a video ad.

  1. Fill in the problem and the emotion attached to it.
  2. Describe the feature and the benefit it creates, then the result of that benefit.
  3. Keep asking what each benefit means until a deep outcome (the big reason underneath) is reached, such as a clean home, which leads to comfort and having people over.
  4. Add the avoid-negative-outcome angle (focus on the loss the buyer avoids), since people are more driven away from pain than towards pleasure.
  5. Add a testimonial (a customer quote) and a call to action.
  6. Turn the outline into a full script and plan the shot (one camera scene) for each line.

Fill-in outline. Complete every line before turning the outline into the script. An outline is a short plan:

Hook: [target audience] + [problem, fact or curiosity line]
Problem: [what the customer sees or experiences every day]
Agitate: [how the problem gets worse over time] + [emotion it creates, e.g. embarrassment, anxiety, low energy]
Solution: [product name] + [bold claim or unique selling point]
Features: [feature 1], [feature 2]
Benefit ladder: [feature] -> what does that mean? -> [result] -> what does that mean? -> [deeper outcome, e.g. comfort, having people over]
Avoid negative outcome: [what happens if the customer does nothing]
Social proof: [testimonial or review line]
Call to action: [clear action, e.g. Shop now, Buy now, Click here, Learn more]

Further details

1 min
  • Ad copy formula for ecommerce (writing for an online shop): (1) Identify and confirm the problem your target audience faces. (2) Agitate the problem slightly to increase attention. (3) Present the solution your product provides. (4) Explain benefits of clicking the ad, particularly using your unique mechanism (the specific way your product works that sets it apart). (5) For Facebook ads with extended space, add text-based customer reviews as quotes. (6) Confirm a clear call-to-action.
  • Pattern for a problem-agitate-solution ad: name the problem the buyer already knows, make it worse by showing the consequence, then present the solution. Example: 'Tired of guests seeing pet hair on your sofa? Every visit feels like a cleaning test. This brush clears the hair in minutes.' Second example: 'Still buying lint roller paper every week? Each roll runs out in days and costs more than you think. Sweep up the hair and keep going.'
