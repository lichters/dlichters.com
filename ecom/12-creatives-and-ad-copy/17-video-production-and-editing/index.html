Video production and editing

Section 12.17 · 17 of 26 in this chapter · 38 min read

In short

  • A modern smartphone is enough to film ads. Learn lighting and camera moves first, and buy gear later.
  • Light matters more than the camera. Film next to a window or under a cheap lamp, not in direct sun.
  • Keep the picture at least 1080p (full HD). Use 4K only if your phone already offers it.
  • Write the script first, then film, then cut, then add text last.
  • Put a transition wherever the scene or subject changes, and match its type to the camera movement.
More key points
  • Zooms and jump cuts grab attention. Use them through the whole ad, not only at the start.
  • Keep background music quiet under the voice, and fade it out at the end.
  • Use the same wording on every platform, but match the text style to each platform.
  • Video quality matters most. Film, edit and test before you buy more gear.

Phone filming: camera and resolution

2 min
  • A modern smartphone is enough to film Facebook ad video. Learn the basics of lighting, camera movement and shot technique from free online tutorials or a low-cost course of about $50 before you hire someone to produce it.
  • A recent smartphone with a 4K camera (4K is a very sharp video size, about 4,000 pixels wide) can produce product footage good enough for organic (unpaid posts) and paid video. Shoot on the phone and edit on a computer. Expensive cameras are not needed at the start.
  • TikTok compresses uploads (squeezes the file, which lowers quality a little) to at least 1080p (full HD, a picture 1080 pixels tall). A back camera with good light is enough. The back camera is the rear lens, better than the front selfie lens. 4K is not needed for phone uploads.
  • Video must be at least 1080p, with 4K preferred. Older advice: do not overthink production. A modern smartphone can produce video as good as a DSLR (a large professional camera) or a lower-end video camera. The video does not need depth or movement to work as an ad.
  • Both rules apply: 1080p is the floor (the minimum) in both sources. Use 4K if the phone offers it, but do not pay for it.
  • an iPhone X records 4K at up to 60 fps (fps means frames per second, the number of pictures shown each second). That is enough for product shoots, unboxings (filming the opening of a package) and talking-head videos (a person speaking to camera). A mid-range (mid-priced) recent smartphone, used in 2020, is a sufficient starting camera.
  • The main difference between a smartphone and a dedicated video camera (a camera made only for video) is the lens (the glass that focuses the light). Telephoto and other interchangeable lenses (lenses you can swap) can cost a lot on their own. Current phones already produce high-quality video.
  • about 80% of the instructor's own footage was shot on a phone. The footage (raw video) was moved from the phone to Google Drive (online storage), then downloaded to the computer for editing.

Lighting

1 min
  • Lighting has the biggest effect on video quality.
  • Shoot indoors with artificial light (lamps) or next to a window. Do not shoot outdoors in direct sunlight.
  • Direct sun produced weaker quality even on an older phone.
  • Light matters more than the camera. Control the light on the subject (the person or product being filmed) and let the phone camera handle the rest.
  • Light sources and softening:
    • A ring light, clip-on light or phone light adds light to the subject.
    • A diffuser (a soft cover that spreads the light, also called an umbrella filter) over a light softens it so it is less harsh.
    • If your body blocks daylight, add an extra light source.
  • Do not rely on the back camera's low quality when the light is poor. The light setup decides the result more than the camera.
  • dim lighting turns viewers off quickly. Natural daylight in a room is the best light source, but avoid direct sunlight. Without enough natural light, use affordable artificial lights.
  • for a simple talking-head ad, place the speaker so daylight from a window lights the face. Natural light was enough for good results without a studio. Avoid large or expensive lighting kits as a starting point.
  • In the smartphone UGC workflow (UGC means user-generated content, filmed like a real customer rather than a studio ad), set up lighting first: a ring light or a cheap LED light (an energy-saving light), plus daylight from a window.

Production workflows

1 min

Choose the workflow that matches the type of video.

Smartphone UGC production, in order

1 min
  1. Know the product, its unique features (what makes it different), its benefits (what it does for the buyer) and the audience (who buys it).
  2. Write the script covering each feature and benefit.
  3. Set up lighting first (a ring light or a cheap LED light, plus daylight from a window).
  4. Film the A-roll (the talking-to-camera voiceover, the main speaking footage) first, using the teleprompter app (an app that shows your script while you speak) if needed.
  5. Film the product showcase shots: hook (the opening shot that grabs attention), pan (a slow side-to-side camera move), zoom (moving closer) and parallax (moving around the product so the background shifts).
  6. Film the feature and benefit footage that matches each script line.
  7. Film context and lifestyle shots: establishing shots (wide opening shots of the place), props (objects that fill the scene), and people using the product.
  8. Assemble the footage over the A-roll audio in an editor such as CapCut (a video editing app).

Short footage-led ad, in order

1 min
  1. Write the script first and decide what each line says.
  2. Find footage that matches each line of the script.
  3. Cut the footage to the script with plain cuts and remove dead time.
  4. Add text overlays (words placed on top of the video) that support the key benefit.
  5. Add transitions (the change from one clip to the next) only after the story works.
  6. Add sound effects, music and polish last.

Presenter ad with A-roll and B-roll, in order

1 min

Plain terms: A-roll is the main speaking footage. B-roll is supporting footage shown over it.

  1. Pick the background music and the mood first, since the atmosphere (the feel of the video) shapes the rest of the edit.
  2. Write the script and decide the main talking or presenting footage (the A-roll).
  3. Cut the main footage into the final story order and remove every mistake, pause and filler word (such as "um" and "ah").
  4. Lay supporting footage (B-roll) over the main track (the main video layer), and only then add product demonstrations and close-ups.
  5. Add the music bed (background music under the speech), set its volume and fade it out (slowly lower it to silence) at the end.
  6. Generate auto captions (automatic subtitles from the speech), delete the unwanted ones and style the remaining text.
  7. Add transitions, animations, effects, sound effects and keyframe motion (movement set by markers at points in time) last.
  8. Check that all text and graphics stay inside the safe zone (the area of the frame that stays visible) for each placement (the spot where the ad appears).

Note: the footage-led ad and the presenter ad order the work differently (music first in the presenter ad). Use the footage-led order when the ad is built from found footage. Use the presenter order when a person speaks to camera.

Principles for the order of work

1 min
  • Decide the order on purpose. Script and footage decide the ad. Text comes after cutting. Effects come last.
  • Adding text before the cut is final wastes time, because wording and timing will still change.
  • Place the main talking footage on the main track and put supporting product footage above it, so the spoken story stays easy to follow.
  • Record the A-roll first, then match each script line to footage, so you know exactly where each scene belongs.
  • Footage can be layered over the voiceover later.
  • A-roll can be faceless (no face on camera). The voiceover can be recorded by you, a script writer or an AI voice (a computer-generated voice).
  • The script must be recorded before the B-roll is filmed.
  • Start uploading right away. While waiting for equipment such as a ring light, upload drafts and experiment with phone footage. Consistent uploads are needed to learn what performs (which ads work).
  • UGC-style video can look rougher than polished brand content and still work for ads.
  • The core requirement is that the shots clearly show what the product is, its features and its benefits.
  • Beginners can start with this rougher style. High polish is optional.

Traditional TikTok-style ad from a reference video

1 min
  1. Edit the video in Splice (a mobile video editing app).
  2. Screen-record the on-screen text and speech of a reference TikTok video (an example video you copy from).
  3. Add matching text and only non-copyrighted sounds (sounds you have the right to use) in Splice.
  4. Export the video to the computer (save the finished file).
  5. Upload it in TikTok Ads Manager (TikTok's ad tool) and run it as a traditional ad.

Shot list and footage

3 min
  • Write a shot list (a plan of each shot) before filming that matches the story the ad tells.
  • Film several angles (camera positions) of the product being made and of the product in use.
  • Film more angles than you think you need: a static shot (the camera does not move), a slide or pan across a surface, and slow zooms.
  • Keep the product centred and the camera level, so it does not look skewed (tilted) when cut in as B-roll.
  • Main shot types for UGC and ads:
    • Product showcase.
    • Close-up (a tight shot of one detail) when discussing a specific feature.
    • Use-case shot: the product in action.
  • Shot types and their effect:
    • A wide shot shows the setting and context.
    • A close-up isolates a detail or shows facial emotion.
    • Upward-angled cowboy shots (framed from about mid-thigh up) make a subject look more powerful. Downward-angled shots make it look smaller.
  • B-roll is secondary footage layered over the main narrative to support it, for example a product being made while the presenter talks.
  • Text and shapes can sit on the upper layers too.
  • Establishing shot: open with a wider shot of the setting (for example the bath) before zooming in on the product, so the viewer understands the context.
  • Props (objects in the scene) should fit the product's context (for example rubber ducks in the bath).
  • Avoid props that distract from the product, such as bath bombs (fizzing bath tablets) that create foam or colour.
  • Test the product's claims on camera, such as waterproofing, by actually doing it.
  • Then film that test as a proof shot (a shot that shows the claim is true).
  • To show built-in lights:
    1. Film the product lit in a normal room.
    2. Switch off the room lights and film the product glowing in the dark.
    3. Cycle through each light mode as its own shot. Keep the phone steady and use a slow zoom or parallax move for each state.
  • To prove a connectivity claim (a claim that the product links to a device), such as Bluetooth pairing (wireless linking with another device):
    1. Start a screen recording on the phone.
    2. Pull down the quick-settings panel (Android) or Control Centre (the iPhone's quick-access panel).
    3. Open the device connection menu while the product connects.
    4. Add this clip next to a close-up of the product's pairing mode (the mode it uses to link).
  • For a floating product, film a movement shot by following it across the water surface at the same speed as the product drifts.
  • This shows buoyancy (the ability to float) in motion rather than in a static frame.
  • If the home has no suitable location, film in a public place such as a shop aisle, handling the product the way a customer would.
  • For water products, you can also film at a pool or beach. Shoot when the location is quiet so the shot stays clean.
  • Supplier warehouse footage: filming in the supplier's warehouse (the storage building of the company that sends the goods) makes a small brand look larger.
  • It also differentiates the brand (sets it apart) from competitors selling the same product.
  • Owner-filmed content (video filmed by the business owner) shows the business as real and established.
  • Demonstrations work for physical products: live product demonstrations, how-to tutorials and entertaining presentations.
  • Video is effective for showing benefits and differentiating from competitors (setting yourself apart), even where it is under-tested in some categories.
  • ASMR-style close-ups (soft, close sounds and views, such as tapping or pouring) of products, such as bottle caps or drops, are popular on TikTok on a small budget.
  • 10 to 15 second videos are also popular.
  • When a product must be shown in use but you do not want to appear on camera, hide your face with a mask, film only the body or the back of the head, or use a full costume.
  • Video backgrounds strongly affect conversion (the share of viewers who buy).
  • A clean, aesthetic (pleasing to look at), branded (showing your brand) background looks more professional than a plain wall. It can make the same product convert noticeably better.
  • Use better backgrounds when starting out, even if the product is simple.
  • Check the background before uploading. A messy or busy background, such as a checkered red-and-white pattern, made a video look old and interfered with the camera.
  • Use a clean, plain setup.

Camera moves and framing

1 min
  • Turn on the 3x3 grid (nine equal boxes drawn over the screen) in the smartphone camera settings to frame shots.
  • If the grid is unavailable, keep the product in the exact centre of the frame, because the camera is sharpest at the centre.
  • Do not hide the main features of the product or leave it off-centre.
  • Off-centre footage loses sharpness and clarity on the key feature.
  • Choose the aspect ratio (the shape of the picture, width to height) by platform before filming.
  • Use square or 4:5 for Facebook feed ads (the main scrolling list). Use vertical portrait for TikTok and Instagram Stories (full-screen vertical posts).
  • Leave enough space in the frame for text and overlays.

Focus lock

1 min
  1. Press and hold the screen on the product until the focus lock appears (a marker showing focus is fixed).
  2. Move the camera without the focus shifting.

Pan shot with a towel slider

1 min
  1. Place the phone on a towel laid across a surface so it can slide smoothly without sticking.
  2. Tap and hold the screen on the product to lock focus.
  3. Slowly drag the phone across the product in one fluid movement.

Level pan

1 min
  1. Turn the phone upside down on a flat surface.
  2. Slide it along the base (the flat surface it rests on), keeping the lens low so the product is filmed straight-on at a consistent height.

Slow zoom

1 min
  1. Keep the product centred in the 3x3 grid.
  2. Zoom in slowly on the product while holding the camera steady.

Parallax shot

1 min
  1. Walk slowly around the product with the feet placed wide and the body braced (held firm).
  2. Keep the product centred in frame as you move.
  3. Practise before the final take. It is the hardest shot to keep stable.

Reveal technique

1 min
  1. Open the clip with the presenter reacting to the product close to the lens.
  2. Move the phone or body out of the frame to reveal the product as a transition.
  3. Alternatively, start the reveal in darkness and move into light as the product comes into view.

Common framing and movement mistakes

1 min
  • Avoid sloppy handheld pans and zooms (moves made by holding the phone in the hand).
  • Move slowly, lock focus first, and use a support (a towel or the phone base) to keep movement fluid (smooth).

A-roll and script recording

1 min

A-roll recording

1 min
  1. Set the phone to the back camera (the rear lens) with good light and frame yourself in the shot, or flip to the front camera (the selfie lens).
  2. The back camera is lower quality but usable when the light is good.
  3. Use a teleprompter app to read the script.
  4. Record the full voiceover.
  5. Write down which footage should go over each line of the script.
  • For scripted videos, write the full script word for word beforehand.
  • Read it while recording, looking at the script and then at the camera.
  • The editor cuts out the glances at the script.
  • The cutting also removes hesitations and filler words such as "um" and "ah".
  • Teleprompter options:
    • A free online teleprompter on a computer or tablet screen, placed right next to the camera, costs nothing.
    • A paid teleprompter attached to the camera, or an iPad held near the lens, are other options.
  • Record narration separately when the person does not need to appear on camera, so the audio can be combined with images or other video later.
  • Reading the script or blog post aloud works well for this.
  • Mistakes on camera are normal and should not stop a UGC shoot.
  • They are removed in the edit.

Cutting and trimming

1 min

Cutting the A-roll

1 min
  1. Drag the footage onto the timeline (the bar where clips are placed in order) and find the start of the speech.
  2. Use B to cut at each unwanted word, pause, "um" or "ah". B and A are keyboard keys. Then use A to select the unwanted segment and delete it with Delete or Backspace.
  3. Trim clip edges by dragging their sides to shorten them.
  4. Go through the whole script, then play the result to check the flow (how smoothly it runs).

Trim and cut clips

1 min
  1. Drag the start or end of a clip to shorten it.
  2. Use the razor tool (a tool that splits a clip in two) to cut out a section and delete it.
  3. Hold Shift (a keyboard key) and click several clips to select them, then move them together.
  4. Close any gap left behind by deleting a section so the cut flows.
  • Cut B-roll to fit the spoken words. Each B-roll clip must be cut so it does not carry unwanted audio.
  • UGC does not need high polish, but a few clean product shots (for example slow pans, zooms and close-ups) make the ad look credible (believable).
  • Cut dead footage that repeats what viewers already saw.
  • In one example, a 9 to 10 second pouring segment was removed because it was too long.
  • Shorten a decent video by trimming dull moments, removing unnecessary frames or speeding up sections.
  • This is a valid way to repurpose it (use it again in a new form).
  • Change the clip roughly every 3 seconds to keep the middle of the video fast-paced, so viewers do not lose interest.
  • The middle carries the education, solution, benefits and features.
  • On-screen text should animate in and out quickly (appear and disappear with motion), so viewers cannot read it all in one pass and must replay the video.
  • This pushes rewatches (people watching again), but only works when the story itself is compelling.
  • Before overlaying B-roll (placing it on top of the main clip), lower the clip's own volume to zero in the Tools panel.
  • Sound recorded with a shot, such as a phone scratching across a desk, would otherwise play under the ad.
  • Mistake: dragging a clip onto a higher track in CapCut pushes the clips below it along the timeline, which shifts the whole edit.
  • Duplicate the clip onto the upper track and trim the copy instead, so the main timeline stays in place.

Captions and on-screen text

1 min

Safe zones and placement

1 min
  • Canva (a design app) safe zone for a 4:5 video ad: set up the 4:5 ratio at 1080 × 1350 px (pixels).
  • Place critical text, such as the offer, in the safe zone where Facebook will not crop it.
  • Stories show text at the top and bottom. The main feed may cut off bottom text. Design accordingly.
  • Source conflict (two sources disagree): the Canva note gives 1350 × 1920 px for 4:5. That frame is not 4:5 (1350 ÷ 1920 ≈ 0.70, while 4:5 = 0.80).
  • Use 1080 px wide × 1350 px tall for 4:5, the vertical size that matches the ratio.
  • Use 4:5 (1080 × 1350 px, vertical) because the feed is viewed mainly on mobile and 4:5 fills more of the screen.
  • Mobile viewing drives most sales for social video ads, so design creatives (ad designs) for the phone screen first.
  • The source claims about 90% of sales come from mobile.
  • Create two versions of each video: one format for the Facebook and Instagram feed, and a separate one for Reels (short vertical videos) and Stories.
  • The two versions are needed because the two placements display content differently.
  • Test video ad dimensions and aspect ratios.
  • Some videos may appear differently sized than expected, which affects ad performance and reach (how many people see it).
  • Place the on-screen call-to-action text (the words that ask viewers to act) high enough in the video so it stays visible.
  • It must not be hidden by platform UI elements (the buttons and labels the app adds on top). A low position was noted as a weakness in the example.

Why captions matter

1 min
  • Feed viewers often watch without sound, so the caption carries much of the message.
  • Put a benefit-driven line (a line about what the buyer gains) of 4-5 words on screen for each clip, with an emotional trigger (a word that stirs feeling).
  • Viewers are often distracted, for example waiting in line or in a crowded place, so messages must be understood quickly.
  • Add subtitles, because many viewers cannot hear the sound.
  • A person scrolling while queuing in a store has split attention and may have no sound on.
  • Design the creative to work silently, for example with subtitles on videos.

Caption tools and steps

1 min
  • Auto captions in CapCut (desktop and mobile) create a text layer (a separate text strip over the video) from the spoken words.
  • The text can then be edited or deleted. Generate captions only after the voiceover is final, otherwise you will have to regenerate them (make them again).
  • Auto captions in CapCut:
    1. Select all clips with the voice track (the audio layer with speech).
    2. Go to Text and click Auto captions at the top.
    3. Check the caption language setting, since the auto-detected language can be wrong.
    4. Click Create.
    5. Review the captions track above the timeline and correct any misheard words in the captions list.
    6. Use the captions list to split or merge (join) caption lines and adjust their length.
  • Vide.io (an online video tool): upload the video, open the subtitles function and let it generate the text automatically.
  • Then choose the subtitle style (plain or in a box), set the size and select the font.
  • On CapCut mobile, the auto-caption step asks which caption animation to apply.
  • The Pro list (the paid list) includes an animated caption background that changes colour as each word is spoken.
  • This was popular for TikTok-style captions at the time, but it may not suit every ad.
  • The desktop version did not offer it during the demo.

Caption styling

2 min
  • Style captions in the text panel: add strokes (outlines around letters), add a background, and align text left, middle or right.
  • For a TikTok-style look, use a rounded rectangle background (a box with round corners) and increase its height and width for padding (the space inside the box around the text).
  • Caption styling in CapCut, under Text > Basic:
    • Use a readable font. The system font is recommended for UGC.
    • Set the text colour.
    • Either add a stroke for a simple TikTok or Instagram look, or switch on the background, set it to a rounded rectangle with white fill and black text, and increase the height and width to round the corners.
    • Tick Apply to all (a setting that changes every text layer at once), then increase the line height (the space between lines) if it looks cramped.
  • Every text layer on video needs a background, a stroke or a drop shadow (a dark shadow behind the text).
  • Light and dark areas of the footage can make plain white text disappear.
  • Glow (a soft light halo around text) and drop shadow are other options in the text settings.
  • Text over video needs readability treatment (ways to make text easy to read).
  • On a video with white text, place a gradient (a smooth fade from one colour to another) behind it or add a black stroke.
  • On TikTok, the native text style (the built-in default) uses a black stroke over white text.
  • Avoid CapCut's template text styles (ready-made text looks) for UGC.
  • They look overdone and not like user-generated content.
  • To style one caption differently, untick Apply to all in the caption settings.
  • Then move, resize or restyle that caption independently.
  • Text animation duration is set under animation at the bottom of the timer (the time bar for the text).
  • A value of 0.4 seconds gives a quick entrance for short-form ads (short videos).
  • Use the same short duration across lines so the rhythm (the timing pattern) stays consistent.
  • Split a long claim into several short text lines, stacked (one above the other) or staggered (offset), so each benefit appears on its own.
  • For TikTok, keep text short and punchy. On Facebook you can use more varied title styles.
  • When text layers are stacked and move together, untick the Apply to all checkbox so each layer can be moved independently.
  • Once unlinked, each line can be positioned and timed on its own.
  • To give different lines of one multi-line text layer different colours in CapCut, select the specific line in the text layer.
  • Then change its colour in the text section of the control panel. Each line can be styled separately without creating a new layer.
  • Duplicate a styled text layer with Ctrl/Cmd+C and Ctrl/Cmd+V (copy and paste; Ctrl on Windows, Cmd on Mac). This keeps the same font, stroke and size.
  • Then edit only the words. This is faster than styling each bullet from scratch.
  • Blinking text effect: a blinking text effect can lift CTR (click-through rate, the share of viewers who click), especially for seasonal promotions such as Black Friday.
  • It is a small change, but creative is a major factor in results.

Benefit overlays and bullet graphics

1 min
  • Benefit list overlay: while the speaker names a product's benefits, short text lines appear on top of B-roll one after another, in sync with the words.
  • Formula: spoken claim, then one benefit per line, timed to the moment each benefit is said.
    • Example 1: "Calms the mind and helps you unwind" appears while the speaker says "calm evenings".
    • Example 2: "Lasts up to 60 hours" appears while the speaker says "long-lasting burn time".

Bullet-point graphic

1 min
  1. Create a text layer for each benefit with the same style.
  2. Pick a checkmark from the Stickers tab (a menu of ready-made graphics) or download a free check icon, and place it beside the text.
  3. Position all items so they fit together on screen.
  4. Select the group and drag each line onto the timeline at the moment the speaker mentions that benefit.
  5. Resize the items so they are legible (easy to read) on a phone.
  • Add icons, ticks, arrows and emojis next to claims to emphasise them.
  • Make them appear at the same time as the text they support.
  • Keep these graphics smaller than the main text so they do not compete with it.

Transitions, zooms and attention cuts

4 min

Transitions

  • A transition is an effect that joins two cuts. A cut is the point where one shot ends and the next begins. Transitions are built into CapCut (a free video editing app) and can be added between two cuts. A zoom or pull-in suits an introduction of the product. A duration of about 1.2 seconds was used as a starting point. Drag the transition onto the cut point and adjust the duration in the control panel.
  • Adding a transition between two clips in CapCut. A clip is one piece of video on the timeline (the editing strip):
    1. Transitions can only go between two clips, so duplicate the clip and place the copy on a track above. A track is a row on the timeline.
    2. Trim the top copy so the cut lines up with the transition point.
    3. Open the Transitions tab and drag a transition, such as Slide down, between the two clips.
    4. Preview the transition to confirm it works.
  • Use a transition every time the scene or subject changes, so the viewer stays engaged. Effects such as colour overlays (a coloured layer over the footage), graphics and filters are for style. Use them sparingly.
  • Use transitions sparingly and match them to camera movement. A pan is a sideways camera move. Pan or slide when the camera moves sideways, and zoom when the shot pushes in. Panning and zooming transitions are the most useful. Overusing transitions makes the ad look chaotic.
  • Taken together: add a transition where a scene or subject changes, and choose its type to match the camera movement. Do not add transitions where nothing changes. Keep colour overlays and filters to a minimum.
  • Clip animation brings a clip in, out or both. It is found in the video controls and uses options such as swing or slide. Avoid slide animations that bring in a black background, because they look unpolished on a product ad.

Attention cuts and zooms

  • Zoom into the subject with a keyframe. A keyframe is a marker that saves a setting at one moment. Scale is the size of the clip.
    1. Select the clip you want to zoom into.
    2. Move the playhead (the line that shows the current frame) to where the zoom starts. Click the keyframe button next to Scale at normal size.
    3. Move the playhead to the frame where the zoom ends.
    4. Increase Scale to the target size, which creates a second keyframe.
    5. Play the section to check that the zoom moves smoothly between the two keyframes.
  • Cut-in zoom: split the clip with the blade (the split tool) at the moment of the action, for example a wink. Select the second part, go to Video > Scale and increase the scale so the shot zooms in at that moment. Pair the zoom with a short sound effect, like a ding, to add emphasis.
  • Keyframe zoom in CapCut:
    1. Select the clip and open the scale property in the Tools panel.
    2. Click the keyframe diamond to set a keyframe at the start.
    3. Move the playhead to a later point.
    4. Change the scale, which creates a keyframe automatically.
    5. Drag keyframes to adjust timing.
    • A slow zoom over the whole clip builds engagement. A fast zoom in the first seconds grabs attention.
  • Jump cut (a cut that skips a little time): cut with the blade just before a small action, such as a wink. Then scale the new clip in so the eye line stays where it was. The eye line is where the person is looking. Pair the jump cut with a short sound effect at the moment of the cut to grab attention.
  • The two main attention cuts are a slow zoom into the subject and a quick jump cut. Both can be layered with sound effects. Use them across the whole ad, not only at the start.
  • Cuts, zooms and motion keep attention and are used throughout the ad. Zoom in during speech to add emphasis. Zoom out to make a cut out. Both help retention, which means how long people keep watching.
  • Cut types:
    • A straight cut joins two shots back to back. It is the main cut for basic ads.
    • A jump cut skips time or zooms in between shots.
    • A J-cut starts the next shot's audio before its picture. The sound leads.
    • An L-cut keeps the previous shot's audio playing after the cut. The old sound carries on.

Motion, easing and blur

  • Things in motion speed up and slow down. A linear move, one at the same speed all the way, looks mechanical. Apply Ease Out (a smooth slow-down) to the first keyframe and Ease In (a smooth speed-up) to the second. You can also use a custom speed graph. This makes motion look natural.
  • Motion blur on a fast zoom: increase the shutter angle in the motion blur settings. The shutter angle sets how much blur you get. A high shutter angle creates visible blur during quick movement, which makes the motion look more natural.
  • Motion blur and easing make movement look natural. Without motion blur, fast-moving objects look harsh. At the time of this demo, the desktop version did not offer motion blur. Check whether your version has it before you plan moving text.
  • Any property with a keyframe diamond can be animated over time. This includes scale, position and colour. Position keyframes can move a product or a hand in from off screen. Text can be animated the same way.

Overlays, cut-outs and compositing

2 min

Compositing means combining layers into one picture.

  • Before placing an emoji or graphic over footage, check that its PNG (an image file type that can be see-through) has a transparent background. A solid background covers the video underneath. If it does, find another PNG or remove the background before placing it.
  • Arrows and graphics are found in the Stickers tab. Drag them onto the timeline. Move, resize and rotate them with the rotation handle in the Tools panel. Add position keyframes to slide them in from off screen.
  • Chroma key removes one colour from a clip. Use the chroma key option in the Cut out panel to pick a colour. This is how green-screen footage is cleared. Auto cut out can isolate a person from the background, so the person can be placed in front of an overlay.
  • Removing the background with auto cut-out:
    1. Duplicate the clip with the person on the timeline.
    2. Open the Tools panel, go to Cut out and choose Auto cut out.
    3. Wait for processing to finish. The person is now isolated from the background.
    4. Place graphics such as wings behind the cut-out layer, so they appear behind the person.
  • Chroma key on green-screen footage:
    1. Place the green-screen clip on the timeline above the background.
    2. Open Cut out and choose Chroma key.
    3. Use the colour picker to select the green background.
    4. Raise the strength of the chroma key until all green is gone.
    5. Turn off any shadow so the cut-out subject blends cleanly.
  • Blend mode overlays. A blend mode sets how a layer mixes with the layer under it:
    • Multiply keeps the dark parts of the overlay and removes the light parts.
    • Screen (or brighten) keeps the light parts and removes the dark parts.
    • Soft light is a gentle overlay. It blends an effect into the footage without overpowering it.
    • Example 1: a soft light glow over a candle video.
    • Example 2: a multiply texture over a dark background.
  • Animated social proof workflow. Social proof means pop-up comments or testimonials (reviews shown on screen):
    1. Design the static graphics, such as comment cards or testimonial frames, in Canva (an online design app).
    2. Export them as image files.
    3. Import the images into a video editor and animate them. Add pop sound effects and motion.
  • CapCut is a quick editor for animated social proof, because it includes built-in pop sound effects.

Sound design and music

4 min

Background music

  • Set the music volume low enough that the voiceover is clearly audible. A starting point of 12 to 16 in the volume control of the panel worked in the example. Then adjust by ear. Music should sit in the background and create atmosphere.
  • Background music setup in CapCut:
    1. Go to Audio and search a mood keyword, a word that describes the feeling, such as "lo-fi".
    2. Drag the track onto the timeline and trim it so it starts at the beginning.
    3. Lower its volume in the Tools panel so the voice is clearly audible.
    4. Pick music that fits the product's mood, for example calm lo-fi for home decor.
  • Pick the track that fits the mood of the ad, not the most popular one. Envato Elements is a stock library (a site of ready-made media). It provides stock video, music and sound effects. You search it by keyword, for example "home decor music".
  • Fading music: select the music clip. Drag the small circle at the clip's start or end to fade the music in or out. Fade it out at the end of the ad.
  • Auto fade out: use the auto fade out switch on the audio track. The music then fades slowly at the end instead of stopping abruptly. The fade belongs at the end of the ad, after the closing call to action, the line that tells viewers what to do.
  • Polished background music in a short product video can make a simple, low-cost product feel more premium. A minimal video with a refined soundtrack can look more premium than a busy, text-heavy ad.

Sound effects and cues

  • Sound effects for transitions and emphasis: open Audio > Sound effects. Pick a sound such as a whoosh (a swoosh sound) for a transition, or a ding (a short bell sound) for a wink. Drag it onto an audio track aligned with the action. Lower the volume so it is subtle.
  • Use sound effects like whooshes to support transitions. Align them with the cut, and turn them down so they stay in the background. A sound effect should never be the main element of the piece.
  • Sound design has two purposes:
    • Ambient sounds match what is on screen, for example train noise for a train.
    • Stylistic cues are tied to a visual moment, for example a ding on a wink or a whoosh on a transition.
    • Stylistic cues are in CapCut's sound library at no extra cost.
  • Audio cues that fall on the same beat as a visual cut make the edit feel intentional. Layer cues sparingly, so they guide attention rather than distract.
  • Shocking visuals, animations or sounds can hold attention. Use them only when they fit the brand and the audience. Test them against a plain version before committing.
  • Ads should avoid silence. Continuous sound and visual movement hold attention, unless a pause is used on purpose for effect.

Trending and recreated sounds

  • Use trending sounds (popular audio many people are using now) and seasonal trends to attract viewers. Only do this if they fit the product. Examples mentioned: a major sports event that was still running, and the US holiday travel trend around Christmas and New Year.
  • Viewers must be watching or listening at every moment. Text-to-speech (a computer voice that reads text aloud) over a strong satisfying sound distracts from the sound. Use plain on-screen text there instead.
  • For a satisfying product sound, use plain text instead of text-to-speech, so viewers can hear the sound. For an ordinary sound, text-to-speech and on-screen text can run together.
  • Keep the original trending sound at a very low level, around 5. Viewers then hear the recreated sound and the environment, rather than the trending track.
  • Recreated sounds usually sound better than originals, because you control the recording. Re-record a sound and layer it in place of the original. Layering means placing one sound on top of another.
  • Find a trending sound on the For You page (the main feed of suggested videos):
    1. When a sound plays on the For You page, tap the sound circle in the bottom right corner.
    2. Note how many videos already use the sound.
    3. Open the original video. If there is no original, open the most popular video using the sound.
    4. Check when the sound was first used.
    5. Look at view counts of other recent videos that use the sound.
    6. Use the sound only if recent videos using it still get large views. Skip sounds that are about six months old, or whose videos are old.
  • Recreate a product sound and raise its volume in Splice (a video and audio editing app):
    1. Record the real product sound on a phone with the Voice Memos app. Use a second cup and water if needed.
    2. End the recording, then share the audio to Splice.
    3. In Splice, tap Audio, then Music, then import the recording.
    4. Detach the audio from the video at the point where the sound should play.
    5. Split the audio where it starts and where it ends.
    6. Open Volume and raise the level. Volume starts at 100. Set it to about 180 to 200, so it plays louder without distorting. Distorting means crackly or fuzzy sound.
    7. Review the result. Keep the recreated sound on the cue point, the moment it should play.

Editing tools

1 min

CapCut

  • CapCut is a video editing app good enough for beginners to make their own ad videos. Learning to make creatives (ad videos and images) yourself also lets you brief outside creators more precisely.
  • CapCut is the editor used to cut TikTok Shop mentions, Orange Cart mentions and non-matching offers out of a copied video.
  • Edit simple problem-and-solution ads in CapCut. Use existing footage from AliExpress or YouTube, arranged as a short story: problem first, then solution.
  • Editing for UGC-style content can be done with free tools such as CapCut. UGC means user-generated content, videos made by everyday people. CapCut allows text overlays and short-form edits. A voice-over (a spoken narration added over footage) can be added to filmed product footage.

Other tools

  • Splice is used for the traditional TikTok ad workflow above, and for recreating product sounds.
  • Final Cut Pro (a professional video editor for Mac) can make a TikTok version:
    1. Open the project settings and change the format from YouTube to TikTok.
    2. Move the character to the left, so the speaker stays visible in the vertical frame (the tall, phone-shaped picture) before exporting, which means saving the finished video file.
  • Opus Clip (an AI tool) can cut one long customer video into several shorter clips. It can also add an AI voiceover, a computer-made voice. Use it to turn existing UGC into many ad variations for split testing, which means showing different versions to see which works best.
  • Basic editing skills, such as cropping out unwanted text and adding simple transitions, are enough to repurpose (reuse) footage for ads. Advanced production is not required to start testing.
  • Freelance editing: if editing is needed, send the footage and a shot list (a list of planned scenes) to a freelance video editor, an independent editor you hire. Find one on Fiverr or Upwork, which are freelancer marketplaces. A story and footage must exist before you outsource the edit.

Repurposing across platforms

2 min
  • Repurposing (reusing a video) can mean re-uploading an identical video on the same platform. It can also mean moving content from TikTok to Instagram Reels or YouTube Shorts, to reach more audiences.
  • Repurposed content is valuable across platforms. A strong TikTok video can be reused as an Instagram Reel, and a successful Reel can be reposted. Use this to capture more reach from one production effort.
  • Reuse winning TikTok videos on Facebook in the same format. Or have an editor cut them into Facebook formats. Test organic TikTok first, meaning posts that are not paid for. Then use the video that goes viral, meaning it spreads very fast, for Facebook ads.
  • Adapt each video to its platform in format and tone. Trends and jokes that work on TikTok may not work on Facebook Reels, which has an older audience.
  • Compilation (one short clip made from the best moments of several videos):
    1. Pick three videos that performed well.
    2. Extract only their highlights.
    3. Combine them into one clip of about 10 to 20 seconds. For example, three 3-second highlights make a 9-second video.
  • Cross-platform workflow:
    1. Edit the video once in a mobile editor such as InShot.
    2. Upload it to Instagram. Add the overlay text (text placed on top of the video) using Instagram's own text tool, with the platform's font.
    3. Upload the same video to TikTok. Use TikTok's text tool and font for the overlay, and keep the same thumbnail.
    4. Upload to YouTube Shorts. Use YouTube's own text tool and font for the overlay.
    5. Use the same wording on each platform, but match the text formatting to the native style, the look each platform uses by default.
  • Example: a DIY phone tripod hack was filmed on a camera and edited in InShot. Then it was posted with the same thumbnail. The overlay "DIY phone tripod hack" was added with each platform's native text feature. That feature is said to boost the algorithm, the system that decides who sees a video.
  • Mistake: uploading one video to every platform with the same unchanged text styling. This misses platform-specific optimisation, which means adjusting for each platform. Use each platform's native text features and fonts, because format is part of what the algorithm rewards.
  • Watermarks are logos or marks that stay on a video. The animation tool's watermark (bottom left) and the subtitle tool's watermark (top) remain on the video unless covered. Cover them with a screenshot or overlay. Then add your own logo in the bottom left corner.

Thumbnails and hooks

1 min
  • The thumbnail is the still image people see before the video plays. It must grab attention, stop the scroll (stop people swiping past), and show the product being used. It is the first visual impression of the ad.
  • Use the thumbnail to speak to the main pain point (the main problem) of direct customers. Keep the same thumbnail with a matching opening clip. That combination can be a winning pattern.

Quality bar and mistakes to avoid

1 min
  • The quality of the video is the most important factor. A good video can be re-uploaded or repurposed later, and may perform again. A bad video will not succeed, whatever hashtags or posting tricks you use.
  • Clips must match what is being said. Low-quality or pixelated (blocky, grainy) footage should not be used. Poor visual quality makes an ad look cheap.
  • Basic production is enough to start: a modern smartphone, a plain setup and consistent uploads. Film, edit and test before buying more.

Build the cut before the polish

1 min
  • Build the cut structure first, meaning the order of shots. Then add transitions, sound effects and voiceover. Working in that order keeps the story clear before you polish it.

Voiceover

1 min
  1. Write the script first.
  2. Record the voice on the phone's recorder app, reading the script over the edit.
  3. Upload the recording to cloud storage (online file storage), then download it to the computer.
  4. Import the audio into the Audio bin (the folder where imported media is kept). Place it under the visuals and apply the dialogue preset, a ready-made sound setting for speech.

Text and graphics in Premiere Pro

1 min

Premiere Pro is a professional video editor made by Adobe.

  • Use the text tool in the Graphics panel to type text.
  • Adjust the font, capitalization and scale. Use the alignment guides, the lines that appear to help you line things up, to centre it.
  • Style the text with a fill color, a stroke (outline) and a background.

Speed changes and motion

1 min

Speed ramp (a change of speed during a clip) in Premiere Pro with keyframes:

  1. Add a speed keyframe at the start of the section you want to change.
  2. Keep the first part slow.
  3. Add a second keyframe where the action should speed up, and set that section to fast.
  4. Play it back to check that the timing matches the cut.
  • Ease in and ease out the start and end of a movement or transition. A movement that starts and stops abruptly looks unnatural. Easing makes it feel smooth.

Transitions

1 min
  • In Premiere Pro's Effects panel, the video transitions folder contains presets, which are ready-made settings. One example is Dip to Black, which fades a clip out to black.
  • Drag the preset onto the cut point between two clips to apply it.
  • Apply easing to the start and end of transitions as well as movements.

Captions

1 min
  • Animated word-by-word captions (subtitles that appear one word at a time) suit YouTube Shorts engagement. But in the tests described, they did not perform well for ads.
  • Test animated word-by-word captions against standard captions before using them for paid ads.

Color and filters

1 min
  • In Edits (the video app), the Paris filter is a Story-style filter, a filter made for short vertical stories. It brightens the footage overall.
  • To keep one consistent look across all clips in a project, use the "Apply filter to all clips" option. Do not filter each clip separately.
