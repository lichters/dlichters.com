Creative strategy and the production system

Section 12.1 · 1 of 26 in this chapter · 30 min read

In short

  • Every ad has three parts: the idea, the style and the hook. The hook is the first thing that grabs attention, and it matters most.
  • Make many different ads that are useful and checked. Do not make one perfect ad, and do not flood the account with unchecked ads.
  • Most of your effort goes to simple ads you can make in about one day. These are called workhorse ads.
  • Get the message right first. A new format will not save an ad that misses the customer's problem.
  • Ads (creatives) decide most results. Make many new ones every week.
More key points
  • Start by copying what competitors run. Once a product makes about $500 to $600, make your own ads.
  • Do not drop a product after one batch of failed ads. Make several more batches first.
  • Keep files tidy: one dated folder per product.

Creative strategy as a system

2 min
  • Treat creative strategy as a system. Creative strategy is the plan for which ads you make and why.
  • A system has clear parts. Each part has one specific role.
  • Clear steps let the process be followed step by step and scaled (made bigger).
  • This structure makes it possible to hand the work to AI (computer tools that generate content).
  • Creative strategy runs in four stages: conception, planning, production and management.
    • Conception means coming up with the idea.
    • Production means making the ad.
    • Management means running and checking the ads.
    • AI can support each stage.
  • Creative strategy involves three parties.
    • The first is the ad platform, where the ads run.
    • The second is the brand's potential customers. They search for or use that platform.
    • The third is the creative team. That team is the brand itself.
    • The brand's job is to make all three work together in a relevant way.
  • No creative strategy works unless the advertiser understands how each party behaves.
  • Before any ads are planned, understand three things.
    • Platform needs.
    • Audience habits, meaning how customers behave.
    • Production capacity, meaning how much the brand can make.
  • Decide which kinds of ads you want to make first.
  • Decide this before you choose a team structure or AI tools.
  • Without this decision, the team, the tools and the instructions cannot be set up properly.
  • Mixing too many opinions dilutes them, which often lowers effectiveness (how well the work performs).
  • A committee (a group of people) with different priorities decides worse than one knowledgeable person.
  • Before using AI for creative work, choose one proven source or method (a set way of working).
  • Train the AI on that one source. Do not mix many.
  • Testing creatives (the ads and their visuals) is one of the most important activities in e-commerce (selling online).
  • New creative ideas should flow continuously through a pipeline (a line of stages that ideas move through).
  • Keeping ideas visible and moving them through stages stops good concepts from being forgotten.
  • Do not over-engineer the brief (the short instructions for an ad). Over-engineering means making it too complicated.
  • The product problem, the customer's desire and the solution do not all need to be written on paper.
  • The worksheet (a fill-in form) is a guide to shape the message. It is not a task you must finish in full.
  • A product page (the page where people buy) and its creatives only need to be good enough for a test.
  • Perfection takes too much time and money before any data (results numbers) arrives.

Campaign ideas and creative methods

3 min

Three levels of creative method

  1. Swapping a product name into a proven headline (the main line of an ad).
    • This is the shallowest and most common method.
    • It works only when the product, market (the group of people who might buy) and timing match the original.
  2. Formulas (fixed patterns you fill in) help with how a headline is worded.
    • The idea itself cannot be fitted to a formula.
  3. Analysis is the only reliable approach.
    • It gives a unique answer for every product, market and moment.

Building a national campaign

  1. Find the dominant image or idea that will sell the product the first time.
    • It must stay true to the market over many uses.
  2. Compress that image into one statement or picture that is strong enough to sell alone.
  3. Do not repeat the same headline or lead picture every time.
    • Present a series of variations.
    • Each variation comes from the central idea.
    • Each one must be different enough to make the reader read on.
  4. Keep the central idea recognisable across the series.
    • Past desire is reused.
    • The variations renew interest.

Repeated-headline pattern

  • Formula (a fixed pattern): a central campaign idea is repeated word for word in every ad as the headline.
    • Each ad has a changing series of illustrations or opening scenes.
    • These make the same question fresh each time.
    • The reader must look at the picture and the copy together to get the answer.
  • Why it works: continuity (a consistent feel) is kept.
    • Familiarity and boredom are avoided.
  • Example A: the same tagline (a short catchy line) for a shampoo in every ad, with a new fashion scene each week.
  • Example B: the same question about a bread brand in every ad, shown with a different family breakfast each time.

Focal points and product-led series

  • The central idea can be carried by a single focal point (the one thing the eye goes to) rather than a slogan.
    • The focal point might be a product quality shown in every photo.
    • Or a consistent style in typography (the look of the lettering) and fashion.
    • Or the product itself, shown from a new angle in each ad.
    • Each ad reveals a new fact about the product.
    • Subject matter and visuals stay consistent.
  • A product-led series (ads built around the product) can use a bare background, close-up views and one new fact per ad.
    • Each ad alone should be enough to interest a buyer.
    • Together, they build an impression of novelty (newness) and value.
  • Each headline should touch the dominant desire (the strongest want).
    • It should express the campaign theme.
    • It should draw the reader into the body copy (the main text).
    • This often works through a visible contradiction (a clash) between the headline and the picture.
  • Build the campaign message from slogans or taglines used as themes (main subjects).
    • Unify them with a core principle of belief.
    • The formula is Impact + Frequency + Time = Winning Message.
    • Impact means how strongly the message hits. Frequency means how often it is seen. Time means how long it runs.
    • The core message is the engine.
    • Biases (what people already tend to believe) are accelerants (fuel that speeds things up). They are not the main driving force.
  • Brand messages should appear in ads, emails and content at the same time.
    • The brand's core claims should be repeated at every touchpoint (every place a customer meets the brand).
    • This builds a community around the problem the brand solves.

Volume, diversity and relevance

2 min

The three pillars

  • Three pillars (main supports) guide creative strategy: volume, diversity and relevance.
  • They work best as objectives the brand aims for across its whole ad account (the account where ads run).
  • Platforms such as Meta (owner of Facebook and Instagram) and TikTok look for all three.
  • Aim for these three objectives in ad production.
  • Relevance means each ad is useful or valuable for the viewer.
  • It must not create a negative user experience.
  • Account-level objectives of volume, diversity and relevance act as guardrails (safety rails that keep things on track).
  • They cover the whole ad account.
  • They are separate from the single-ad variables of idea, style and hook (explained below).

Volume

  • Volume means running a high number of ads that might perform well.
  • It does not mean uploading an occasional ad.
  • Platforms such as Meta and TikTok give little support to accounts that upload rarely.

Diversity

  • Diversity means running different kinds of ads.
  • Do not keep repeating one ad, especially one that is underperforming (not getting good results).
  • Winning ads are found by testing varied approaches.

Relevance

  • Relevance means ads must be genuinely valuable and useful to people on the platform.
  • Platforms fear that annoying ads will push users to competing apps.
  • So the algorithm (the platform's automatic ranking system) rewards brands that publish useful, relevant content.
  • Creative quality is becoming more important than audience targeting (choosing who sees the ad).
  • Platforms are moving toward broad targeting (showing ads to a wide group and letting the platform find buyers).
  • When targeting is broad, the ad creative does most of the work of finding the right buyers.
  • So new creatives must keep being produced and tested.

Failure modes of the pillars

  • Mass-producing low-quality AI ads without checking them produces poor results.
    • Example: 300 ads per month with no review.
    • This wastes time.
    • At worst, it can lead to restricted or banned ad accounts (limited or shut off).
    • Visible flaws hurt performance.
    • Examples of flaws: off shadows, spelling mistakes, or very short low-effort videos.
  • Spending a long time polishing one ad with AI leaves no time for other ads.
    • Examples of polishing: movie-level backdrops (very cinematic backgrounds), endless editing and repeated revisions.
    • In the same time, five to ten more diverse ads could have been produced and tested.
  • The best approach avoids both extremes.
    • No unchecked mass output.
    • No perfectionism on single ads.
    • Create a steady range of diverse ads that are relevant and reviewed.
  • Heavy editing of ad creatives in dropshipping (selling products you do not stock; the supplier ships them) often lowers performance.
    • It often means a worse CTR (click-through rate: the share of viewers who click).
    • It often means a higher CPM (cost per 1,000 views).
  • Simple, direct creatives tend to outperform polished ones for typical dropshipping products.

Ad variables: idea, style and hook

2 min

Definitions

  • Every ad is built from three variables (parts you can change): idea, style and hook.
  • Each one can be changed to alter how the ad is perceived.
  • The pillars describe the account goals.
  • The three variables describe what a single ad is made of.
  • The idea is the core message of an ad.
  • The idea is its main point, sales argument or selling point (the reason to buy).
  • It answers what the ad is trying to say.
  • The idea is the core message. A separate selling point usually makes a separate idea.
  • An ad only holds attention when its message is relevant to the viewer.
  • So an ad for an unrelated problem gets scrolled past.
  • The style is how the idea is presented.
    • Examples: a founder speaking to the camera, a static (still) comparison with another product, or a demonstration of the product.
    • The same idea can be delivered in many different styles.
  • The hook is the element that grabs attention first.
    • In a static image, it is the layout or visual element that catches the eye instantly.
    • In video, it usually falls in the first seconds and can run longer.
  • Ad concept (the overall plan) = idea + style.
  • An ad cannot have a concept with only an idea or only a style.
  • The hook is the opening part of the concept that the viewer sees first.

Priority of the hook

  • The hook makes more difference to ad performance than any other variable.
  • This is seen repeatedly in testing.
  • So the hook deserves the most attention when creating and testing ads.

Avoiding over-complication

  • Many agencies (companies that make ads for brands), brands and coaches push teams to consider nine or ten variables.
    • Examples: sub-message (a smaller message inside the ad), angle (the way the ad frames its message), motion (movement) and sub-proof (a small piece of proof).
  • This becomes confusing.
  • It delays testing the three variables that matter most: idea, style and hook.
  • Creative merit (how good the ad is) depends on quality, angle and narrative (the story).
  • Try different angles and story structures before deciding a creative has failed.

Levers and copy

2 min

The three levers

  • Each ad has three levers you control: visuals, written copy and narrated copy.
  • Pulling them in different directions changes how the ad looks and is perceived.
  • Visuals are everything shown on screen.
    • They include static images and assets (media files).
    • They include B-roll (extra supporting footage shown while the main voice speaks).
    • They include camera angles.
  • Written copy is all text appearing on the creative.
    • It includes subtitles (words shown on screen with the speech).
    • It includes a core caption (the main line of text).
    • It includes a text tile (a box with text).
  • Narrated copy is the voiceover (spoken audio over the video).
  • Music rarely changes performance much.
  • The best ads point all three levers in the same direction.
    • Aim them at one chosen idea and style.
    • Visuals, written copy and narration should reference and show the same thing.
    • Do not mix different styles.

Ad copy

  • Ad copy (the written text in the ad) is often the real cause of poor results.
  • It is not only the image or video.
  • Write copy carefully. It can decide performance.
  • Before focusing on the store (your online shop), improve existing footage and images.
  • Add AI tools where they are useful.
  • Communicate the offer clearly.
  • The goal of these creatives is a strong CTR.

Checklist for a good ad

  1. Attention-grabbing hook that highlights the problem and the main benefit, relevant to the target audience (the people you want to reach).
  2. A compelling (strong, convincing) offer.
  3. A clear call to action (CTA: the line or button that tells people what to do and where to go).
  4. Benefit-focused copy (text about what the buyer gains).
  5. Social proof (signs that others trust it, such as testimonials (customer quotes), case studies or reviews).
  6. Scarcity or urgency (a reason to act now), such as an end date or limited stock.
  7. High-quality visuals that illustrate the point.
  8. Educational or entertaining content.
  9. Curiosity built up without hiding what the product is.

Native style and iteration

  • Build creative iterations (new versions) by combining your best images with your best videos.
  • Use split-screen layouts (two pictures side by side).
  • Add overlays (layers placed on top) for colours and sizes.
  • Layer image ads as overlays.
  • Test endcard (the final screen of a video) variations.
    • Endcards can show colours, before-and-after pictures, offers or CTAs.
  • Editors should include voice-over, subtitles and product benefits.
  • Format them in the native style (the look that fits each platform).
    • TikTok style for TikTok.
    • Hormozi style (a storytelling style) for storytelling.
  • Winning ads match the target season's aesthetic (look and feel).
  • They feel emotional and look native to TikTok and Reels (Instagram's short videos).
  • They use lifestyle or POV (point of view: the camera shows what the person sees) angles.
  • They must not feel salesy (pushy).
  • Viewers buy when the ad blends into their feed (their scrolling list).
  • Scroll-stopping creatives (ones that stop people scrolling) drive impulse buying (buying on a sudden urge).
  • The first seconds must make a product look desirable and relatable at a glance.
  • When producing new ads, replicate the lighting, colours and camera angles that match the season.

Workhorse and warhorse ads

4 min

Categories

  • Ad creatives fall into three practical categories, based on effort and quality.
  • The names are animal labels: donkey, workhorse and warhorse.
  • Donkeys are low effort, low quality.
  • Workhorses are low effort, high quality.
  • Warhorses are high effort, potentially high quality.
  • Most creative effort should go to the middle ground, not to either end.
Category Effort Quality Guidance
Donkey Low Low Avoid
Workhorse Low High Core of production
Warhorse High Potentially high Make sparingly

Donkeys

  • Donkeys are quick, generic ads.
  • They exist only to fill a slot (an open spot in your plan).
  • They are often made with unchecked AI output.
  • They are rarely seen as relevant or valuable.
  • They often lack creative diversity because styles and ideas are not tested.
  • Avoid them.

Warhorses

  • Warhorses are very high-effort productions.
    • Examples: hiring a full studio, booking many people, commissioning custom B-roll and using AI to add cinematic backdrops.
  • They can look impressive.
  • But they may become a cinematic piece instead of a sales ad.
  • The test is whether they sell, not how good they look.
  • Make warhorses sparingly (rarely).
  • Even very high-effort productions do not always perform well or reach high quality.
  • Their extra effort does not guarantee sales.
  • Field-shot ads (filmed outdoors with real people, such as street interviews) are warhorses if the team cannot currently film them.
  • They require going out to meet people.
  • Choose a style the current team or AI tools can produce now.
  • Keep field-shot formats for later.
  • Some warhorses become workhorses over time.
  • As a brand builds assets (reusable materials), learns its processes and gets better at AI, warhorses take much less effort.
  • The workhorse share should grow.

Workhorses

  • A workhorse is an ad that one person can complete in about one day or less.
  • It uses existing assets or a set of assets already in production.
  • Anything that needs a new studio booking or new camera equipment for that single ad is a warhorse.
  • If a studio or shoot is already booked for other content, extra footage captured during it can count as a workhorse.
  • It adds little extra effort.
  • If the brand already has B-roll and only needs AI to fill the rest, the ad is a workhorse.
  • A workhorse is high quality mainly because it is relevant, not because it is polished.
  • The idea and message matter most.
  • A simple, clear presentation with a strong hook that targets the right audience is enough.
  • Because it is cheap to produce, the brand can keep up the volume and diversity of tested ideas, styles and hooks.
  • Most static ads are workhorses.
  • They can be built quickly from templates (ready-made layouts).
  • One person can fill in a template, generate or adjust visuals with AI, and finish the ad.

Allocation

  • Allocate about 80% or more of creative effort and energy to workhorse ads.
  • Allocate about 20% to warhorse ads.
  • These percentages are flexible.
  • A brand can start with 100% workhorses until it has the time and budget for warhorse productions.
  • The line between workhorse and warhorse differs by brand.
  • It has no universal definition.
  • Each brand should decide based on what it finds simple and quick to create.
  • Borderline cases are normal.

Balancing extremes

  • Mass output without review is a mistake.
  • Perfectionism on single ads is also a mistake.
  • The goal is a steady range of diverse, relevant and reviewed ads.

Procedure: listing workhorse ads

  1. Decide before using AI what it will be used for in your ad production.
  2. List the workhorse ads you could produce: the ones that take little effort and could be relevant and sell.
  3. Describe what each workhorse ad would look like.
  4. Break each workhorse ad into a step-by-step production process, so each step is clear and doable.
  5. Use existing inspiration, such as other ad accounts and swipe files (saved examples of ads), to check which styles you can realistically produce.

Procedure: choosing workhorse ads

  1. Which ideas and styles are relevant to the product and the audience?
  2. Which of these can you actually produce now with your current team, capacity and skills?
  • Example: a founder-style ad (the founder speaks to the camera) is a workhorse only if the founder can speak about the product confidently and fairly easily.
    • The benefit could be, for example, how it relieves back pain.
    • If that is possible now, the ad can be produced immediately.

Procedure: breaking down the levers

  1. Decide where the visuals will come from.
  2. Decide how the written copy will be created.
  3. Decide how the narrated copy will be recorded.
    • Check whether you have a microphone to capture good audio.
  4. Check that all three levers are simple to produce.

Hook and style feasibility

2 min
  • Before committing to a workhorse ad, check that the idea is easy to identify.
  • Check that the style is easy to create.
  • Check that the hook is simple to produce.
  • A hook that takes a long time to produce turns the ad into a warhorse.
    • Example: a scientific diagram made without AI help.
  • Check each hook's feasibility (whether it can be done) before committing.
  • Re-check hooks with current AI image tools.
  • Many complex visuals can now be made quickly.
  • So they may qualify as workhorses.
  • Most static ads are workhorses.
  • UGC-style (content that looks made by ordinary people) and TikTok-style raw footage recorded on a phone converts better (turns more viewers into buyers) than studio ads for most products today.
  • Authentic, raw footage with a normal background makes viewers feel a neighbour is recommending the product.
  • This relatability drives action more than polished brand footage.
  • Viewers recognise polished brand footage as an ad.
  • UGC-style and TikTok-style content is recommended over classic salesy ads for Facebook and Instagram.
  • Classic ads can be used for testing.
  • Hire creators once the budget allows.
  • Low-production, native-looking content, such as a smartphone video that looks casually uploaded, outperforms polished traditional ads on TikTok.
  • It increasingly does the same on Instagram Reels.
  • In the owner's testing, UGC-style content was ahead of traditional ads.
  • A strong offer can still lift polished ads.
  • But UGC style is the default starting point.
  • For the mentor's product, picture ads outperformed video ads over the past year.
  • So test picture formats alongside video.
  • Products that need explanation of what they do for the customer still benefit from video.
  • Simple novelty products (new, fun, simple items) need less explanation.
  • Video ads are much harder and slower to produce for a complete beginner.
  • Many people manage them anyway.
  • Start with image-based bottom-of-funnel ads (ads aimed at people close to buying) to validate (check) a custom product.
  • Do this before investing in video.
  • Food-based videos are more time-consuming and messier to produce.
  • The products must be bought, prepared and filmed.
  • Factor the production effort into the decision to test a food-related product.
  • Content for a product that gives a one-time result runs out quickly.
  • The creator cannot repeat the same demonstration over and over.
  • Consider how many different angles a product can support before committing to it.

Choosing the message before the format

2 min
  • The instructions given to a person or AI decide the kind of ad that comes out.
  • A static image with a cost-focused benefit and a UGC-style video talking to the camera need different messages, skills, instructions and production styles.
  • This is true even for the same brand.
  • A vague request, such as asking AI to make an ad for the brand, gives unpredictable results.
  • Results can be the wrong format, the wrong message or a poor mix of styles.
  • Define the ad type and the message before asking AI to create anything.
  • Letting AI generate ad concepts without checking them first produces ads that are not relevant to the audience.
  • Check them against your own research and proven best practices (methods that work).
  • Use research to choose the idea, hook and style first.
  • Then use AI to produce it.
  • If the messaging misses the customer's pain point (the problem that bothers them), changing the ad format will not save the ad.
  • This applies whether the format is a flipping book, street interview, conference, seminar, podcast, claymation (animation made with clay figures), cartoon or UGC AI (AI-made UGC-style content).
  • Messaging comes before format, budget and targeting.
  • Choose two value levers (the main reasons to buy): save money, better quality or save time.
  • Map those two levers onto the list of twelve ad creative formats.
  • Examples of formats: versus-them, product value proposition and before-and-after comparisons.
  • Start with three or four formats that match your strongest differentiation (what makes you different).
  • Do not produce all twelve.
  • Doing everything usually means doing each one poorly.
  • Choose social media channels based on your business type.
  • B2B (business-to-business: selling to other companies) stores may need a LinkedIn presence.
  • Newer consumer brands may need Pinterest or Instagram.
  • A store already running Facebook retargeting ads (showing ads again to people who visited) still needs its own decision about which additional social channel to add.
  • Demand-generation channels (platforms that create interest in products people did not search for), such as Meta, TikTok and YouTube, can create demand for a new hybrid category (a new kind of product that mixes two existing kinds).
  • So a niche (a small, specific market) may be marketed there even when Google search volume (how many people search for a term) is low.
  • Google search and Google Ads only capture demand that already exists.
  • Different platforms need different creative styles.
  • Classic polished Facebook ads from freelance marketplaces (websites where freelancers sell their work) do not work on TikTok.
  • TikTok needs UGC, lifestyle videos and a fast pace.
  • A trending sound is important.

Team, production and tools

2 min

Team

  • Pick which products get custom AI (artificial intelligence) images first. Making them takes time for each product.
  • Good first choices are new launches, best sellers that need a higher CTR (click-through rate, the share of viewers who click), and weak sellers that need a push.
  • Different people like different creatives (ad images or videos). So the founder must make many creatives and learn to customise them.
  • Test many versions of the same winning idea.
  • Real, authentic production starts with the founder's own skills. The founder-style example in the workhorse section applies here.
  • Only ads the current team can speak and film now belong in production.

Tools and costs

  • Photoshop costs about £10 per month. It edits product photos and replaces backgrounds. (Source lists the price in pounds; verify current pricing before budgeting.)
  • Canva offers free graphic design and video editing, with a small course. CapCut is free for video editing.

Organising production files

  • Give each product its own dated folder, named with the date and product name, such as 2023-04 plus the product name.
  • Inside each product folder, keep three folders: Assets (files such as static images, emojis and logos), Audio (music, voiceovers and sound effects) and Video.
  • Split the video folder by theme. Examples are testimonials (customer praise), TikTok winners, paid influencer or UGC (user-generated content, videos made by ordinary people) footage, and dated shoots.

Producing your own creatives

  • Once a product reaches about $500 to $600 in revenue and shows potential, create your own ads. (Sources differ on the threshold; see the proof-of-concept figures below.)
  • Make your own ads in one of three ways: edit the footage yourself, order new footage, or brief a UGC creator to make a video.
  • After a few days of steady sales, make the product stand out. Order the product, then film your own videos or hire creators and actors.
  • Give the product a unique custom name. Custom content makes it harder for copycats (people who copy your ads) to reuse your videos.
  • Own creatives and unique marketing angles (the main reason or story you sell with) set a store apart from competitors who use the same ads.
  • Without them, growth stays slower, even when the product sells.
  • When launching, use existing drop-shipping video (ready-made supplier videos, for products shipped by a supplier) to test quickly.
  • Then switch to custom content for products that show promise. Speed and resources matter early, but custom content gives a stronger return later.
  • A store can first build an organic (free, unpaid) video library.
  • Then run ads on only the one video that performs well. This keeps paid spend focused on proven creative.

Lean starts and when to go custom

2 min

Starting a test with competitor material

  1. Copy the competitor's active ads exactly to see how they perform for your store.
  2. Once the product reaches about $500 to $600 in revenue and shows potential, create your own ads.
  3. Create your own ads by editing the footage yourself, ordering new footage, or briefing a UGC creator to make a video.
  • Start a product test (a short trial to see if a product sells) by copying competitor ads. This shows whether the product works.
  • Once the product reaches roughly $1,000 to $2,000 in sales, invest in your own creatives to stand out.

Proof-of-concept gates (sources differ)

A proof of concept is an early sign that an idea works. A gate is the number you must reach before the next step. VSL means video sales letter, a long sales video. Deep-research ads are ads built on detailed customer research. The testing campaign is the ad campaign used for product tests.

  • The three thresholds measure different things: daily spend, revenue and total sales. They come from different sources.
  • Use the one that matches the metric your store tracks.
  • Treat the proof of concept as a signal that the offer works, not as a fixed rule.
  • Before reaching the gate, keep it simple. Run static image ads (still pictures).
  • Also test the offer structure, discount levels and page layout.
  • VSL first, statics later: VSLs convert (turn viewers into buyers) better, and they force you to state the full message.
  • Extract the winning angle from the VSL, then turn it into static ads.
  • If you cannot make VSLs in-house, hire an agency (an outside team). Outsourcing a high volume of VSLs is expensive.
  • Creative quality usually matters more than website quality for a new store.
  • Strong creatives can make an average store convert. The website alone rarely fixes weak ads.
  • After a product has passed validation (proof that it sells), creative quality is the main lever (the thing that moves results most).
  • A decent product can still perform well with strong video ads.
  • Build the test around finding proven creatives. Do not try to rescue a weak product with heavy editing.
  • Before focusing on the store, improve existing footage and images.
  • Add AI tools where useful, and communicate the offer clearly.

Creative as the main lever

2 min

Ranking the levers (sources differ)

  • Creative is the second biggest lever in a paid ad account, after product and offer.
  • Fresh creative matters more than most campaign tactics or bidding tricks (how the platform buys views or clicks).
  • Without a steady stream of newly tested creatives, cost caps (limits on what you pay per result), bid caps (limits on what you pay per click or view) and structure changes do little.
  • Creative is described as the main lever in current Meta (the company behind Facebook and Instagram) and TikTok advertising.
  • Invest in better creators. Understand how hooks (the first seconds that catch attention) and hold rates (how long people keep watching) work.
  • Creative is the main lever in Meta ads now.
  • A business that stops testing new creative usually sees its campaigns decline within one to two months.
  • So constant creative testing is a core process, not an occasional task.
  • The creative is the main lever in Facebook ads. When it is strong, Facebook learns from the people who engage with it (like, comment or share).
  • So creative quality matters more than the interests chosen.
  • Creatives are the main driver of Meta campaign performance.
  • With weak creatives, even good ad sets (groups of ads with the same targeting, inside a campaign) fail.
  • Strong creatives let broad targeting (no interest filters) perform, because the algorithm (the platform's automatic matching system) finds buyers.
  • Creative is the main driver of performance on TikTok. Roughly 90 percent of the outcome depends on it.
  • When CTR is poor, change the creative rather than the audience. The audience is rarely the cause.
  • On TikTok, creatives matter more than targeting.
  • Test many creatives. Vary the hook, length, caption style and trends, not only the product shot.
  • Creatives drive targeting results more than CBO or ABO settings. CBO and ABO are two Meta budget setups: campaign budget or ad set budget.
  • Facebook increasingly targets through creatives when targeting is broad.
  • The main priority is avoiding overspending during product tests.
  • With the same audience and budget, the creative decides how many viewers engage, click the call-to-action (the button or line asking for action) and reach the website.
  • So it is the main variable to optimise.
  • Other factors, such as CPM (cost per 1,000 views), matter less over the long run.
  • Creative merit (quality) is a main driver of results. Strong engagement lets Facebook push the ad to more people.
  • Creatives with strong engagement get a higher engagement ranking.
  • This tends to lower CPM and CPC (cost per click) compared with competitors.

Reconciling the rankings: the sources agree that creative is the decisive lever once the product and offer are validated. They differ on where it ranks before that point. Validate the product and offer first. Then treat creative as the main lever for scaling (spending more on what works).

Video and platform differences in the ranking

  • On TikTok, creative drives most of the outcome.

Testing cadence and refresh

2 min

Creative testing volume

  • Creative testing is a numbers game. Brands can test 100 to 200 creatives per week.
  • Three to ten winners can cover the testing cost.
  • Plan for many variants rather than expecting one video to win.
  • In the early phase, one operator (the person running the ads) launched at least five new creatives per week.
  • That operator tested about 20 offers. The landing page (the page people reach after a click) converted at about 3.5%.
  • Testing volume came before optimising any single ad.
  • On TikTok, test at least three new creatives per week while a product is running.
  • TikTok is almost entirely creative-driven.
  • Upload at least 20 videos within the first month, or in one to two weeks for fast learning.
  • Do this before judging your creative skills and expecting results.
  • Test new creatives every week.
  • The expert said this is the rule, even though his own schedule often prevents it.
  • Consistent weekly creative testing is the target.

Refresh rhythm (sources differ)

  • Plan to test new creative concepts (one core idea, with creatives as its versions) roughly every two weeks.
  • A single creative typically delivers good results for about one month. Then performance declines steadily.
  • Refresh the testing cycle with new creatives weekly when a fresh set is available.
  • Otherwise, refresh at least every two weeks.
  • Each creative typically performs well for about one month before results decline.
  • So the testing pipeline (a queue of ads moving through stages) must keep producing new concepts.

Expected rotation size

  • Creatives in rotation (ads shown at the same time) = concepts × creatives per concept.
  • Example: 10 concepts × 5 creatives each = 50 creatives in rotation.
  • The count grows as you iterate (make new versions of what works).

Using the testing pipeline

  • Not every creative will convert (turn viewers into buyers).
  • Many creatives work as cheap touchpoints that help other creatives convert.
  • So test a large amount of variation.
  • Creative testing should use a pipeline of stages.
  • A typical example is seven creatives in the pipeline, with five in preparation and two in production.

Testing a new market with creatives

  1. Create four creatives for four audiences, with different benefits and call-outs (short text highlights on the ad).
  2. Go deeper into the one that responds best.
  3. Aim for a range of concepts, such as seven creatives in the pipeline with five in preparation and two in production.

Not dropping a product too early

  • When a product's first batch of ad creatives fails, do not drop the product right away.
  • Produce several more creative batches and test them.
  • Only then decide the product does not work.
  • Start the test with the widest range of approaches before judging a creative as failed.
  • Merit depends on angle (the main idea) and narrative (the story), as well as quality.

Volume versus perfection

  • When producing ad creatives, volume beats perfection.
  • Launching more variations quickly and gathering test data outperforms spending time perfecting a small number before launch.
  • The creative agency goal is to produce volume of creatives that each generate positive contribution margin (profit left after product and ad costs).
  • Do not aim to find one rare breakout ad that matches your highest-performing creative from the past.
  • Many creatives will underperform.
  • Creative success should be measured by the batting average: the percentage of creatives generating positive contribution margin.
  • Do not measure success by finding one breakout ad that beats all previous records.
  • The goal of a creative agency is to produce volume of creatives that each generate positive contribution margin.
  • This is like professional baseball players, who succeed only 20 to 30 percent of the time at the plate.
  • Success is measured on the percentage of ads hitting positive contribution margin, similar to a professional batting average.

Output limits

  • More creative volume is not automatically better.
  • The real question is how much is too much for a given brand.
  • For larger brands, plot revenue against the number of creative assets. This shows whether more output goes with more revenue.
  • Map creative launches per product.
  • This exposes mismatches, such as heavy spend on one product while only three or four new creatives launch for it each month.
  • This approach suits brands with three or four products.
