Ad formats and concept templates

Section 12.15 · 15 of 26 in this chapter · 46 min read

In short

  • An ad format is how an ad is shown: video, a still picture, or a set of images to swipe through. A concept is the kind of piece you make in that format.
  • Pick the format for each product and its buyers. Then test it, because what wins for one product can lose for another.
  • Test social-proof ads first: ads that show real customer quotes, ratings or reviews.
  • Video is not always better than pictures, so test both.
  • Only promise guarantees you can really honour.
More key points
  • Giveaway ads: show 1 of N winners, name the prize, and always include the chance-of-winning disclaimer.
  • Start with cheap tests: try many image or meme variations, and keep the ones people click.
  • Put your product's benefits first, then add customer quotes, and use no more than three quotes per ad.
  • Retargeting (showing ads again to people who already visited) works best with a reminder or a free resource, not a cold sale.
  • Test formats with real numbers. Picture ads can beat video, so do not assume video is needed.
  • Show the problem first, then show your product solving it.
  • Compare your product to a clearly weaker option, so it looks better.
  • Offer a free gift or use real customer reviews to get people to act.
  • Run native ads from a person's page, never from the brand page.

Format, concept and how to choose

1 min
  • An ad format is the medium an ad uses, such as video, a static image (a still picture that does not move) or a carousel (a set of images the viewer swipes through). A concept is the type of piece made in that format. Examples: a VSL (video sales letter, a long video that sells one product), a listicle (a list-style post, such as "5 reasons to buy"), an explainer (a video that shows how something works), an unboxing (filming the product being opened), a before-and-after, or a text-only black screen.
  • Choose the format by the product and the audience (the people you sell to). Then test it. A format that wins for one product can underperform for another, and some products will not fit a layout at all.
  • Test a mix of formats to find the winner: lifestyle images (photos of the product in daily life), offer graphics with features and reviews, and "this or that" benefit layouts.
  • Use the best assets available (assets are your photos, videos and files). Clip (cut out a part of) or repurpose (reuse) competitor videos if needed.

Testing system: swipe file and test rules

2 min
  • Keep a small swipe file (a folder of proven ads you can copy from) of ad creative templates (ready-made ad designs) that already worked. This lets you test several products or offers quickly, without waiting for slow or unreliable outsourced creative (design work paid out to other people). Fast testing shows which product and angle (the main point the ad pushes) and which benefit (the good result a buyer gets) respond best, before you spend on polished assets.
  • A testimonial (a real customer's quote about the product) in a Facebook ad, run at a £20-30 daily spend (money spent per day on the ad), produced a strong early result. So social proof (proof from other people, such as reviews) is worth testing first.
  • Test each template against each product. Do not assume it works for every product.
  • Keep templates editable in Canva (a simple online design tool). Then product photos, colours and text can be swapped in minutes.
  • Track which template and offer combination gets the best click-through rate (the share of people who see the ad and click it) and spend efficiency (what you get back for each unit of money spent). Do this before you scale it (spend more on it).
  • Vary creative formats within one set: one with text on the image, one with no text, and one short video with a slowly moving background. Winners differ by audience, so test all of them.

Image, video and carousel: where sources differ

4 min

Sources disagree on the best format. Use both views, depending on the product:

  • Video first: Video creatives significantly outperform image-only ads, so use video whenever possible.
  • Video often wins: Video often outperforms static images for engagement (people liking, commenting or watching), but image ads can also work well.
  • Video not always: Video creatives do not always beat static images. A product suited to video can still perform better with pictures, so test both formats rather than assuming video wins.
  • Image ads can be cheaper and convert well (convert means make people buy), sometimes better than video. This is especially true for products that are not obvious problem solvers.
  • Video usually converts better overall, but it costs more to run.
  • Image for self-selling products: Image ads can suit visually appealing products that sell themselves, such as home decor. Video is usually expected to convert better for problem-solving products.
  • Demonstration needs video: Video is the better format for products that need demonstration, such as a scent, a texture or a visible use. Image ads can perform well for self-explanatory products. Jewellery usually needs close-ups (zoomed-in photos) and in-context shots (photos of the product in real use), so test both.
  • Case: picture ads won on traffic (visits to the store):
    • A clothing brand hired a photographer for a product video. It ran both video ads and picture ads.
    • The picture ads drove more traffic to the store.
    • So the video moved to retargeting (showing ads again to people who already saw the brand). This kept the product in front of people who had already seen the brand.
    • Video ads were also more expensive per thousand impressions (per 1,000 times an ad is shown) than picture ads.
  • Static jewellery in context: Static images of products such as jewellery can work when shown in context, such as worn with an outfit or in an elegant setting. Video or UGC (user-generated content, meaning real customers' own photos or videos) adds more story. Use it when images do not tell the story well.
  • Reframing with UGC: Reframing means showing a product from a new angle. For example, a gift from a partner or a personal story. This can turn a flooded market (a market with too many sellers) into a new segment (a new group of buyers), because the ad speaks to a specific person in a specific situation. Customer testimonials in a natural voice (UGC) are often more convincing than a static product image.

Carousel and multi-image rules:

  • When using image creatives instead of video, use the carousel format so customers can view multiple angles and understand the product from different perspectives.
  • In one fashion test, three formats were used: a single picture ad, a carousel ad and a slideshow ad (images that change every few seconds on their own). The carousel performed best in that test.
  • Carousel ads work well for products with a lot of lifestyle imagery, such as fashion. Single image ads can also be strong. Natural lifestyle image ads should be included in testing alongside designed graphics (ads made with text and design).
  • Carousel ads can show several images in one unit (one ad). This helps vary the message within a single ad.
  • Carousel ads that show several products and point to a collection page (one page with many products, not a single product page) work well. This is especially true for a buy two, get the third free offer.

Format combinations:

  • A split-screen layout (the screen divided into two halves) can combine an endcart (the final still screen that shows product information) with a vertical video (filmed tall, for phone screens).
    • The endcart carries static product information.
    • The video adds dynamic engagement (movement that holds attention).
    • Both formats sit in one creative unit.
  • Founder videos filmed to camera are a common workhorse format (a format that is used often because it reliably works), if the founder is comfortable on camera. Each video can carry a different message, idea or angle at low cost.
  • A product demonstration filmed with a camera on a desk, showing how the product works, is a common workhorse format. It needs little setup and can be repeated for different products or angles.
  • Other UGC formats include before-and-after and testimonial videos. The problem-solution framework (show the problem, then the product as the fix) is a reliable default structure for most products.
  • A user-generated style clip, where a buyer describes receiving a product as a gift, makes the message feel personal and credible. Static images can work for products whose appeal is in the look. But a story in context often gives more.

Same message, different styles

1 min
  • The same message can be shown in different styles: large text on a static image, a person showing the problem in a video, a founder ad, or a versus ad (two products compared). A hook (the opening line or first seconds that grab attention) can differ within one style. For example, a scientific diagram versus a real-life demonstration. They attract different kinds of viewers.
  • For an interview-style ad:
    • The visuals should look like a real interview.
    • The copy should mention the interview with an expert.
    • The audio should clearly show a question followed by an answer.
    • Every element (each part you control) should make the format obvious.
  • Storytelling suits simple gadgets such as security cameras. Creators can invent scenarios that show the product being useful. Use it only if you can tell a convincing story, and keep the claims honest (say only what is true).

Testimonial ads

1 min

Core formula

1 min
  • Pain-to-relief transformation (moving from a problem to relief): The customer states the original problem or emotional state. Then they describe the moment of change. Then they end with a specific result, with no negatives. This works because it shows transformation through experience rather than claims. The absence of drawbacks ("no meds") is as persuasive as the benefit.
    • Example 1: "Dealt with afternoon fatigue for years, now I have steady energy all day without jitters."
    • Example 2: "Couldn't focus during meetings, this changed my work life and I'm actually productive again."
  • Keep testimonials short. Testimonials under 20 words outperform long narratives, because social media users scan (skim quickly) rather than read. Each testimonial should name one specific transformation ("life back", "control again", "migraines stopped"). A negative comparison (what the product is NOT: "no meds", "no side effects") increases credibility.
  • Avoid generic testimonials such as "Amazing product!", "Great quality" or "5 stars". They provide no social proof (evidence other people trust it), because any competitor could claim the same. Specific testimonials tied to the exact problem and transformation work because they are hard to fake. Examples: "Finally, real relief without meds", "Gave me my life back".

Testimonial concept template

1 min 
Build a testimonial ad by pairing a customer's face with a direct quote about their experience, such as "I tried three things before this and nothing worked until I found it." Display the product clearly in the creative, ideally showing the customer actively using or holding it. This establishes credibility by demonstrating how a real person achieved success with the product.

Targeting skeptics

1 min
  • Testimonial ads for skeptics (people who doubt) and proof-driven buyers (people who want evidence first) follow this order: past struggle → tried other solutions → discovered your product → current success.
    • Example 1: "Tested four competitors until finding this one-finally solved the problem."
    • Example 2: "Spent weeks searching for alternatives before discovering ours works better."
    • This works because skeptics need peer validation (approval from someone like them) before they trust.

Testimonial creative pattern

1 min
  • Formula: volume of happy customers + believable, varied reviews + two trust badges (small labels that show a promise, such as "free returns"). Buyers trust visible proof from other people more than brand claims.
    • Example: "Over 12,000 happy homes" with three different reviews and a free returns badge.
    • Example: "Trusted by 5,000 runners" with a 30-day fit guarantee badge.
  • Use several strong testimonials. Set the colour scheme to your brand colours. Do not use testimonials from one reviewer only, because it looks unrealistic.
  • Use testimonials from different people, because a set that all comes from one reviewer looks fake. Adapt the brand colours and the font to your own branding (your logo, colours and style). Keep the background open for colour changes.

Trust signals

1 min
  • Place trust signals (short promises that build confidence) at the bottom of the ad. Examples: a 60-day money-back guarantee and free shipping for life. They reduce purchase hesitation (doubt before buying) in the ad itself. Use only guarantees you can actually honour.
  • Trust signals at the bottom of a testimonial creative reduce purchase hesitation in the ad itself.

Video testimonials

1 min
  • Video testimonials are an affordable way to share the message and generate many sales. In the example, the best video testimonial ad produced a couple hundred thousand dollars in revenue. Reviews were reinforced by sending the reviewer a brief (a short list of the topics to cover).
  • A client testimonial video can send viewers straight to the product page (the page where they buy). Then everyone who watched it can be retargeted.
  • A testimonial video ad can send viewers straight to a marketplace listing (a product page on a shopping site) through an affiliate link (a link that pays a commission on sales). The people who watched can then be retargeted. The example used an Amazon affiliate listing as the destination.

Proof-heavy layouts

1 min
  • In print, a flyer with a headline, a photo of the seller and dozens of quotes from participants, arranged in columns, feels overwhelming in its proof. A numbered list of 22 reasons to buy, or a brochure with 101 customer stories, reads as convincing. It does so even before the reader checks the claims.

Social proof ads

1 min
  • Social proof ads target herd-followers (buyers who follow the crowd) and bandwagon buyers (buyers who join what is popular). They use: customer volume + satisfaction rating + credible third-party mention (a mention by an outside source people trust, such as a magazine). These buyers trust decisions validated by large customer bases combined with respected authorities.
    • Example 1: "150,000+ customers. 4.8 stars. Featured in Forbes and TechCrunch."
    • Example 2: "98,000+ users. 4.9 rating. Recommended by Wall Street Journal."

Social proof concept template

1 min 
Social proof concept: "50,000+ customers. 4.8 stars. Featured in Vogue and Men's Health." Use numbers, ratings, and media mentions to signal credibility and show that others trust the brand.
  • Social proof ad: a short customer quote with a five-star rating and the store name, linked to the product or store. It works because buyers trust other buyers more than the brand itself.
    • Example 1: "Five stars. The fit was perfect and delivery took three days, says a happy customer."
    • Example 2: "Five stars for the quality, says a repeat buyer. Shop the store today."
  • Stacking emotion and credibility: merge a customer testimonial with social proof stats (numbers that show popularity) in one concept.
    • Example: "I had back pain every day by 3pm. After 2 weeks with Posture Pro, it's totally gone. 50,000+ relieved users. 4.8 stars. Featured in Vogue and Men's Health."
    • This stacks emotional trust (a personal story) with credibility (numbers, ratings, media mentions).

Us vs them and comparison ads

2 min

  • Us-vs-them comparison (your product next to a rival's): Place your product on one side and the competitor's on the other. Make the competitor's background dimmer, so your side looks like the clear winner. Name specific points where you are better, such as seam quality or protective features. The viewer sees the difference in one glance. A light pattern and a white gradient (a soft fade of colour) laid over the image add texture. They keep the layout from looking flat.

    • Example: "Our jacket has double-stitched seams and reinforced zips, while theirs has single stitching and exposed zip edges."
    • Example: "Our kettle boils in 90 seconds with auto shut-off, while theirs takes 4 minutes and has no safety cut-off."

  • Them-vs-us creative: competitor product on the left, your product on the right, with a short list of what makes yours better. Formula: the competitor side shows the weakness, your side shows the strength, and both sides stay visually simple. It frames the comparison in one glance, without long copy.

    • Example: "Our zipper outlasts their plastic clasp", with the old brand dimmed on the left.
    • Example: "Their bottle leaks, ours seals in 3 seconds", with a matching split layout.

  • Comparative framework ("We vs the Others"):

    1. Use a split-screen layout.
    2. Display your product's USP (unique selling proposition, the one thing that sets it apart) and its advantages versus what competitors lack.
    3. Include a clear call to action (CTA, a clear instruction such as "Shop now").

  • Comparison video pattern: show a well-known product the audience already knows. Then introduce your product as the better alternative. The hook says the audience has seen the known product, and asks whether they have seen yours. It works best when the compared product is familiar to viewers.

    • Example hook: "Everyone has tried this popular kitchen gadget, but have you seen the one that actually cuts prep time in half?"
    • Example hook: "You know the classic desk lamp everyone owns, now meet the one that adjusts itself to your workday."

  • Trend comparison pattern: use a trending audio (a popular sound clip the platform is promoting right now). A speaker compares a competitor with their own product. Then on-screen text (words placed over the video) lists the product's benefits in quotation marks. Comparisons and POV formats (videos filmed from the viewer's point of view) also perform well.

    • Example: a kitchen tool shows a messy competitor result, then its own clean result with on-screen text such as "Cleaner edges, no sticky residue".

Benefit and feature callouts

1 min

Benefit concept template

3 min 
Benefit concept: Place a headline at the top (e.g., "Sleep through the night, wake without back pain. Every morning."), list 3-4 concrete benefits below (e.g., "Relieve back pain in as little as 7 days," "Improve posture and boost confidence"), put the product image in the middle, and add a discount or CTA at the bottom. Keep it simple and outcome-focused.

  • Benefit ad pattern for outcome-first and problem-aware buyers (people who want the result, or know their problem): problem state → transformation promised → concrete result. These buyers already recognise their problem. They respond to specific outcome promises.

    • Example 1: "Stop dealing with [pain point]. Achieve [desired state] daily."
    • Example 2: "Never again experience [problem]. Get [benefit] consistently."

  • Worked example, posture corrector (a brace that helps you sit and stand straight):

    • Headline: "Wake Up Pain-Free. Every Single Morning."
    • Bullet points: "Relieve back and neck pain in as little as 7 days," "Improve posture and boost confidence," "Lightweight, comfortable and invisible under clothes."
    • Product image centred.
    • CTA "SHOP NOW", with the discount "20% OFF Today, Offer ends Sunday" and the regular price crossed out.
    • This structure shows benefit first, proof second, urgency third.

  • Feature callout pattern: one large benefit as the headline. A few features point to specific parts of a cut-out product (a product photo with the background removed), placed in the centre. A simple step-by-step line sits at the bottom. It links one clear promise to visible product details. It works best for products that can be cut out and placed centrally. It does not fit every product.

    • Example: "Big benefit: Sleep through the night. Features: quiet motor, blackout cover, 8-hour timer, with arrows pointing to each part."

  • Benefit callout creative: one large headline states the main benefit. Several short feature lines with arrows point to product parts that support it. Formula: headline benefit + two to four supporting features + a simple call-to-action step at the bottom. One clear promise is easier to grasp than a list of features.

    • Example: "Sleep deeper in minutes", with arrows to a weighted lining and a cooling layer.
    • Example: "Clean floors without bending", with arrows to a telescopic handle and a swivel head.

  • A benefit callout creative works best for products that can be cut out and placed centrally, with features written around them. Products that do not fit this layout may still work with a different structure, so test each product.

  • Feature point-out framework:

    1. Start with a close-up image of the product.
    2. Add 4-5 text-based advantages surrounding the product.
    3. Use Canva or Photoshop (professional image-editing software) to make variations (versions of the same ad) easily.

  • Single big-benefit creative: one benefit in a large headline, with a small social-proof badge (a label showing a number, such as a count of happy customers). Formula: one benefit + one badge + short direct language, with no feature list. A single promise reads at a glance, and the badge adds trust.

    • Example: "Stops leaks in one wipe", with a badge reading "Thousands of happy customers".
    • Example: "Dries in 90 seconds", with a badge reading "4,000 five-star reviews".

  • One clear benefit headline with a badge reading "Thousands of happy customers" avoids the bad outcome.

  • Test the single benefit callout against feature-led layouts (ads that lead with a list of features), since results vary by product.

Offer, free gift and urgency ads

1 min

Free gift concept template

2 min 
Free gift concept: Lead with "If you order today in the next 24 hours, we'll throw in [companion product or resource] free with your order." This creates urgency by setting a 24-hour deadline and adds perceived value. Use as primary text and headline.
  • Free gift ad pattern for fence-sitters (buyers who have not decided yet) and value-focused buyers (buyers who weigh what they get against the price): core product + bonus offer + time anchor (a deadline). These buyers decide on total perceived value (how much the deal feels worth) compared to the price. Bonuses shift that ratio in your favour.
    • Example 1: "Order this month and get the [accessory] free."
    • Example 2: "Buy now and receive [complementary product] included."
  • Offer creative: product image and name at the top, the top three benefits, a big discount or free-gift element with the price difference shown, and a clear call to action. It answers what the product is, why it is worth buying and what the deal is, in one image. Use it to test offers quickly before building video.
  • Offer test creative: product image, three core benefits and one clear offer, such as buy one get one free, a free gift or 40 percent off. Formula: product + three benefits + one offer, in a single static layout. It shows which offer a market responds to, faster than a full video.
    • Example: "Buy one, get the second free", with three benefits and a product shot.
    • Example: "Free travel pouch with every order", with three benefits and a gift image that shows the gift's price so the value is clear.
  • Offer images that display "choose this or that" options for a free gift, list multiple benefits, and showcase the main product prominently are particularly effective.
  • Offer graphics highlight the promotion prominently, with features called out and customer reviews displayed.
  • Promotion ad: name the event and the offer clearly, then add a call to action. A named deadline and a concrete offer make people act during the sale window.
    • Example: "Cyber Monday: buy one desk lamp, get the second free, tap to claim."
    • Example: "Summer clearance: 30% off all bags this weekend only, shop the deal now."

Urgency concept template

1 min 
Urgency concept: "Only 45 left in stock. Sale ends Sunday at midnight." Combine scarcity (limited stock) with a deadline (specific day and time) to push procrastinators over the line.
  • Urgency ads for procrastinators (people who delay buying) and almost-sold buyers (people who nearly bought) use a scarcity indicator (a sign that stock is low) + a specific deadline. Procrastinators need both a reason to act now (a time limit) and proof of consequence (low stock) to overcome delay.
    • Example 1: "Only 22 left. Sale ends Friday midnight."
    • Example 2: "Stock running low: 17 remaining. Offer closes Thursday."

Giveaway ads

1 min
  • Headline pattern: start with WIN, state the prize count as 1 of N (N is the total number of prizes), name the product, and add a clear call to action (CTA, the words or button that tell people what to do), such as Enter Now. The pattern works because a specific prize and a small win ratio (the chance of winning) create curiosity and produce cheap clicks. A disclaimer about the win ratio must still appear on the ad.
    • Example: "WIN 1 of 3 Limited-Edition Hoodies: Enter Now."
    • Example: "WIN 1 of 5 Summer Bundles, Enter Below."
  • Static image ads (still pictures, not moving) work well for giveaway funnels (a path of steps from ad to sale). Run several variations and test them. Use the giveaway for new product drops (launches of new products), because it generates a large amount of awareness (how many people know your brand) at very low CPM (cost per 1,000 views, the price of showing an ad a thousand times).
  • The example giveaway ad carries two calls to action: an entry button linking to the giveaway landing page (the page a click opens), and a separate Shop now button linking to the store. Using both serves entrants and buyers at the same time.

Meme-style and native-looking ads

2 min
  • Meme-style image ads (funny picture style that looks like a normal social post) look native (like normal content) in the Instagram feed (the main scrolling page of Instagram) and feel less like ads, so they tend to earn high attention and click-through rates (CTR, the share of viewers who click). Replace the text with a provocative hook (a strong opening line that grabs attention) or a major product benefit, then swap the image to test new products quickly.
  • Meme ads can hold attention and generate clicks, but in one case they still sold less than user-generated content (UGC, content made by customers). Use them as a testing format and scale (spend more on) the ones that respond well.
  • Meme-style video ads produced an attention rate (the share of people who keep watching) around 60% and a click-through rate around 4% in one case. They were kept running because they improved Google Ads performance (results from Google's ad system), even though they did not show direct Facebook sales. This was observed once and should be checked before relying on it.
  • Static meme-style image ads perform well on Facebook. Build them in Canva (an online design tool) by placing the product image with a short line of text and an emoji (a small picture symbol) on top. Meme-style image ads performed well in a test.
  • Simple image ads: the product photo with a short caption that gives the reason to buy, placed on top of the image.
    • Example 1: "The shoe you wanted, now in your size."
    • Example 2: "Ready for winter nights? Get yours today."
  • Jokes that rely on outdated trends or references lose their effect quickly. Check whether a reference is still current before building a video around it.
  • Trend-format pattern: adapt a popular format (a post style people already know) in which people list what they like about a subject. Then replace the subject with your product and name its main benefits. Viewers already understand the pattern and can focus on the benefits.
    • Example 1: "Things I love about my cordless vacuum: it fits under the couch, it charges in a minute, it finally keeps the stairs clean."
    • Example 2: "Why my commute got easier: the foldable backpack fits a laptop, it dries fast in the rain, and it has a USB port."
  • Story hook pattern: open with a relatable incident (a situation the viewer recognizes), such as a robbery or a theft caught on camera, then cut to the product as the solution. Viewers want to see how the story ends.
    • Example 1: "Someone walked into the garage at 2 am and the owner's first clip was caught by a light bulb camera."
    • Example 2: "A package disappeared from the porch, so the homeowner set up a hidden camera that shows the whole story."
  • Visual pump-and-dump product (a plain, fast product showcase): a plain product showcase with short overlay text (words placed on top of the image or video) and no listicle or advertorial can be enough, since the product itself is the message. Short clips of about 15 to 20 seconds with simple text worked as winners (ads that performed best) in the example.

Listicle, advertorial and editorial formats

4 min
  • Three editorial styles (article-style formats) are common: listicle (a numbered list of benefits), long-form blog-style article, and news-article style with bold claims, big numbers and research data. Choose the style that fits the product and the audience's problem.
  • Listicle pattern: a numbered list of benefits, often 7 or 10 points, about a trending or little-known product. It is framed as why it is taking over or why people should own it, and the product is pitched only at the end. The reader meets neutral content instead of an ad, and the trend framing makes an unknown product worth checking.
    • Example 1: "7 Reasons Compact Ergonomic Mice Are Taking Over Home Offices".
    • Example 2: "10 Things Physiotherapists Wish Their Patients Knew About Morning Back Pain".
  • Blog-style pattern: a long-form article that opens with a relatable character's problem, agitates it (makes the problem feel bigger), explains why common fixes fail, introduces a new approach, and only then presents the product in the last part. Trust grows when the author byline (the author's name shown on the article) shows an expert, such as a doctor or vet, and when photos break up the text.
    • Example 1: "Why Your Sleep Keeps Breaking at 3 AM: What a Sleep Specialist Found in Her Clinic".
    • Example 2: "The Kitchen Habit That Dulls Knives in Weeks: How One Chef Changed Her Routine".
  • News-style pattern: the layout and headline imitate a news article, using a bold claim, large numbers and cited research data (studies quoted as proof), with the product pitched after the story. The reader trusts the apparent reporting and sees the problem as urgent and proven.
    • Example 1: "Study Finds 6 in 10 Remote Workers Lose Focus Before Lunch, and a New Fix Is Gaining Ground".
    • Example 2: "Data From 4,000 Home Gym Owners Shows Why Most Resistance Bands Snap Within a Year".
  • Advertorials (ads written to look like articles) also come in a listicle format: a numbered list of reasons, such as eight or five reasons a product solves a problem. It works like a story-based advertorial: it builds interest first, then bridges the visitor (moves them) to the product page. Problem-solving products, such as toys, suit this format best.
  • Listicle advertorial headline: "Here are [number] reasons why [product] is on [audience]'s top wish lists", followed by a numbered list of points and then a sales page (the page where people buy). The format promises a quick, ordered read that makes the product feel chosen by people like the reader. Test headline variants (different versions of the headline), because the first line decides whether visitors keep reading.
    • Example 1: "Here are 7 reasons why this compact pet-hair roller is on busy dog owners' top wish lists".
    • Example 2: "Here are 5 reasons why this personal alarm keychain is on night commuters' top wish lists".
  • Cold audiences (people who do not know your brand yet): send paid clicks first to editorial-style advertorial pages that look like articles, to warm the audience up before they reach the sales landing page. Then run the sales page with messaging matched to the same audience. Use this when a product needs more explanation or trust than a short ad can give.
  • Physical products are more visual. An image, a short product video or a product explainer that shows the feature, benefit and outcome often converts better (gets more buyers) than a long sales letter. A long-form sales letter fits products that need more explanation, and the right format depends on the market.
  • Dialogue-style videos, where the creator responds to a viewer's comment or promises to feature the top-liked comment, drive strong engagement (likes, comments and shares) and follower growth, especially on new accounts. They usually convert less well than hooks that name a viewer problem and present a product solution right away.
  • A warehouse or behind-the-scenes video showing the brand's own logo on packaging adds proof and trust.
    • Example: "See our packing team ship every order in its branded box."
    • Example: "Watch how we pack your order before it leaves our warehouse."
  • Winner-and-envious-rival scene (an ad that shows a buyer's success next to a jealous peer): show the buyer succeeding in a visible moment while a peer watches with envy. Let the product be the implied reason, without making a claim.
    • Formula: "[Buyer] using [product] in a moment of success, [rival] in the background watching or waiting."
    • Example: "A landscaper unloads a truck with the company name visible while a rival crew sits in a jam behind them."
    • Example: "A home cook serves a tray of bread to friends while a neighbour stands at the door with store-bought loaves."
  • Long-running story case: a Facebook video story of about nine minutes, far beyond the length usually recommended for that platform, was posted with a $187 spend in the first week, then paid promotion stopped. The video was a personal story about a pet and a hard time, filmed on location, with no sales pitch. It reached 2.4 million views, 59,000 likes, 31,000 shares and over 4,000 comments, many of them long personal replies. The creator later noted that deeply emotional storytelling built lasting loyalty that a shorter, safer clip could not match. The same story opened the email sequence (a series of automatic emails) to build a bond with new subscribers.

Image angles for demand generation

1 min

Plain words for the table below:

  • Demand Gen: ads that create interest in products people have not searched for.
  • Editorial flat-lay: a photo taken from above on a neat surface.
  • Negative space: empty room in the picture for text.
  • Side-by-side hierarchy: two items next to each other in a ranked layout.
Angle Goal Directions
Informational Create curiosity without solving the problem Problem-focused (everyday struggle); Clinical/Editorial (anatomy or article style); Curiosity-driven (unusual or intriguing)
Comparison Imply that someone has done the research Editorial flat-lay; Lifestyle evaluation scene; Side-by-side hierarchy
Offer-focused Make the product the hero High-contrast product hero shot with negative space for headline or urgency text

All three angles: photorealistic (looks like a real photo), no heavy filters, no clutter. Only the offer-focused angle carries text on the image.

  • Informational images that worked as headline partners include a medical-textbook style anatomy illustration (a drawing of the body's structure), an X-ray style image, a doctor reviewing anatomy, and a pain-point lifestyle photo (a photo of an everyday problem) that looks like stock imagery (generic photos sold for any use). A headline such as "doctors explaining hidden reasons a treatment fails" pairs well with a clinical (medical-looking) anatomy image.

Retargeting ads

2 min

  • Reminder retargeting ad: a short question that points at what the visitor viewed, followed by a reason to return, such as free shipping. It reminds the visitor of their interest instead of pitching cold.

    • Example 1: "Still thinking it over? Your saved items are waiting, with free shipping today."
    • Example 2: "Left something in your cart? Come back and check out in one click."

  • Informant ad (older advice, 2021-2022; an ad that sends people to a helpful article instead of straight to a sale): promote an existing, valuable article instead of the product directly. Formula: the article's feature image, a headline in the form "Learn how to [main outcome the article delivers]", body copy that names the main problem the reader faces and says the article solves it, and a CTA such as "Read now" for text or "Watch now" / "View now" for video. It works because the ad speaks to a problem the viewer already feels and promises a useful answer rather than a sale.

    • Own examples: "Learn how to stop a squeaky office chair in five minutes" (body: squeaks break your focus at work and the guide shows the three quick fixes; CTA "Read now").
    • Own examples: "Learn how to pick a hiking backpack that fits your torso" (body: most packs sit wrong and cause shoulder pain; CTA "Learn more").

  • Informant ad steps:

    1. Choose the existing content piece the ad will lead to.
    2. Use that content's feature image, checking it is high quality and directly matches the content on the page.
    3. Write a headline that names the main thing the reader will learn from the content.
    4. Write body copy that describes the primary problem the content addresses, so the viewer feels the connection.
    5. Pick the CTA: "Read now" for text content, "Watch now" or "View now" for video, "Learn more" as a general alternative.
    6. Keep the same image, headline and CTA across all placements, changing only the copy length and format per network (per platform).
    7. Set up the ad in the ad platform's interface (the screens where ads are built) once the audiences (groups of people the ad is shown to) exist.

  • The feature image should show people who look like the target audience and be directly relevant to the main problem of the content, so it stands out in a feed. A generic or off-topic image weakens the ad, because it does not signal that the content solves the viewer's problem.

  • Since the content already exists, most of the work is done and the ad takes little time to produce. In practice, one such ad takes around 10 minutes to create, and once the initial audiences are set up the process is quick.

Lead magnet ads

1 min
  • Lead magnet ad (a free item offered in exchange for contact details): name a useful free resource in the headline, add a short benefit line, and end with a button such as "Learn more". The formula sells the free item instead of the product.
    • Example 1: "Free weekly meal plan for busy parents, grab yours here."
    • Example 2: "Free fit guide for trail runners, get it now."

Introduction and owner-led ads

1 min
  • Showing the business owner in an introduction ad (an ad where the owner introduces themselves) builds trust and a personal connection that many e-commerce stores lack, which helps them stand out from competitors. It works best when the owner introduces themselves in their own voice, rather than using a professionally produced ad with no personal link to the business.
  • Avoid phrasing such as "thanks for visiting our site" in retargeting ads, because it feels creepy to visitors. Instead, have the owner introduce themselves and invite people to get in touch.

Introduction ad layout

1 min 
Ad layout for an introduction ad, listing what each field must contain. Image or video: show yourself on camera. If you choose video, say who you are, describe your business and how you assist people, then add a call to action asking the viewer to phone your business. Headline: address the customer in a manner that catches their eye; this can be quite open-ended. Body copy: urge the customer to reach out to your business. With a video ad, simply restate the video's main message. With more room, add a short background story and cover the business, your unique selling point and the way you help people. Call to action: test wording such as "Call Us", "Get In Touch" or "Call Me".
  • If you are dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships, without keeping stock) and cannot film yourself with the products you sell, use a plain image of yourself alone instead of product images. A video with the owner showing the products is even better when possible.
  • Use the body copy (the main text of the ad) to encourage the viewer to contact the business and build the relationship. Experiment with call-to-action wording such as "Call us", "Get in touch" and "Call me".
  • Placements (where the ad appears) for an introduction ad concept:
    • A static Google Display Network remarketing banner (a still image shown on Google's network of websites to people who visited before) with a short text and a call-to-action button.
    • A Facebook news feed video ad.
    • A Google Display leaderboard banner (the wide banner shown at the top of a website).
    • An Instagram video ad with a Contact Us button. The same image and text are reused across placements.

Concept briefs for new-customer ads

3 min
  • Ad concept requirements (applies to at least 5 concepts aimed at new customers who have never heard of the brand; a concept is one complete ad idea):
    • Each concept must contain the following:
      • a name;
      • positioning strategy (how the product is presented: new mechanism, meaning a new way it works; superior product, meaning a better product; or identity marketing, meaning appealing to who the buyer is);
      • format (static image, video, carousel (several images to swipe through) or UGC);
      • primary avatar (a made-up profile of the ideal buyer);
      • key desire or pain point (the main want or problem);
      • 5-7 hook variations using proven patterns (different opening lines that have worked before);
      • visual concept (benefit, problem, product or product in action);
      • ad structure (the order of the parts);
      • full script (the word-for-word text);
      • visual proof elements (images that show the result is real);
      • call to action;
      • urgency element if appropriate (a reason to act now);
      • handling of the top 2-3 objections (reasons a buyer may say no);
      • an explanation of why it works, citing research findings.
  • Each ad concept should focus on one desire or problem only. It must show understanding of the viewer's desires, problems and identity, use language found in the research, pre-empt objections (answer them before they come up), use hooks proven in the niche (the product's market), and create belief through visual proof. It should also stand out from competitor creative (competitors' ads).

Concept architecture (for at least 5 concepts):

  1. Name the concept and target avatar with distress level (how much pain the viewer feels).
  2. Opening emotion (0-3 seconds) and whether it narrows (speaks to one specific person) or broadens (speaks to a wider group) attention, with the reason.
  3. Agitation emotions (feelings that make the problem feel stronger), intensity and duration.
  4. Transition trigger (the moment the ad moves from problem to hope), permission language (words that let the viewer feel okay) and bridge emotion (the feeling that links problem and solution).
  5. Solution emotions and the identity transformation (change in how the viewer sees themselves) promised.
  6. Action emotion (urgency or possibility) and the final push.
  7. 5-7 hook variations, each tagged with its emotion, narrowing or broadening, and the distress level it suits.
  8. Negative label (a bad self-image the viewer may have) addressed and positive label (a better self-image) offered.
  9. Mirror moment within 3 seconds (a moment where the viewer feels seen).
  10. Emotional permission granted.
  11. For high distress: how positive impairment (a technical term, meaning the way the ad works with the viewer's hurt) is handled, how long to stay negative, and what validation comes first (validation means saying the feeling is understood).
  12. Full script with [NARROWING] and [BROADENING] tags and a clearly marked transition.
  13. Visual concept supporting each phase.
  14. Emotional objections and fears addressed.
  15. Why it works, citing research findings and the avatar's profile.

Brief per avatar, in this order:

  1. Concept name.
  2. Emotional architecture: opening emotion (narrowing or broadening), agitation emotions, transition trigger, solution emotions, action emotion.
  3. Five hook options, each tagged with emotion and type.
  4. Identity strategy: negative label addressed, aspirational label offered (a better self-image the viewer can aim for), transition presentation.
  5. Mirror moment within 3 seconds.
  6. For high distress: how long to stay in negative territory, what validation is given and what permission is granted.
  7. Objections pre-empted.
  8. Best format and best time from the data.
  9. Script timing:
    • 0-3 s: hook (emotion)
    • 3-15 s: agitation (emotional development)
    • 15-25 s: transition (permission to hope)
    • 25-45 s: solution (positive emotion building)
    • 45-60 s: CTA (action emotion)

Choosing the format: static, image and video

1 min
  • Static formats (still, non-moving ads) include static image ads, catalog ads (ads that show many products from your product list), advertorials, and listicles. Static image ads are the simplest starting point before custom video production.
  • Test a book with several ad formats: long-form copywriting (long, persuasive written text), native ads (ads that look like normal content), catalyst ads (a format name), image ads and AI UGC ads (ads using AI to make customer-style content). Each format needs heavy customization before the test reaches a usable stage.
  • Video works best for products that need storytelling, use-case demonstration (showing how the product is used) or an emotional or spiritual connection, while simple attractive products can work well with carousel or image ads.
  • Image ads with the offer printed on them (the deal written on the picture) can be sufficient for some products, and they were the strongest creative in one example. Whether a product needs video depends on the product itself.
  • Fashion items can usually be tested with picture ads only, because they do not need video to sell. Compare CTR (click-through rate) and store reaction before adding video.
  • For fashion products targeting an audience aged 45 and over, picture ads (single image, carousel, or slideshow) often outperform video ads. This was observed on fashion products in Australia, so it should be tested rather than assumed.
  • Test social-proof creatives (ads showing what other people think) alongside value-proposition creatives (ads showing what the product gives the buyer), instead of relying on one format.
  • Show the product in use in short video, because motion and experience carry the emotional part of the message.

Testimonial and review ads

1 min

Structure and length

1 min
  • A workable testimonial ad (an ad that uses customer quotes) states the core benefits first and then adds testimonials as proof. Placing the testimonials after the benefit claims lets social proof back up what the ad has already promised.
  • Use no more than three testimonials in a single ad. A short slideshow of three brief testimonials works well, but more than three wastes viewer time. For long, full video testimonials, use only one per ad so the viewer is not bored.
  • Testimonial length determines how many to show: short one-line testimonials can be stacked (placed together), while a long story-style testimonial (a customer describing their full journey) should appear only once in an ad.
  • Testimonial ads can run as static images or as video, and both formats performed fairly well in testing. Animated testimonial sequences, where one quote rolls into the next with subtle movement such as a slight breathing motion, were reported to work well.
  • A pop-up social proof format shows many comments or reviews appearing one after another (pop, pop, pop) until the screen fills. This displays a lot of social proof (signs other people trust it) without boring the viewer. Build it with real or designed comment cards and sound effects in a video editor.

Sourcing and testing

1 min
  • Real customer comments taken from your own store or product page are a lower-risk option than invented comments, because the feedback is genuine. Recreating a comment card by hand takes more design effort, so real feedback is the simpler starting point.
  • Run customer reviews and testimonials as ad creatives on Meta (the company that runs Facebook and Instagram), even when there are only around 11 reviews. Split test (compare two versions to see which wins) two or three review or UGC creatives inside one ad set (a group of ads sharing the same settings).
  • Testimonial and review-style videos work particularly well for retargeting, because viewers have already seen the ads but not acted. Pair them with an offer in the ad copy, headline, or description.

Procedure: learn to produce a testimonial ad

1 min
  1. Find examples from large established brands known to run testimonial ads.
  2. Search for "testimonial ads" in Foreplay (a tool that collects ad examples) and Google.
  3. Study which testimonial ads are clean and well spaced.
  4. Build your own version from these references instead of starting from scratch.

Low-cost creatives

1 min
  • Two low-cost ad creatives need no production team. An ad creative is the picture or video shown in an ad.
  • Type one: a screenshot of the checkout page (the final payment page) showing the discount or savings.
  • Type two: a screen recording of someone browsing the site, with a voiceover (spoken narration added over video) from the customer's point of view.
  • Both can run alongside other creatives in the same test. A test means running several ads at once to see which works best.

Problem and demonstration ads

1 min
  • A problem-scene creative shows the buyer in the moment the problem happens. Example: a rider whose headphones do not fit under a helmet.
  • Then it shows the product solving that problem.
  • Name the specific problem in the ad copy (the written words of the ad) too. The viewer then spots the situation quickly.
  • Ads that open with a relatable before-and-after story about a specific pet problem attract owners in a similar situation. Relatable means people see themselves in it.
  • Emotional storytelling was used to make people feel the problem before the advertorial. An advertorial is an ad made to look like an article.
  • A lifestyle video (a video showing everyday life) in which a creator (a person who makes content) presents a product as their own new tech and shows it in daily use performed strongly organically. Organically means free, not paid for.
  • The video showed the product as a solution in the creator's own routine, not as an advert.

Us-versus-them and comparison ads

1 min
  • An us-versus-them ad puts the product against a clearly weaker alternative, such as an inferior method or a poor competitor.
  • The product looks better by comparison.
  • This works through anchoring. Anchoring means the first thing seen sets the standard for judging the rest.
  • The viewer judges the product against a low benchmark (a standard used for comparison).
  • Example: "Our 3-layer hiking socks vs. cotton socks that soak up sweat"
  • Example: "Our cordless mop vs. a bucket and rag that just spreads grime."
  • Comparison ads can work well. Start by comparing against an everyday alternative, such as a café coffee at about $6 a cup.
  • Do not attack an established brand directly at first.
  • A before-and-after comparison can show the time and effort of a competitor's option. Example: a barista coffee bought on the run, versus a quicker product.
  • This matches the time-saving value lever. A value lever is a reason to buy, here saving time.
  • Direct brand-on-brand comparison (your brand named against another brand) carries pushback risk. Pushback means the other side hits back.
  • Use it only once your brand has enough traction (proof of real sales and trust).
  • Only use it when your brand is ready to answer a counterattack.

Listicle and advertorial ads

1 min
  • A list-style advertorial headline (a headline that promises a numbered list) uses this formula:
    1. Promise a specific count of reasons the product matters.
    2. Tie it to a status signal the reader wants. A status signal is a sign of belonging to a group people want to join.
    3. Present the points as a numbered list before the sales page. The sales page is the page where people buy.
  • Why it works: a numbered promise sets clear expectations.
  • The status tie makes the reader want to see if they belong.
  • The list makes reading easy, so the sales page feels earned.
  • Examples: "7 reasons home bakers are switching to a magnetic spice rack" and "5 reasons outdoor runners keep a reflective vest in their car."

Print and mail formats

1 min
  • Postcards suit consumer mailings (mail sent to shoppers) with simple offers and existing relationships.
  • Postcards are weaker in business-to-business (B2B) mail. Here, gatekeepers (staff who sort the mail) discard B-pile mail (the low-priority pile).
  • Large fold-out self-mailers (printed pieces that open up and mail flat, without an envelope) are useful for longer copy.
  • Roadmap postcards (cards that unfold to about eight pages) are also useful for longer copy.
  • Tear-sheet or advertorial mailing: a piece made to look like a page torn from a newspaper or magazine.
  • It comes with a handwritten sticky note from a friend.
  • People read articles more than ads. So 30 to 50 percent of recipients can start reading such a mailing.
  • A standard sales letter gets about 10 percent.
  • Example: a sheet styled as a magazine article with a sticky note reading "Thought you would want to see this, J."

Lead magnet and reward ads

1 min
  • Ad copy for a lead magnet (a free gift people receive in exchange for their contact details) offers a free, related resource.
  • It does not offer a discount or a product.
  • Formula: free + specific related topic + a short call to action (a short instruction telling the reader what to do), such as "get your copy."
  • Example: "Free guide: how to choose your first dog harness. Get your copy."
  • Example: "Free checklist: the history of camping stoves. Grab yours."
  • Offer a reward item in the ad. A reward item is a gift the prospect (a possible buyer) claims.
  • To claim it, the prospect returns a request form with qualifying questions (questions that show how well someone fits).
  • The answers show which products each responder (each person who replies) is likely to want.
  • They build a targeted list (a list of people with shared interests) for follow-up (contacting people again later).

Giveaways

1 min
  • Giveaway contests (free prize draws) attract people who want free items rather than buyers.
  • Avoid them when the goal is paying customers.

Social proof and post-engagement ads

1 min
  • Social proof means showing other people's positive experiences.
  • Formula for a post-engagement ad (an ad aimed at people who already interacted with a post) that asks for social proof:
    • Say that the customer loves the product.
    • Invite them to comment with a photo or description.
    • Mention a small reward.
    • Keep the request simple for older audiences by asking only for a written comment.
  • Example 1: "Enjoying your new mug? Tell us what you think in the comments and get a free sticker pack."
  • Example 2: "Got a photo with your jacket? Post it in the comments and we will send you a discount code."
  • Post-engagement ads are cheap to run.
  • They can be linked to Minichat (an automatic message tool) for an automatic reward.
  • Successful page posts can be reused in other campaigns as layered social proof. Layered means stacked on top of other proof.
  • Show comments and photos to cold audiences (people who do not know the brand yet), who are then asked to buy.

Trusted advisor and review headline ads

2 min
  • A trusted advisor ad (an ad built around customer reviews) can reuse the product image from the product reminder ad (an ad that reminds people of a product).
  • Reuse the image because the shopper already knows it from the product page.
  • Change the headline (the big top line of an ad), the body copy (the main text under it) and the review, so the ad feels new.
  • Formula for the opening line of a review-based ad: name a group of happy buyers, state what they say about the product, and name the product in the same line.
  • Why it works: the first line is the only copy most viewers see.
  • A clear summary with a named product creates enough curiosity to tap (click) more.
  • Example 1: "Home cooks are raving about the Alpine cast-iron skillet, and it cooks evenly on any stove."
  • Example 2: "Dog owners say the Cloud-soft bed is worth every penny for older pets."
  • Key attributes of a trusted advisor ad:
Element Requirement
Image The best quality image of the product, or an image supplied by a customer showing them with the product
Headline The headline from a strong review, or a summary of one
Body copy At least one review. Where there is more space, as on Facebook ads, add several reviews that highlight different things about the product or the business
Call to action Test Learn More, Read More, Get Yours Today or Shop Now
  • Business-wide review headline: a plain statement of what customers like about buying from the store.
  • Place it before the review text, so the ad is about the business, not one product.
  • Why it works: the shopper learns the ad's point in the first line.
  • Social proof about the seller's service builds trust when product proof is not the main issue.
  • Example 1: "Customers keep coming back because our shipping is fast and our support answers quickly."
  • Example 2: "Buyers tell us they trust our store because returns are simple and the checkout is clear."

Creator and whitelist (native) ads

3 min
  • Whitelist ads run through a person's page instead of the brand page. Native ads look like ordinary posts.
  • Pitch the same product in two voices.
  • The brand voice is a direct, company-led call to buy.
  • The whitelist voice is a first-person usage story (written as "I") that includes a time frame and a result.
  • Why it works: the brand voice informs, while the personal voice reads as a recommendation.
  • Example 1, brand: "Get the new 1 L steel bottle, now in stock."
  • Example 1, whitelist: "I took this 1 L bottle on three 10 km runs this month and it has not leaked once."
  • Example 2, brand: "Our new collagen drink is here, order today."
  • Example 2, whitelist: "I added this collagen drink to my morning routine for 21 days, and my energy stays steady until lunch."
  • Formula for ads run from a character page (a page for a made-up person):
    1. Open with a personal aside, a line that sounds like a real confession or a question the character often gets.
    2. Promise a specific number of tips or steps.
    3. Name the one tool or product the character uses.
    4. Show the character as the sponsor, with a Sponsored label (a tag showing the ad is paid).
    5. Put a destination domain (the web address the ad leads to) under the creative.
    6. Write a headline naming the concrete outcome, and add a Learn More call to action (a button label).
  • Why it works: it reads as content from a person first and an offer second.
  • A concrete promise invites clicks.
  • Example 1: "Honest confession: my knee pain was ignored for two years until I changed one habit with my running shoes. Here are the three checks I run before buying any pair now, plus the insole I still swear by." Headline: "3 Checks Before Buying Running Shoes".
  • Example 2: "Every week someone asks me how I keep my kitchen grease-free without harsh chemicals. Here are four cheap habits that changed how often I clean, plus the one spray I keep on the counter." Headline: "4 Kitchen Habits That Cut My Cleaning Time".
  • Case: a fictional character page started at zero followers.
  • Ads ran from the page for one month, from March to April.
  • The page reached about 1,000 followers organically through the ads.
  • People chose to follow via the ad itself, without buying followers.
  • Zero followers did not stop people from buying.
  • Native ads must always run from a whitelist page, a person's page rather than a brand page.
  • Do not split-test them against a brand page. A split test compares two versions side by side.
  • Running native ads from a branded page ruins the ad.
  • Native ads only work when they look like an ordinary post by a person.
  • Whitelist ad copy is written in an organic, personal voice, not a brand voice.
  • Brand copy uses direct selling, such as "buy today, 50% off, see the reviews."
  • Whitelist copy opens with a personal hook (a first line that grabs attention).
  • Whitelist copy names a question the character is often asked.
  • Whitelist copy promises a short, concrete takeaway and mentions the product in passing.
  • Keep the static ad layout (a still image ad) the same. Swap only the brand name and logo for the character's face.
  • Example 1: "Real talk: I used to wake up exhausted every single day. Here are the 3 wind-down steps I still follow, and the one thing that made the biggest difference."
  • Example 2: "I get DMs about my knife every week, so I finally wrote down why I stopped buying cheap ones. Here is what to check before you spend a dime."

Further details

1 min
  • Post-it Notes Hook Framework (a set of steps for a handwritten attention grabber):
    1. Create a handwritten hook on a physical post-it note.
    2. Record a 15-30 second video showing the post-it with the product and its benefits.
    3. Use the personal feel of handwriting and the uncommon format to stand out.
  • Handwritten post-it hooks are effective because audiences respond to the personal feel of handwriting.
  • The format is uncommon and memorable.
  • A 15-30 second video is short enough to hold attention while showing core benefits.
