Customer research and voice of customer

Section 12.2 · 2 of 26 in this chapter · 20 min read

In short

  • Copy buyers' exact words from reviews, forums, surveys, and complaints. Reuse those words in your ads.
  • Ask buyers 3 questions after they buy. Their answers are the best source for ad ideas.
  • Count repeats. When about 20 people say the same thing, give it more space in your ads.
  • Never paste review text into your store as if your own customers wrote it.
  • Ask buyers why they bought, and write down their exact words.
More key points
  • Use those exact words in your ads, headlines, and sales page.
  • Ask customers for reviews 28 days after the order.
  • Do not guess what customers want. Ask them.
This section has 12 parts. Tap one to open it.

Where buyer language lives

2 min
  • Buyer language sits in reviews, forums, community threads, surveys, social comments, service emails, calls, and the publications your buyers read.
  • Collect exact words, phrases, and slang from all of these. Reuse them in your copy.
  • Scan forums, Amazon product pages, competitor ads, and competitor websites.
  • For each audience, record:
    • who they are,
    • what problems they solve,
    • The context in which they face the problem: when, where, and why.
  • Record the language and terminology they use. Note what they reveal about your product and competitors.
  • Reviews reveal the exact words customers use to describe benefits and complaints.
  • Customer objections (reasons people hesitate to buy) are best found in complaints, comments, and feedback from people who viewed the website but did not buy.
  • Their reasons for not buying show which messages and proof the ads must address.
  • In a crowded category (many similar products), customer language can reveal your biggest competitor.
  • Recurring complaints, such as constantly buying replacement paper, can become a copy angle (the main message of an ad). This angle positions your product against the existing solution.
  • Review snapshots from a TikTok Shop listing (a product page on TikTok's shop) can be used as research input. For example, use them to find the main pain points buyers mention.
  • Use them to shape copy. Do not paste review text into the store as if it came from your own customers.
  • Use the wording your target customer uses and relates to. The reader then feels the writer speaks their language.
  • Harmony between reader and writer is what lets the persuasive message come through.
  • When you have only a zip code or thin data about a customer group, research them in this order:
    1. Use your own customer records and mailing list vendor data (data a list company holds about people: age, income, hobbies, politics, magazines read).
    2. If that is missing, drive slowly through the neighbourhood on several different days to get a feel for the residents.
    3. For business buyers, attend their meetings and read their trade journals (magazines for one industry).
    4. Read several years of back issues of the trade publications they subscribe to.
    5. Talk to someone in the industry over lunch and ask questions.
    6. Spend time where they gather, such as trade conventions or hotel bars, and listen to their conversations.

Reddit Answers research

1 min
  • Reddit Answers is a platform for asking real customer questions and getting verbatim voice-of-customer data. "Verbatim" means word for word. "Voice-of-customer data" means what customers say in their own words.
  • Use the six standard research questions to extract fears, failed methods, emotional drivers, and the exact language buyers use.
  • Ask exactly six research questions, one at a time. Replace [SITUATION], [PROBLEM], or [CATEGORY] with your own:
    1. What's so hard about [SITUATION]?
    2. Why do people struggle with [PROBLEM]?
    3. What have people tried for [PROBLEM] that didn't work?
    4. What finally worked for people with [PROBLEM]?
    5. Why do people give up on [PROBLEM]?
    6. What do people dislike about [CATEGORY]?
  • Copy every complete answer into one document without picking and choosing. The prompts will sort the signal (the useful part).
  • Copy whole answers word for word, not cherry-picked snippets (small picked-out pieces), into your research document. The prompts that follow sort the signal from the full data and extract the useful pieces for avatar building. An avatar is a made-up profile of one typical buyer.

Use this prompt on the collected research. Plain words: a persuasion angle is one main message, and a mechanism is the reason the product works.

From customer voice data, identify the awareness stage the buyer occupies using Schwartz's framework, trace the underlying emotional fear and whether it points toward future dread or present shame, document what previous solutions failed them, pinpoint the emotional moments that trigger purchase intent, compile their exact words about the problem and desired outcome, and rank distinct persuasion angles (each pairing a fear with a mechanism) by how strongly the data supports each.

Amazon review mining

2 min
  • Community Arbitrage Method:
    1. Go to amazon.com's book section, select your niche, sort by bestsellers, and find a book related to your product.
    2. Read the top reviews sorted by "most helpful". Highlight points that reflect the reader's inner dialogue (what they tell themselves) in yellow. Highlight emotional triggers (words that cause a strong feeling) in red.
    3. Extract the essence of these points and convert them into bullet-point benefits.
    4. Combine them into a complete marketing piece: headline, sub-headline bridge (a line that links the headline to the rest), bullet points (benefits), and a hard call to action (a clear, direct request to buy).
  • To mine product copy and marketing angles from Amazon:
    1. Search Amazon for products in your niche and open the best-selling listings.
    2. Read the five-star reviews, which tend to describe in detail what buyers value and why.
    3. Write down the specific phrases, problems, and benefits buyers mention.
    4. Rephrase the strongest angles in your own words and use them in headlines, benefit lines, and product descriptions.
  • Mine market angles from Amazon reviews:
    1. Open the Amazon listings of competing products in the niche.
    2. Read the five-star reviews, which give detailed descriptions of the benefit.
    3. Write down the phrases and marketing angles customers use.
    4. Rephrase the angles in your own words.
    5. Put the rephrased angles into headlines and bullet points on the store.
  • Read five-star Amazon reviews to learn customer language, pain points (problems), and copy angles.
  • Use amazon.fr for France and amazon.com otherwise. These reviews are often descriptive and useful for ad copy.
  • Use ChatGPT's web browsing (letting it read web pages) and Amazon review analysis to get audience ideas.
  • Validate them against real data, because the browsing tool may fail to read some pages.
  • Turning reviews into ad ideas works best on a recurring schedule, such as weekly, so ads keep speaking in customers' own words:
    1. Scrape (automatically copy) customer reviews of your product and of competing products on a schedule.
    2. Have the AI summarise (shorten) what customers say about your product and the alternatives.
    3. Turn those summaries into ad ideas written in the customers' language.

Review-to-audience workflow with ChatGPT

1 min
  • ChatGPT can turn customer reviews into audience profiles and ad copy when fed in batches (small groups at a time).
  • Workflow:
    1. Export the product's Amazon reviews with a review export browser extension (an add-on for your web browser that saves reviews to a file).
    2. Start a new chat and give ChatGPT the product title and description, asking it only to digest and understand the information.
    3. Feed about 30 reviews per message, since more than about 50 reviews breaks the chat.
    4. Ask ChatGPT to identify the target audience segments in terms of the four pillars: demographics (age, place, and similar facts), psychographics (values, beliefs, and lifestyle), behaviours (what people do), and needs.
    5. Continue feeding batches of 30 reviews (30, 60, 90) and repeat the analysis.
    6. Check the output yourself against forums and Amazon, since AI output may miss context.
    7. Use the segments to write ad copy and creatives (ad visuals and videos) for each audience.

Building the research base

2 min
  • Compile all research in one Google Sheet (an online spreadsheet).
  • List fears, challenges, pain points, needs and desires, core beliefs, objections, objection-related mind states, motivators, and lifestyle influences per customer archetype (a type of buyer).
  • Add forum, blog, and article findings about the problem, current market solutions, and how big the problem is.
  • Check these psychology angles for each customer archetype and collect them in the research sheet:
Angle category Examples to capture
Fears Fears about own situation or about dependants (e.g. a parent's fears for a child)
Challenges Current challenges the customer faces
Pain points Present problems the customer experiences
Needs Functional or emotional needs
Desires Wants and deep desires related to the product
Core beliefs Underlying convictions of the customer
Objections Reasons customers hesitate to buy
Mind states Emotional state of the customer
Motivators What pushes the customer to act
Lifestyle Lifestyle influences on buying
  • Build an emotional language library.
  • Collect at least 20 sourced phrases (taken from real customers) for each of these emotions: fear, shame, guilt, anger, helplessness, grief, hope, relief, pride, and belonging.
  • Aim for at least 200 phrases in total.
  • For each emotion, also record:
    • signal words (words that show the feeling),
    • sentence patterns (how sentences are built),
    • metaphors (comparisons, such as "drowning in bills"),
    • typical questions,
    • patterns such as "should have", "if only", "I can't", "nothing works", "I used to", "maybe", "finally", "no longer", and "people like me".
  • Feedback from surveys and comments should be combined and sorted by avatar.
  • Ask Claude to group answers by avatar, since each group reveals different objections and hooks (opening lines that grab attention).
  • The more feedback loops (rounds of collecting feedback) you collect, the easier it is to map a clear marketing angle for each audience.
  • Typeform (an online form tool) can separate qualified from unqualified leads (people likely to buy from people who are not).
  • Use rating scales from 1 to 10 to measure emotional attachment to the topic.
  • Segment responders (sort the people who answered) by their answers and show dynamic results (results that change based on the answers).
  • Use Barnum statements (general statements that feel personal to almost anyone) in the results to build familiarity and validate readers.
  • The link link.sean.co/typeform gives 10% off.

Post-purchase surveys

4 min
  • Asking buyers after purchase for their own words gives ad copy and testimonials (customer praise) that match how real customers describe their problem and your brand.
  • Keep the answers exact, because precise phrasing is what makes copy persuasive.
  • Post-purchase survey answers are more reliable for ad research than guessing or brainstorming.
  • Respondents have already spent money and can state why they bought, what problem they had, and what result they want.
  • Ask only three questions so buyers finish the survey.
  • Q3 (the problem in the buyer's own words) is the most important question for ad research.
  • Post-purchase survey, shown after checkout (the payment step). All three questions are open-text (buyers type a free answer), with one question per slide (one question per screen):
Q1: What finally made you decide to buy [product]?

Q2: Describe [the person or situation the product is for] and the main thing you hope will change.

Q3: Before you found [product], how would you describe the problem in your own words?

Closing line: Your answers are anonymous. Thank you!
  • Use the survey to collect material for ad copy, testimonials, and UGC scripts (UGC means user-generated content: videos and posts made by regular customers).
  • Send it after the buyer has bought. Ask the questions in the listed order.
  • Fill the placeholders with the brand, the problem, and the category behaviour.
  • Keep the buyer's answers exactly as given, without edits.
Run a post-purchase survey to collect material for ad copy, testimonials and UGC scripts. Send it after the buyer has bought, ask the questions in the listed order, fill the placeholders with the brand, the problem and the category behaviour, and keep the buyer's answers exactly as given, without any edits.
  • Q1 reveals the purchase trigger (the event that made them buy). It can be a review, a recommendation, a discount, or fear that the problem will get worse.
  • Look for repeated triggers and use them in offers and ad angles.
  • Q2 reveals the outcome the buyer wants, such as less stress, better behaviour, or more confidence.
  • Buyers buy the outcome. The product is only the vehicle (the tool that gets them there), so sell the outcome in ads.
  • Adapt Q2 to the buyer's situation while keeping its structure: who the product is for, plus the main change they hope for. For example, a kids' book asks about the child.
  • Buyers describe their problem in plain everyday words, and marketers rarely use those words.
  • Use the exact phrases from survey answers in hooks, headlines, and copy, instead of clinical or marketing terms.
  • also supports the principle that copy in the buyer's language sounds like a customer, not like the seller.
  • Using a post-purchase survey for ad research:
    1. Add a post-purchase survey in Zigpoll (a survey app for online stores).
    2. Collect a useful number of responses, for example 50, 100, or 200+.
    3. Export the responses.
    4. Paste the responses into Claude and add them to the project knowledge (the files the AI keeps as reference for later questions).
    5. Ask Claude to find pain points, desires, and objections from the responses.
  • Launch-to-script workflow:
    1. Launch the product and install the post-purchase survey app on the store.
    2. Ask the three survey questions after purchase.
    3. Collect responses until you have a usable number, around 50, 100, or 200+.
    4. Export the responses as a CSV file (a spreadsheet saved as plain text) from the survey tool.
    5. Upload the CSV to a Claude project's knowledge.
    6. Ask Claude for pain points, desires, emotions, repeated phrases, and ad angles, then pick the strongest unique angle.
    7. Write the 90-second script (the spoken words for a video) from that angle.
    8. Build hooks, headlines, and ad copy from the buyers' own phrases.
  • Use the top Zigpoll answers as the basis for new creatives: image ads, UGC scripts, and advertorials (ads written like articles).
  • Surveys work best as the source for advertorials and statistic-based image ads.
  • Survey question set for use-case research:
Survey question 1: Why did you decide to buy this product?
Survey question 2: How do you plan to use it?
Ask both questions in a post-purchase survey and collect answers from 30 to 50 buyers. Group repeated answers into use cases, and turn the most common use cases, which may be ones you did not expect, into new angles for videos and creatives.
  • Case: a team surveyed buyers on why they bought and how they used the product. This gave them 30 to 50 different angles.
  • Ten to fifteen of those answers shared one use case (a way of using the product) that the team had not known about.
  • The team used it in videos and creatives, with strong results.

Reading post-purchase answers

4 min
  • Post-purchase survey answers from buyers can be used to read the following signals. Each answer type has its own use. Sensory language means words about what people feel, see, hear, or smell. Dosing means how much of the product to use. De-positioning means showing why rivals fall short.
Answer about What it reveals How to use it in copy
Why the previous solution failed Which alternatives buyers already tried, what was missing in them, and how aware the market is of the solution type An "others have failed you" angle that compares your product against the buyer's past experience
What was happening just before they searched The triggering event and the breaking-point moment A before-state hook and a catalyst story that shows the moment the buyer decided to change
Physical, emotional, and relational symptoms Sensory language that body copy needs The buyer's exact words for these symptoms in the first three seconds of an ad, so the viewer feels understood immediately
Tried alternatives that did not help Competitor weaknesses in the buyer's own words, and how sophisticated the buyer already is De-positioning of competing authorities and an explanation of why the buyer is still searching
The decisive reason they chose this brand (mechanism, guarantee, specific review, dosing, or angle) What actually closes sales Lead every ad with this trigger, because it is the point that converted the buyer
Whose fault the problem is The external force the buyer already blames Shift the blame toward that same target, because the audience already agrees with it
How they want to feel and who they imagine becoming Desired identity and self-image Positive future-state copy and the wording of the call to action
First noticeable change after starting the product The first tangible result, the symptom that moved first, and the timeframe in the buyer's words Week 1 to 2 testimonial copy and proof of the mechanism in a before-and-after story
What most people do not understand or talk about Hidden pain, a desire the buyer cannot yet say bluntly Copy that names something most ads in the category avoid saying
What almost stopped them from buying Objections and gaps in understanding or in the marketing Answer each objection directly in the copy, because these doubts keep other buyers from converting
  • Use exact phrases from Q3 answers in hooks (opening lines that grab attention) and headlines.
  • Analysis prompt for the export:
You have an export of post-purchase survey answers from buyers of [product] in this project. Read the full file, then group the answers by the three questions: purchase trigger (Q1), desired outcome (Q2), and the problem in the buyer's own words (Q3). For each group, list the most common themes with the number of responses that mention each one, and quote the exact phrases buyers used most often without rewording them. Then list the customer emotions you see, the top 5 phrases that repeat across responses, and the strongest ad angles, each with a one-line reason. Finally, state which awareness stage the buyers appear to be in: unaware, problem-aware, or solution-aware.
  • If the analysis returns zero for keyword counts, the keywords are probably spread across different columns in the CSV.
  • Ask the AI to pull the most frequent verbatim phrases instead of counting keywords.
  • Ad-gap prompt:
From my survey, what is a strong ad angle for [product], and what is the buyer's most repeated phrase? Then, from my survey, what is missing in my current ad [paste ad text] that I could add? Quote the survey answers you use as evidence.
  • Case: a children's parenting book sold in a Shopify store (an online store platform). The seller ran a post-purchase survey with three questions:
    • what finally made the buyer purchase,
    • what the buyer hopes changes for the child,
    • how the buyer would describe the problem before finding the product.
  • The 312 responses were uploaded to a Claude project and analysed.
  • Problem answers grouped into these themes:
    • feeling like a failing parent (69 mentions),
    • tantrums, meltdowns, and explosions (63),
    • defiance and not-listening power struggles (62),
    • specific daily flashpoints (trouble spots), such as the morning routine and a two-hour bedtime battle (repeated),
    • secondary themes: child anxiety (about 22) and walking on eggshells (about 14).
  • Desired outcomes:
    • help the child calm down and self-regulate (43),
    • less conflict and fewer battles (about 34),
    • cooperation (about 24).
  • Purchase triggers:
    • price or bundle value (43),
    • a recommendation or review (23),
    • a final-straw incident (19),
    • desperation after trying everything (17).
  • The analysis chose the feeling of self-blame as the angle. Competing ads only address the child's behaviour. Buyers privately judged themselves as failing and believed nothing had worked.
  • A 90-second talking-head script (a person speaking to the camera) followed:
    • a hook about the parent's late-night thought,
    • a reframe that the failure was a missing tool rather than a willpower problem,
    • The product named at 0:52,
    • proof and relief at 1:08,
    • a call to take a look at 1:22.
  • No sales results were recorded for this example.

Sample sizes and thresholds

1 min

Different sample sizes apply to different steps. Use them as follows:

Threshold Applies to Source
About 20 respondents mentioning the same thing Treat as a recurring theme and give that point more space in ads
50, 100, or 200+ responses Total usable survey responses before analysis
30 to 50 buyers Use-case survey: group repeated answers into use cases
30 to 50 different angles Collected in the use-case survey before grouping
  • When many respondents mention the same pain point or desire, for example 20 people saying the same thing, give that point more space in your ads.
  • Repeated phrases across answers show which messages to lead with.
  • When about 20 responses mention the same thing, treat it as a recurring theme.
  • Repeated phrases across responses are where the strongest angles usually come from.

Customer calls, emails, and recorded conversations

2 min
  • Questions to ask prospects (people who might buy) on a call or by email, in this order:
1. What made you start looking for a solution like this?
2. Why do you use what you use now? What issues do you have with it?
3. What have you already tried, and what happened?
4. What result would make this worth it for you?
5. Which feelings do you want to have when this problem is solved?
6. What is holding you back from deciding today?
7. What would make you confident this is the right choice?
8. What would you want to hear from someone who already solved this?
  • Buyer interview questions (questions for people who already bought):
1. What led you to buy this?
2. When did you actually make the decision to buy?
3. At what point did you conclude that I was the right person or company to learn from or buy from?
4. Was there a particular piece of content or sales page that persuaded you?
5. What emotional outcome were you hoping this product would give you?
6. Which feelings pushed you toward buying?
7. What other products like this have you purchased in the past, and what did you appreciate or dislike about them?
8. What outcome were you hoping to achieve with this product?
  • Email template (a ready message you copy and change) asking why they chose the product:
Hi there, one quick question: what made you decide on [PRODUCT NAME]? Knowing this helps me serve you and the rest of our customers better. Simply reply to this email and tell me.

A reply can be as short as a phrase like "great price," or as long as you want. Each response helps us make our offer better.
  • Find the click moment (the moment that made a buyer say yes) for your buyers:
    1. Contact recent buyers. Ask why they bought and when they decided.
    2. Ask which piece of content, email, or sentence first brought them into your world.
    3. This counts even if it was not a sales message.
    4. Record each answer. Group the moments that repeat.
    5. Build your next campaign around the most common moment. Keep producing that content.
  • Every buyer has a click moment. It can happen minutes or months before the purchase. Often the buyer does not know what tipped them over, so ask them directly.
  • Turn recorded sales calls into sales copy (the words that sell) in this order:
    1. Record calls with prospects and customers, with their permission.
    2. Have each recording transcribed (written down word for word).
    3. Mark the sentences where buyers describe their problem, their desired outcome, or what they tried before.
    4. Arrange the marked sentences in a logical order. Add short linking words so the text reads smoothly.
    5. Open with the buyer's own words. Then add the offer and the close (the final push to buy).
    6. Test the page against your current page before you send more visitors (traffic) to it.

Building a list of exact customer language

1 min
  • Build a list of the exact words, phrases, and slang (informal words) your buyers use. Add emotional trigger phrases (words that stir strong feelings). Collect them from reviews, forums (online discussion boards), community threads, surveys, and social comments. Then reuse them in your copy.
  • Copy that repeats the customer's own words feels effortless to read. A reader who sees their thoughts written down is more likely to trust the message.
  • Using the wrong jargon (industry terms outsiders do not know) signals that the seller does not understand the market. Buyers may reject the message immediately. Learn the correct terms before you write.

Turning customer language into ad material

2 min

  • Strong hooks (opening lines that grab attention) borrow the exact language customers use when they describe a problem in forums, reviews, and comments. Only people living that problem would use those words, so they feel immediately recognised.

  • Ad copy (the words in an ad) should reuse the exact questions, language, and concerns your audience uses in forums and communities. Collect these phrases during research. Use them in headlines (big title lines) and hooks.

  • Reuse the customers' exact phrasing from research for hooks, headlines, and primary text (the main body of an ad).

  • Customers describe their problems in simpler, more emotional language than marketers do. That language converts better (gets more buyers) in ads. Copy the exact phrases from survey answers. Reuse them as hooks, headlines, and ad copy. Ads built on what marketers guess customers care about often miss the real pain (the real problem).

  • Build hooks from what buyers say, not from marketer assumptions. Writing ads based on what you think customers want, instead of asking them, leads to guessing and weak messaging. Survey data removes the guessing.

  • Problem-solution leads (opening text that names a problem, then offers a solution) start from the prospect's most pressing worry. Name that worry precisely and tie it to emotional weight (the strong feeling behind it). Do this before offering hope. Good worries are found by listening to customers in service emails, calls, forums, and the publications they read. Also note the patterns in what they repeat.

  • Example: for men in their mid-50s to 60s with savings to invest, common worries in financial publishing (newsletters and publications about money) include:

    • running out of money before running out of life
    • bills in retirement after paychecks stop
    • inflation (prices rising, so savings buy less) shrinking savings
    • taxes
    • missing a big opportunity

    These worries are useful starting points for problem research in a retirement or investing niche (a small, specific target group). Name the most pressing one precisely in the opening.

  • Reuse the phrasing in copy like this: buyers describe problems in plain words. Match that language in the first lines of every ad.

  • Older advice from Source B (2021 to 2022): producing content by guessing what customers want, putting random material together, and hoping something fits, wastes time. Content built on documented (written down) customer problems is what makes content valuable. It also improves conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).

Testimonials and review automation

1 min
  • Set LUX Reviews (a review app) to request reviews 28 days after the order. By then the product has arrived and fulfilment (packing and shipping the order) is complete.
  • Collect testimonials (written praise from customers) like this:
    1. First decide the story you want the customer to tell.
    2. Then ask 3 to 5 open-ended questions (questions that need more than yes or no) that guide them to that story.
    3. Example questions:
      • "Did you make a lot of money thanks to [seller]?"
      • "How much money and how fast did you do it?"
      • "How did you do it?"
      • "Would you recommend [seller]'s service?"
