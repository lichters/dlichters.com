Video ad scripts and creator briefs

Section 12.16 · 16 of 26 in this chapter · 24 min read

In short

  • Write the ad script before you film, find clips or edit.
  • Make the first 3 to 5 seconds stop people from scrolling past.
  • Most ads should be 20 to 60 seconds long.
  • Show the problem first, then the product as the fix.
  • Show proof first: a before and after picture makes people believe the result.
More key points
  • Show each feature on screen, and say its benefit out loud.
  • Plan every scene on paper before filming.
  • End with a clear call to action, but test it per product, because it can lower views.
  • Reuse the same intro to make daily production faster.

Script-first rules and priorities

2 min
  • Write the ad script before you film, brief creators or find clips. Creative testing (trying several ad versions to see which works) needs written scripts.
  • Ads made by sending products to creators, or by filming with no creative strategy (a plan for what the ad says and why), do not work well.
  • Write the script as a story: problem, agitation (making the problem feel worse) and solution. Do this before editing, so the message guides the edit.
  • In testing, the message matters more than whether the ad uses images or video.
  • Decide the target audience and the script first. An ad built without them becomes irrelevant.
  • These decisions come before you find clips or edit.
  • Build a video ad in this order:
    1. Define the target audience and the story or script.
    2. Find clips that match each scene of the script.
    3. Sort the clips into story order.
    4. Cut the clips together to get the structure right.
    5. Add the extras: transitions (moves between clips), sound effects, voiceover (a voice speaking over the video) and text overlays (words shown on screen).
  • Before filming, define five things:
    • What the product is.
    • What it does.
    • Its unique features.
    • How it helps the buyer.
    • Who the target audience is.
  • Write the script from these points. Every shot should show a specific feature or benefit.
  • Do not send a video creative service only the product name and ask for an ad. This usually gives a very poor result.
  • Write a detailed script first, using copywriting (words written to sell) principles. Send that script with your brief.
  • Do not film the A-roll (the main footage of a person talking) without a recorded script. Without it, the footage has no timing reference and the edit becomes guesswork.
  • Choose the format before you write. A talking-head script (a person speaks straight to the camera) works for both video sales letters (long sales videos) and UGC-style ads.
  • Before filming a sales video, write the full script first.
  • Treat the script as copy. Apply the same single-action direct response approach to it. Direct response means the ad asks for one clear action now.
  • Apply the priority system below when you write scripts.
Follow a hierarchical priority system when writing scripts: voice and tone rank highest and govern word choice, structure and beat sequence rank second and govern composition, a hard blacklist of forbidden patterns ranks third and requires self-auditing before delivery, the avatar ranks fourth and defines the audience, and psychology frameworks rank fifth and inform the thinking behind the other four. For each assignment, identify the structural mode needed, lock the beat sequence, verify the angle addresses the right avatar fear, write the full script applying voice and psychology together, audit against the blacklist, then flag any conflicts with the reference documents.
  • Read the priority list in order. Voice and tone come first. The avatar (your typical buyer) comes fourth. Psychology (why people buy) comes fifth.
  • Check the script against the blacklist (banned phrases and patterns) before you deliver it.

Core ad structures

5 min

Hook, middle, call to action and endcart

A hook is the opening that grabs attention. A call to action (CTA) tells people what to do next. An endcart is the final screen of the ad.

  • Use this four-part framework:
    1. Hook with a strong problem or product angle (the main reason to buy).
    2. Solve the problem in the middle. Add more features.
    3. Include one clear call to action.
    4. Use the endcart to show colors, sizes, before-and-after pictures, offers or other sales details.

Facebook video, 30 to 45 seconds

  • Hook: the first 3 to 5 seconds. This is the most important part. It must stop the scroll.
  • Product introduction: the next 3 to 5 seconds. Show the product in hand, or a quick unboxing (opening the box), right after the pain point (a problem the buyer feels), claim or suspense.
  • Middle section: about 15 to 20 seconds. Cut it into clips of 2 to 3 seconds each. Change the camera angle or scene every 2 to 3 seconds. Show uses and benefits.
  • Proof: the last 5 to 6 seconds of the middle. Use reviews or testimonials (a customer saying the product worked).
  • Ending: a benefit-driven call to action, such as "shop now" or a website link.
  • A hand holding the product right after the hook makes viewers feel the product is already theirs. This makes the introduction more convincing.
  • A quick unboxing shot works for the same reason.

Classic product video, 30 seconds or less

Use this when no UGC is available and you use existing stock clips. Keep the total length to 30 seconds or less.

  • Hook (first 3 to 5 seconds, scroll stopper): a bold claim, bold headline, pain point or suspense in a caption (text on screen).
  • Product introduction (next 3 to 5 seconds): a POV shot (point-of-view, the camera shows what a person sees) or the product in hand or in use. The viewer should feel they are receiving the product.
  • Benefit body (about 10 to 15 seconds): change clips every 2 to 3 seconds. Use a benefit caption or a product-in-use shot. Add review clips and feature captions to build trust.
  • Ending: a benefit-driven call to action.

Six-part ad order with call to action

  1. Hook in the first 3 to 5 seconds. Stop the scroll by showing the product in action with something shocking, unexpected or UGC.
  2. Educate. Show what the product is and how it works.
  3. Solve. Show how the product fixes the viewer's specific problem.
  4. Benefits. State the results the viewer gets.
  5. Features. Show concrete product details, such as fit, materials or uses.
  6. Call to action in the last 3 to 5 seconds. Tell viewers to tap the Shop Now button. Show the store URL (web address) in the closing frame.

Problem-solution video

  • A problem-solution video converts better than a video that is mostly talk or a giveaway (a free-prize post). It shows the product doing its job.
  • The best order is: problem first, solution second, product in use third.
    1. Open with a visible problem in the first seconds.
    2. Build tension around the problem. For example, show a risk such as slipping or failure.
    3. Reveal the solution. It is the product.
    4. Show what the product does. Use zoom-ins (the camera moves closer) and close shots so the result is easy to see.
  • For a problem-solution video:
    1. Show the problem clearly, so viewers recognise it.
    2. Introduce the product as the solution.
    3. Add voiceover or captions if they help the message land.
  • Short social video needs storytelling. Social video means short clips on social apps.
  • The video must do one of two things. It must promise a clear takeaway in the first few seconds. Or it must tell a story viewers want to watch to the end.
  • Short clips that only say "check the caption" fail to hold viewers.
  • The key skill is finding creators who can carry these stories.

Blunt sequence

Blunt means direct, with no soft words.

  1. Open with a blunt statement that stops the scroll.
  2. Name a problem the viewer may never have questioned.
  3. Push on pride or identity, so the problem feels worse.
  4. Label the viewer with the identity they want, such as being tough or a real man.
  5. Show the product and its key specification (its key measurement or detail).
  6. Show the ingredients or features that matter and what they do.
  7. Show how the product is used.

Shock demonstration with fixed close

  • Formula: open each ad with a short, startling demonstration. It must show the central idea. Then repeat the same closing sentences in every version.
  • The shock grabs emotion. The fixed close keeps the selling message the same in every version.
  • Example A: a ball hits a protective shield. Then the same closing line about protection is used in every version.
  • Example B: a stain lifts in one second. Then the same claim sentence is used in each ad.
  • If the main claim can be shown on camera, start with a live demonstration. For example, show water resistance on a phone screen protector.
  • Visible tests stop viewers fast. They show the benefit without a long explanation.
  • A simple 16-second ad with background music, a clear problem solved and a call to action worked well for a low-cost household product.
  • Small problems can still work, as long as the benefit is clear in the first few seconds.

Category examples

  • Pain-relief product: show how the device works on the body. Then present it as the solution to back pain.
  • Name the benefit: use it at home, without a therapist.
  • Close the middle with features, such as a comfortable fit for working, resting or playing.
  • Pet product: show a behavior problem, such as separation anxiety (a dog that is distressed when left alone). Then show the product as the solution.
  • State the benefit: fewer anxiety signs when the owner is away.
  • List the features: soft faux fur, a raised outer rim and a spot that suits sleeping.

Problem-agitate-solution and UGC narrative

2 min

Problem-agitate-solution script prompt

A prompt is the exact instruction you give an AI or writer. Fill the square brackets with your own facts.

Write a problem-agitate-solution video ad script and scene suggestions for [PRODUCT: product name and what it does], based only on the information I have given you: [FEATURES AND BENEFITS], [PERSONA: demographics, behavior, pains and fears], [DESIRE the customer wants to reach or escape], [MARKET AWARENESS STAGE: unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware or most aware] and [MARKET SOPHISTICATION STAGE 1 to 5]. Structure the script as follows: in the first scene, name the problem; then agitate it by spelling out its consequences and how it makes the customer feel; then present the product as the solution; then intensify each benefit by saying what it means for the customer. For every scene give the visual, the spoken or on-screen line, and the approximate length in seconds. End with a call to action, and mention [OFFER] only if it is real and has a true deadline or stock limit. Keep the language plain, specific to the persona, and free of vague phrases.
  • A persona is your typical buyer, with a made-up name and life.
  • The market awareness stage is how much the buyer knows about their problem and about products.
  • The market sophistication stage (1 to 5) is how many similar products the buyer has already seen.

UGC narrative formula

  • Show a problem the viewer feels. Make it feel urgent. Present the product as the solution. Then show the result they want.
  • This works because it follows a story shape people already know and remember.
  • In UGC scripts, treat the problem as a villain the viewer must fight. This makes the story more gripping and the solution more appealing.
  • Outline the problem. Make it urgent. Present the product as the answer. Show the result as the payoff.
  • Kitchen gadget example: a sticky counter makes the host feel embarrassed when guests arrive. The gadget removes the mess in seconds. The closing shot shows the host proudly hosting friends.
  • The shift from embarrassment to pride is what triggers the purchase.
  • Back pain product example: a person cannot sit through a workday because of pain. The product gives relief on the commute. The end shot shows them playing with their kids on the floor.
  • It turns a physical complaint into a clear emotional payoff.

Talking point for creators

  • Formula: Show how [product] [key benefit], making [everyday moment] feel [emotional result].
  • Name the feature or news the video must show. Then describe the tone and the feeling the viewer should get.
  • This gives the creator a clear point to hit and a mood to film for, without a shot list (a list of every camera shot).
  • Example 1: Show how our quiet, compact air purifier keeps a bedroom fresh overnight, making every morning feel like a reset.
  • Example 2: Show how our lightweight travel backpack fits a full week of clothes, making every trip feel effortless.

UGC testimonial script, 30 to 60 seconds

Use this for a skincare stick or a similar product. A real person films it on a phone, in natural light, with no studio set.

Fill every placeholder in square brackets. Do not add claims the product cannot back up.

Hook (0-3 seconds): Speak straight into the camera to the target buyer, for example: [PRODUCT FORMAT] in a stick? I did not believe it at first either, and I am [TARGET IDENTITY, e.g. a woman in her forties with fine lines].

Proof (3-8 seconds): Show a before and after image or clip and state the result you saw: [SPECIFIC RESULT, e.g. less hollow under-eye darkness] in [TIMEFRAME, e.g. three weeks].

Problem (8-15 seconds): Name the exact problem the viewer already has: [PROBLEM, e.g. smoker lines around the mouth or dark hollow under-eyes], and say how long you struggled with it: [DURATION].

Insecurity (15-20 seconds): Admit the personal feeling behind the problem: [INSECURITY, e.g. feeling older than you are in photos].

Authority (20-25 seconds): Mention your credible role naturally: [ROLE, e.g. a nurse who researches products before buying].

Solution and benefits (25-45 seconds): Walk through the ingredients and results in one run, one benefit per sentence: [INGREDIENT 1] helps with [BENEFIT 1]; [INGREDIENT 2] helps with [BENEFIT 2]; continue to about ten benefits, each matched to a real ingredient.

Social proof (45-50 seconds): Mention reviews or demand: [REVIEW COUNT] five-star reviews, and the product [SOLD OUT OR WAIT TIME, e.g. was out of stock for four weeks].

Call to action (50-60 seconds): Create real urgency tied to stock or availability, for example: [URGENCY LINE, e.g. stock looks low, so order now], then point to the link or product card: [CTA].

90-second talking-head script prompt

Write a 90-second talking-head script for [product] based on the angle [angle name]. Use about 215 words at a natural ad pace. Use simple everyday words and the exact buyer phrases from the survey: [paste 3 to 5 phrases]. Follow this order: hook (0:00), name the private wound the buyer feels (0:14), reframe why nothing worked and that it is not the buyer's fault (0:30), introduce [product] by name as something made for this gap (0:52), show the proof and the relief (1:08), and close with an invitation to take a look instead of a tap-now push (1:22). Do not promise anything the product does not deliver. Avoid jargon and hard-sell commands. End with the word count and the estimated runtime.
  • The angle is the main reason the buyer should care.
  • Use the buyer's own words from your survey. Paste 3 to 5 of them into the prompt.

90-second script order

  1. Hook (0:00): open with a private thought the buyer has in a hard moment.
  2. Agitate the wound (0:14): name the self-doubt and the failed attempts the buyer already made.
  3. Reframe and mechanism (0:30): explain that the failure came from a missing tool or method, not a character flaw. The mechanism is how the product works.
  4. Introduce the product (0:52): name the product. Describe it as built for that exact gap.
  5. Proof and relief (1:08): show what changes for the buyer. Show the words they use after it works.
  6. Call to action (1:22): invite the viewer to take a look. Do not push them to tap now.

Formula for the 90-second script

  • Formula: open with a private moment. Name the self-doubt. Reframe the failure as a missing tool, not a flaw. Introduce the product by name as the fix. Invite a look.
  • Example A (sleep product): Most people blame themselves when the nights stay bad. The reason nothing stuck is a missing routine, not a lack of discipline. [Product] gives you that routine in ten minutes a night, so come see how it works.
  • Example B (running insoles): You have tried every insole and your feet still hurt, so you assume running is not for you. Your feet were never the problem; the support had the wrong shape. [Product] is built for that shape, so take a look at how it fits.
  • The reframe must agree with the buyer's doubt that nothing worked. Then explain why it failed.
  • Arguing against that doubt makes the buyer resist the ad.
  • Do not hide the product name until late in the script.
  • Do not use advanced words for simple products, such as books.
  • Simple, warm language sells better than a hard direct-response tone to these buyers.

Voiceover and narration

1 min
  • Narration can repeat the written copy word for word. It can say the copy slightly differently. It can play as a conversation. Or it can be a blind voiceover (a voice with no visible speaker).
  • The choice changes how people see the ad.
  • Add voiceover or captions where they help the message land.

Emotional and identity-based scripts

1 min
  • Formula for emotional products: use a named, relatable persona. Give them a specific problem tied to the emotional goal of your niche (the small market you sell into).
  • The persona finds the product, uses it in a daily routine and sees a visible change in their life.
  • Viewers identify with the person and imagine the same result.
  • This works better than a list of features for products people buy for identity or feeling.
  • Example 1: Lena, a mom of three who never had time to unwind, added a calming lavender roll-on to her evening routine. She describes her nights as quieter.
  • Example 2: Marcus, a night-shift nurse with restless sleep, started a bedtime ritual with a weighted eye mask. He now falls asleep faster after work.

Dialogue and complaint formats

1 min
  • Fake-conversation pattern: stage a short exchange. One character makes a claim. A second character responds. Reveal the outcome at the end.
  • The conflict or outcome should keep viewers watching to the end.
  • Fake-conversation example A: a customer complains about late delivery. The brand replies with a surprising fix.
  • Fake-conversation example B: a friend disputes a product result. The reply reveals the truth.
  • Complaint format: a customer posts a complaint where his own mistake caused the problem. The brand responds.
  • The viewer watches to see who is right, so the customer's error works as the hook.
  • The brand's reply must be fast and specific.
  • Complaint example A: a buyer says the product broke. The brand replies with the correct steps for use.
  • Complaint example B: a buyer claims an item is missing. The brand answers by showing the unboxing footage.

Creator-made and UGC content

1 min
  • A person on camera is optional for a UGC-style ad. The founder can film the product, or a friend can.
  • A self-recorded testimonial with the seller's own face can work as social proof.
  • The condition: the footage must look like a real person using the product. It must not look like a polished brand ad.
  • Creator videos must look genuine and believable. Viewers can tell when a sponsored clip feels fake.
  • A believable review performs better than an over-produced one.
  • Discovery-style opening formula: open with a surprised first-person line that names the source. Then show the product in use.
  • It works because viewers trust a casual find more than a polished ad.
  • Example 1: "Look what I just found on TikTok, it actually works!"
  • Example 2: "Wait, I bought this from a random Instagram ad and it solved my problem."
  • Make ad videos specifically for paid placement (ads you pay for). Do not reuse organic content (free posts).
  • Ad videos need stronger calls to buy, clear reasons to purchase and a strong hook.
  • A video that went viral for free may fail as an ad. For example, it may have gone viral through a giveaway that drew people who only wanted a free item.

Testimonials and demonstrations

1 min
  • A before and after picture placed on top of the video works as visual proof. It works like a testimonial (a customer's endorsement). It shows the result the viewer wants before the speaker makes any claim.
  • Show the product used on more than one body area in a testimonial, for example face and lips, so viewers with a different problem see themselves in the demonstration. The visual widens the set of problems the ad appears to solve without adding a new claim. Only show areas the product is labelled for.

Shot planning and feature demonstrations

1 min

Feature and benefit on screen

1 min
  • Each product feature is a demonstration, and each benefit is the reason the viewer should care. A feature is what the product does. A benefit is what that means for the buyer. Show the feature on screen, such as a Bluetooth connection (a wireless link to other devices), light-up mode or floating, while the script states the benefit, such as connecting to any device or enjoying it in the bath or pool.
  • Feature-to-benefit shot pattern: pair each product feature with a short on-screen demonstration and a spoken benefit line, then show the product in a real use context.
  • Example A (kitchen knife): the feature "blade stays sharp for months" is shown by slicing tomatoes in a clean cut. The benefit "prep dinner in half the time" is spoken over the footage.
  • Example B (foldable yoga mat): the feature "folds into a bag" is shown by folding it in one motion. The benefit "take your practice anywhere" is spoken as the user walks out the door.

Golden rule and shot list

1 min
  • Film for the context. The footage must support what is being said. When the speaker talks about a feature, the scene should show that feature working, such as the brush picking up hair when the script says it does.
  • Use a shot list (a plan of the scenes to film) tied to the script. For every script line, write the scene that shows it before filming. This prep makes the edit faster and reduces wasted takes (recordings you cannot use).

Twelve-scene creator brief

1 min
  1. Create a brief for video creators requesting 12 five-second scenes instead of one 60-second video.
  2. Specify 5 hooks, 5 different use cases, and 3 different calls-to-action (the action you want viewers to take, such as Shop now).
  3. Send the product to the creator for free; they keep it after shooting.
  4. Receive the raw video clips (unedited footage).
  5. Send the clips to an internal video editor (your own person who cuts the video).
  6. The editor combines the clips into 5 to 10 different final videos with varied hook and scene orders.
  7. Test the videos on Facebook or TikTok.
  • This structure yields more content variations for testing than a single video.

Production sequence and reuse

1 min

Turning a winning ad into a script

1 min
  1. Pick the winning creative (the ad that sells best) with the highest website purchases in the Kalodata testing CBO. Kalodata is a tool for tracking product and ad performance. CBO (campaign budget optimization) is when the platform moves the budget to the best-performing ads.
  2. Paste it into Google AI Studio (Google's AI tool) and transcribe it (turn the spoken words into text).
  3. Use the transcript as the basis for a custom VSL (video sales letter, a longer video that sells) with the same winning messaging.

Reusing formats

1 min
  • Reuse the same intro across videos to make three uploads a day easier to produce. Repeated intros keep production fast without losing the format that works.

Calls to action and endings

1 min

Placement of the call to action

1 min
  • Add a call to action (CTA, the request to act) at the end to raise conversion (the share of viewers who buy). Expect it may lower views, because viewers who spot an ad often skip immediately. Test it per product rather than using it by default.
  • Include the call to action inside the video itself, not only in the ad text or buttons.
  • The last 3 to 5 seconds must contain a clear call to action that tells viewers to tap the Shop Now button (the button that opens the store). Include the store URL (web address) in the closing frame (the final image).
  • Conflict between sources: the standard video ad closes with a Shop Now tap in the last 3 to 5 seconds. The 90-second talking-head script closes with an invitation to take a look, not a tap-now push. Use the invitation for talking-head scripts that name a private wound (a personal pain the viewer keeps hidden). Use the Shop Now close for standard video ads, and test per product.

Retargeting reminder

1 min

Retargeting means showing ads to people who left without buying. Script for a collection ad reminder (an ad that shows a set of products), spoken by the store owner on camera.

Hey, thanks for checking out my store. I noticed you never finished checking out, so maybe you got busy. I just want you to know that your cart is still waiting for you. Point up to the link in the ad copy, or point down and say: Click below to go straight back to the product.
  • Keep it short, face the camera and speak as the owner, because a personal video stands out more than a generic image.

Business-owner introduction videos

1 min
  • In a video introduction ad, keep the video to a couple of minutes at most, or even shorter, because it plays directly in the news feed (the scrolling list where ads appear).
  • Order for a short introduction video from the business owner. It should stay within a couple of minutes:
    1. Greet the viewer briefly and say who you are.
    2. State that you are the owner of the business.
    3. Offer help choosing the right product for the viewer's situation.
    4. Say that your team is ready to help and invite the viewer to get in touch.
    5. Describe briefly what the business does and which problems the products solve.
    6. Mention key selling points such as free shipping or easy returns, if they apply.
    7. Repeat the call to action at the end.

Teaching-style episodes

1 min
  • Keep each teaching video at about five minutes, which was the chosen length for this format. A consistent length makes the format easier to repeat and to produce regularly.
  • Episode procedure:
    1. Open with a short greeting and a hook that names the topic.
    2. Read the source text aloud in its original language.
    3. Give the translation.
    4. Explain one clear takeaway for daily life.
    5. Close with a call to watch the next part.
    6. Keep each episode to one passage so the video stays within about five minutes.

More details

  • Before editing, write the ad script around a narrative such as problem, agitation and solution, so the message drives the edit. At testing stage the marketing message matters more than whether the ad uses images or video.

  • Creative testing requires written scripts before filming, because videos cannot be produced effectively just by sending products to creators or filming without a creative strategy.

  • Before cutting any footage, settle the target audience and the script, because an ad built without them becomes irrelevant. These decisions come before sourcing clips or editing.
