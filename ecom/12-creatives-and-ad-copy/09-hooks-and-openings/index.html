Hooks and openings

Section 12.9 · 9 of 26 in this chapter · 31 min read

In short

  • Your hook is the first 1 to 3 seconds of an ad. It must stop people from scrolling past and keep them watching.
  • Show the viewer's problem or the main benefit fast. The first 3 to 5 seconds decide whether people keep watching.
  • Show the product within the first 5 seconds, unless the ad is a strong unboxing (opening a package on camera).
  • Name your exact buyer. The right people stay, and everyone else leaves, which is good.
  • Pick one hook style, test it on its own, and keep what works.
More key points
  • Your first seconds decide if people keep watching. Put your strongest point there.
  • Show a problem or a surprise first. Show the product after that.
  • Test many openings. Change only the first 3 to 5 seconds each time.
  • Start written ads with "Even if..." to answer the main doubt readers have.
  • Show the benefit within 3 to 5 seconds. Waiting 10 seconds loses people.

What the opening has to do

2 min

  • A hook is the opening line of a video ad, in the first 1 to 3 seconds. The word means the line that grabs attention.

  • A hook stops the scroll (the thumb moving on to the next post) and starts a full watch.

  • A hook works by triggering one of seven psychological patterns (mental triggers that move people to act).

    • Direct problem relevance
    • Comparison against current solutions
    • Personal authority from experience
    • Curiosity about outcomes
    • Fear of missing out
    • Daily necessity
    • Removal of cost barriers

  • A hook decides whether the algorithm (the platform's system that chooses which posts to show) records a watch completion (the viewer watches to the end) or an exit (the viewer leaves).

  • The hook is arguably the most important ad element.

  • It must stop the scroll and keep the viewer watching.

  • It should highlight a fact, a problem, the key benefit or the unique selling point (USP, the one thing that sets the product apart).

  • The first 3 to 5 seconds decide whether a viewer keeps watching.

  • If the hook fails, nobody sees the rest of the ad, however good the offer.

  • The first seconds must stop the scroll and show what the product does right away.

  • Shocking, unexpected or user-generated content (UGC) (videos made by customers or ordinary people, not by the brand) can achieve this.

  • Attention is only the first step.

  • The opening must also win interest, or attention fades and is much harder to win back.

  • A strong opening drives the click. It does more than get views past the hook (people who keep watching after the first seconds).

  • A strong ad calls out a problem or a big benefit in the first seconds. This makes viewers already qualified buyers (people who are likely to buy).

  • Attention-grabbing content with no link to the product attracts viewers who will not buy.

  • A hook should relate to the product, the video and the viewer's own situation.

  • A hook should also appeal to emotion.

  • Statements viewers can instantly relate to hold attention longer.

  • Those statements also improve conversion (the share of viewers who buy).

  • Name the audience directly in hooks and copy (the written words of the ad), for example the profession or life situation.

  • People outside the group keep scrolling.

  • People inside the group qualify themselves.

  • Clicks are filtered toward likely buyers.

  • The first three seconds should target one specific audience.

  • Those three seconds should show either the pain point (the problem the viewer has) or the relief (how the problem goes away).

  • The right people stay, and others skip the ad.

  • Simple copy with a clear hook in the first seconds matters most.

  • The opening seconds decide whether viewers keep watching.

Problem first or product first

1 min

Advice differs on what the first frame (the first picture the viewer sees) should show.

  • Problem first: Show the problem the product solves, not its features or specifications (the technical details).
  • Do not lead with features such as "battery lasts 3 hours" or "rechargeable".
  • Show the dirty lawn first, then the clean lawn, so customers understand how the product helps.
  • Problem first, for problem-solving products: Bold claims (strong, direct statements) and pain points are the most useful for product-led ads (ads that lead with the product).
  • The first seconds should show the audience's problem, the face, or the problem before the product appears.
  • Result or demonstration first: Show the working product in the first three seconds (video ads).
  • Or show what the product does right away.
  • Product in first five seconds: Delayed products tend to underperform, unless the video is a strong unboxing.
  • Use the problem or the demonstration: Both are valid.
  • Apply the problem-first rule to products with many features, or to products built around a problem.
  • Apply the demonstration rule when the video is a product demo (a video that shows the product working).
  • No other conditions are given beyond the 10-second rule and the unboxing exception.

Problem and pain hooks

7 min

  • A pain point is the problem that hurts or annoys the viewer.

  • Problem identification and solution: State the specific pain point your audience experiences.

  • Then introduce the product as the fix.

  • Formula: "[If you / Stop scrolling if] you [specific pain]," then offer what fixes it.

  • It works because viewers who have that exact problem recognise themselves.

  • They stop to see the solution.

    • "Stop scrolling if you're tired of planners you never actually use."
    • "If you waste 30 minutes every morning choosing an outfit, this changes everything."

  • Problem-first hook formula: Name the viewer's exact problem in the first sentence.

  • Use the words the target customer uses.

  • Add "this is for you" or an equivalent, so the right person keeps watching.

  • The viewer identifies with the problem within one second and stops scrolling.

    • "If your kitchen floor is covered in crumbs every single morning, then this is for you."
    • "If your car smells like wet dog every time you drive, then this is for you."

  • Problem-first, product as direct fix: Name the customer's everyday problem in the first line.

  • Then present the product as the direct fix.

  • The viewer recognises their situation and understands the benefit at once.

    • "Tired of cold feet at your desk? Our heated foot warmer keeps them toasty all day."
    • "Sick of your car windows freezing at 7 am? This quick-defrost cover clears the glass in seconds."

  • Problem plus benefit with a visible symptom: Start with a question or statement. It names a specific problem and promises a benefit for a named body area or use case.

  • Then show the product doing that job immediately.

    • "Still waking up with puffy eyes? Watch this gel reduce swelling in 10 seconds."
    • "Worried about slipping on wet tile? See how these shower slippers grip the floor."

  • Symptom-first: Name the visible problem the buyer already notices. A symptom is a sign you can see.

  • State what it costs them in time, money or appearance.

  • Then close with a question that invites them to recognise it.

  • Buyers recognise symptoms faster than root causes (the deeper reasons behind them).

    • "Running shoes soaked before the first mile? Stop buying new pairs every month."
    • "Jackets stained after every commute? Your clothes deserve a fix that lasts."

  • Problem-solution opening: Lead with a direct question about the customer's pain.

    • Template: "Sick of the [specific problem]?"

  • Then position the brand as the answer.

  • Use an adjective that appeals to customer psychology, such as "wholistic", "calming" or "natural".

  • It works because it names the problem state, triggers recognition and offers an escape path at once.

    • "Tired of restless nights? This is your natural sleep solution."
    • "Frustrated with back tension? Meet your pain-free workday."

  • Contrast question: Open with a question or contrast that names the viewer's exact problem.

  • Then point to a different path than the one they are already on.

  • The hook is judged by how fast it makes the viewer feel seen.

    • "Are you still getting breakouts on your back after every workout? Here is the two-minute routine that finally stopped mine."
    • "If your running shoes still hurt after 5 km, don't buy another pair yet. Check this first."

  • Frustration and "that is me" statement: Name a frustration the target viewer already has.

  • Connect it to the product as the fix.

  • Phrase it as a short statement that makes the viewer think "that is me".

    • "Still scrubbing grease off the stovetop every night? Watch what happens in two seconds."
    • "Tired of your car smelling like wet gym bags after every rainy trip?"

  • Specific everyday problem, number and action: Open with one specific everyday problem the buyer already notices.

  • State a fix with a number.

  • Then close with one action.

  • Naming the exact situation makes the product feel relevant instead of generic.

    • "Chargers dying by week three? Our braided cable is rated for 10,000 bends, so order a pack of two today."
    • "Boots soaking through on every wet walk? Our membrane liner keeps feet dry for 8 hours, so get the pair before the weekend."

  • Relatable-problem hook: Open with a question or statement about a problem the viewer experiences.

  • Within seconds, show the product solving that problem on screen.

  • Viewers who recognise themselves stay to watch.

    • "Tired of wiping muddy paw prints off the floor after every walk?"
    • "Hate scrubbing burnt cheese off your pan every night?"

  • Problem-specific creative in the customer's words: "Creative" means the ad content itself.

  • Name the exact problem the audience has, in their own words, as found in reviews.

  • Then offer the product as the fix.

    • "Dog hair on your bed every morning? This brush lifts it out of wiry fur in one pass."
    • "Still stuck with cat hair on the sofa? This brush pulls it out before it spreads."

  • Owner-group formula: Name a specific owner group.

  • Describe the everyday annoyance they recognise.

  • Point to the visible symptom.

  • Add a direct call to act.

  • The viewer sees their own situation in the first line.

    • "Cat owners with a sofa full of fur, this roller lifts it off in one pass."
    • "Border Collie owners whose car seats are covered in hair, this attachment cleans them in minutes."

  • Pain-point hook: Open with a relatable daily struggle.

  • Name the feeling it causes.

  • Then reveal the product as the fix in one sentence.

    • "Dreading the wait for your kettle while you still need a second cup? Here is a quick fix that works in any mug."
    • "Tired of brewing bitter coffee that tastes flat every morning? This ready-to-use coffee gives you a smooth cup in seconds."

  • Reassure, explain, act: Open with a relatable pain point framed as a question.

  • Reassure the viewer that the problem is not their fault.

  • Name one mechanism of the product (how it works) in plain words.

  • End with a simple call to action (CTA, the line that tells the viewer what to do next).

  • The viewer recognises the problem first, so the product reads as a solution, not a pitch.

    • "Stuck in an afternoon slump again? You're not lazy, your energy may just need support. One daily capsule helps keep focus steady."
    • "Dog still pulling on every walk? It's not you. This harness shifts pulling tension to the chest so walks feel calm."

  • Precise customer words: Open the hook with the exact words your target customers use to describe the problem.

  • Use the words they use in forums, reviews or comments.

  • Only someone with that problem feels addressed.

  • The reader sees their own inner voice in the first line.

    • "My heel cracks every winter even after three creams, and I am sick of it."
    • "Every shirt is soaked by lunch, and nothing I have bought has fixed it."

  • Pain-point hook over free stock footage: Find a free stock clip (ready-made video from a free library) that shows the frustration.

  • Convert it to grayscale (black and white).

  • Overlay a caption (text on top of the picture) that names the pain point.

  • Add a cross mark over the clip.

  • Viewers recognise their own problem within seconds.

    • Grayscale clip of a dripping tap, caption: "Still mopping up the same puddle every morning?"
    • Grayscale clip of a cluttered desk, caption: "Losing 20 minutes a day hunting for chargers?"

  • Warning hook: Warn the viewer off a product, or ask them to check one detail before they act.

  • It makes the viewer feel protected from a mistake.

  • That holds attention longer than a plain product claim.

    • "Hold off on buying [product] until you check the ingredient list on the back."
    • "Stop reordering [product] before you read the renewal terms in the checkout."

  • Desire-result hook: Name a result the buyer wants.

  • Then name the product or ingredient (an active part of the product) as the way to get it.

  • Use the form "if you want [result] you need [product]".

  • The viewer sees the benefit in the first seconds.

    • "If you want deeper sleep without feeling groggy, you need magnesium glycinate at night."
    • "If your knees feel stiff every morning, you need this collagen drink."

  • Problem and relief in three seconds: Start with the viewer's specific problem.

  • Show the relief within the first three seconds.

  • Viewers with the same problem recognise it and keep watching.

  • Others leave, so the ad reaches a qualified audience.

    • "Tired of waking up with a stiff neck? Watch what happens after 60 seconds on this."
    • "Working from a laptop all day and feeling that back ache again? Here is the stretch that gives relief."

  • Relief sound or reaction: A relief sound or reaction, shown while using the product, works as a recurring hook pattern (a hook you can repeat).

  • Keep the same voice and actions across ads.

  • Change only the camera angle.

  • Testimonials in the problem hook (Pain/Agitate/Solve): Pain/Agitate/Solve is a three-step plan: name the pain, make it feel stronger, then offer the solution.

  • In the problem description hook, use customer testimonies (praise from real buyers) or direct quotes.

  • This gives social proof (other people's trust and praise) at the opening.

  • It comes before the product enters as the solution.

Claim, benefit and transformation hooks

4 min

  • Challenge the standard, offer a better alternative: State what people currently believe or do.

  • Then immediately introduce a different or better way.

  • It creates doubt about the current approach.

  • It positions the product as the smarter choice.

  • Formula: "Everyone says [common belief], but [your approach is better]" or "Don't [current solution], [do this instead]."

    • "Everyone says drink more water for energy, but here's what actually works."
    • "Don't buy those expensive noise-canceling headphones-try this instead for a quarter of the price."

  • Outcome or hidden insight: Promise a specific result or reveal a non-obvious reason.

  • Do not explain it in the hook.

  • Formula: "Here's [the secret / the real reason / what I discovered] about [topic]" or "How I [achieve specific result]," then pause.

  • Curiosity and the wish to know HOW keep the viewer watching.

    • "Here's the real reason your productivity hack isn't working."
    • "How I sleep 2 hours less and wake up more rested (and it's not what you think)."

  • Transformation hook: State that the product changed the speaker's space or health.

  • Then name the result.

  • The viewer sees a before and after before the product is explained.

  • A visible result creates curiosity and gives proof before any claim is made.

    • "This is how my car went from covered in dust to spotless in one week."
    • "This is how a single bottle changed my morning routine and gave me back my energy."

  • Bold claim: Open with one specific, strong statement about the product result.

  • The right viewer relates at once.

  • A direct claim filters the audience in the first second.

    • "This pan fries eggs without a drop of oil."
    • "This charger refills a phone in the time it takes to make coffee."

  • Bold claim with a payoff later: Open with a bold claim or surprising statement.

  • It makes viewers expect a payoff (the reward the opening promised).

  • The payoff can be a shocking reveal or a question they need answered.

  • The payoff must come later in the video.

  • That keeps viewers to the end.

    • Example A: a tiny change in the kitchen that saved a family a surprising amount each month.
    • Example B: nobody expected this result until the final second of the test.

  • Surprising cost or loss: Name a surprising cost or loss tied to an everyday situation.

  • Then present the product as the fix.

  • It grabs attention with a concrete problem the viewer recognises.

    • "Commuters lose hours each month to tangled cables. This organiser keeps every charger in one place."
    • "Gym goers waste money on bottles that leak. This sealed bottle stays dry in any bag."

  • Benefit-led hook: The hook states the target audience and problem. Then it creates curiosity. The product benefit follows.

    • Ergonomic mouse: "Office workers, never ignore these three signs of wrist strain. Wrist pain often starts small, and most people only notice it when it's too late. Our curved mouse keeps your hand relaxed through an entire workday."
    • Sleep mask: "Light sleepers, never ignore these three signs of a bad night's rest. Most sleep problems start with a stray light you barely notice. Our blackout mask blocks 100% of light so you wake up rested."

  • Budget-conscious solution: Acknowledge the cost barrier (the price worry) directly.

  • Then position the product as solving both the need and the price concern.

    • "Want luxury skincare without the luxury price tag? Here's how."
    • "Designer jeans energy on a thrift store budget."

  • Daily use or essential status: Highlight how often people use the product, or its category status.

  • This shows the product is proven and essential, not a trend.

  • Daily use suggests reliability.

  • It builds confidence in real value.

  • Formula: "[Number] products I [use daily / cannot live without / use every morning]" or "My must-have [product category] I use without fail."

    • "Three tools I use before 9 AM that actually change my day."
    • "The one item in my skincare routine I've used for five years straight."

  • Authority through extensive testing: Share your personal journey or quantify the scope of your testing.

  • Use words such as every, all, tested X number, daily or without fail.

  • Testing builds trust.

  • Viewers believe you have done due diligence (careful checking before you recommend something).

  • Formula: "I tried [number or scope], and here's what I kept" or "One [product] I use [frequency] is."

    • "I tested 23 coffee makers this year and kept only one."
    • "The one desk lamp I use every single workday and will never replace."

  • Trusted source and numbered list: A hook that credits a trusted source gets social proof in the first seconds.

  • A trusted source can be a viral trend (something many people are sharing) or a group of people.

  • That source is linked to a purchase decision.

  • A hook that promises a numbered list of reasons sets a clear, quick-to-scan promise.

    • "Busy parents keep switching to this, here is why."
    • "Five reasons your kitchen needs this before the weekend."

Curiosity, suspense and pattern breaks

5 min

  • Suspense (holding back information so people keep watching): Start with a curiosity gap, which is a missing piece of information that makes people want the rest. Delay the product reveal until after the first clip (the first short piece of video). Curiosity keeps people watching long enough to reach the product introduction.

    • "I almost threw this out before I tried it on my kitchen counter."
    • "The one thing my gym instructor never told me until I bought this."

  • Pattern break (something that breaks what people expect to see): Ads must interrupt what people are doing, not blend in. Use headlines of two to three words, the big top line, paired with an unusual image. This stops a reader mid-scroll (while swiping through a feed) long enough to click. The goal is a pattern break that moves attention to the offer.

  • Familiar word, unexpected format: Speak directly to the target buyer, the person most likely to buy. Pair a familiar, high-intent product word (a word that shows the buyer is ready to buy) with a format the buyer does not expect. The curiosity from the mismatch stops the scroll. The buyer identifies with the speaker at once.

    • "Sunscreen in a stick? Here is why it leaves no white cast and why my daily routine changed."
    • "Espresso-style foam in a 30-second jar? I cannot believe this replaced my morning cafe run."

  • Unusual question or event: Open with a specific, unusual question or event the reader cares about. Then bridge to the offer as the way to understand or solve it. Curiosity holds attention long enough for the reader to reach the offer.

    • "Why do some bakeries sell out by nine in the morning while the one across the street throws away its bread every night?"
    • "What is the one check that tells a warehouse manager a shipment will be late before the truck leaves the dock?"

  • Offbeat news hook: Find an unusual article or statistic (a number from research). Use it to open the ad. Then link it to the product through one clear step. Keep a file of such clippings so they are ready when a product needs a surprising opening.

    • "A survey shows that many people reuse their water bottles for days without washing them, so here is a bottle that cleans itself."
    • "A new report finds that most office chairs are sat on for more than eight hours, which is why this chair was designed around the spine."
    • "A survey finds many people reuse water bottles for days without washing them, so this bottle cleans itself."
    • "Most office chairs are sat on for over eight hours a day, which is why this one is built around the spine."

  • Educational hook: Open with a useful fact or warning that names a problem the audience cares about. Then reveal the product as the solution. Viewers accept the lesson first and the sale second.

    • "Three everyday habits that quietly dry out your skin and what to use instead."
    • "Two cleaning products that leave a film on your floors, and the simple swap that fixes it."

  • Educational reveal: Ads that reveal a problem the viewer did not know about tend to perform well. Example: "did you know your dog could suffer from...". They pull the viewer into the problem.

  • Question about owning the product: Open with a question that asks the viewer to picture owning or using the product. Then cut to text-to-speech (a computer voice that reads words aloud). It lists the reasons to buy in a few sentences. End with a short closing line.

    • "Do you see yourself keeping this on your desk every morning?" followed by benefits and a closing line about the daily routine.
    • "Do you see yourself giving this as a gift this winter?" followed by reasons it suits gifting and a closing line urging a purchase.

  • Case: "Do you see yourself needing this?": A jewelry store used the hook (the opening line that grabs attention) "Do you see yourself needing this?" It reportedly reached about 2.5 million views. The speaker cited this as evidence that the hook can perform well across different products.

  • Dialogue hook: Start with an emoji of a person and a text-to-speech question, such as "Why did you give me this free product?" Reply in character that the product is a gift. Change the wording slightly each time. Have the other person say they would like to see more videos, and ask whether they won. Finish by confirming they won.

    • "Why did you give me this free bike?" answered by a gift reply and a follow-up asking whether they won.
    • "Why did you send me this free candle set?" answered by a thank-you and a question asking whether they can join the giveaway.

  • Case: dialogue hook performance: The dialogue hook "Why did you give me this free product?" was uploaded about three to six times before one version got traction. That version reached about 370,000 views and brought about 5,000 followers. Earlier uploads of the same format got barely any views.

  • Text-story hook: Use on-screen text that takes longer to read than the video lasts. Example: 15 seconds of reading in a 7-second video. This forces viewers to replay, which raises average watch time (how long people watch on average). The text must be engaging enough to keep them reading.

    • Example A: a short story where the outcome is revealed only in the final frame.
    • Example B: a running dialogue where each line shows a small surprise.

  • Story opening: Begin with a short exchange in which someone with a real problem asks a respected person for advice. Let the answer introduce a principle that leads to the product. A concrete person and a question make the reader listen. The answer frames the product as the practical result of wise advice.

    • "A plumber with a full schedule asked his most experienced mentor how to stop losing jobs to cheaper rivals, and the answer was to sell the result the customer will see first."
    • "A new mother asked her pediatrician what matters most in a stroller, and the reply began with the one feature that parents regret not checking."

Audience, POV (first-person view) and social-proof (other people's approval as proof) hooks

2 min

  • Niche-targeting hook: Address only a specific group in the first line. Non-members scroll past. Members engage (like, comment, or watch). Engagement from that group signals TikTok to show the video to similar users. This can give better-converting reach (more people who buy) than a large but broad view count.

    • "Every runner needs this free route tracker."
    • "Calling all home bakers: this tool fixes sticky dough in seconds."

  • Niche hook with a cold or problem: Name a specific audience. Name the cold or problem they face in their activity. Then give the product as the direct fix. The hook works because it addresses one identifiable person at the moment of the problem.

    • "For every football fan at winter games: cold feet ruin the match, so wear these insulated socks all game long."
    • "For the dog walkers stuck in the rain: the waterproof jacket that keeps you dry on every walk."

  • Hobby hook, boredom and free time: Name the boredom at home. Then show how the product changed the way the viewer spends free time.

    • "Tired of scrolling every evening? Here is the one thing that filled my weekends."
    • "Your free hours deserve more than the couch. Try this build."

  • POV hook: Place the viewer in a first-person situation in the first second. This makes the scene feel personal and encourages them to keep watching. POV works well when the scene leads straight into a comparison or product demonstration.

    • "POV: you finally find a charger that survives a full week of travel."
    • "POV: your kitchen floor stays clean after the first week with this mop."

  • Shopping-finds hook: Frame the product as something people discover on TikTok, Amazon or shopping feeds. This uses curiosity about what others buy. Viewers trust products that appear socially trending.

    • "The kitchen tool everyone on my feed keeps buying."
    • "Three reasons I stopped paying for the expensive version."

  • Fear of missing out (FOMO) and insider knowledge: Suggest the product is unknown or underused. Position yourself as the one sharing the secret. FOMO triggers curiosity and urgency. Viewers do not want to be left behind.

    • Formula: "Why is nobody talking about [product]," "Calling all [audience] tired of [problem]," or "If you're not [using X / doing X], you're missing out."
    • "Calling all runners tired of blisters-why isn't anyone using this?"
    • "If you're not using this app for your business, you're doing it the hard way."

  • Follow-for-giveaway hook (a free prize drawn from followers): It grows followers quickly. Example: "follow my account and I'll give you a giveaway". It has become less effective for sales because many sellers use the same style. Use it sparingly. Do not rely on it as the main sales driver.

Copy openings (the written words of an ad, and of the landing page, the page a buyer reaches after clicking)

3 min

  • Start copy with "Even if..." to address the main objection (the doubt a reader might have). This jugular objection, the single most common one, must be answered first, or you will lose readers. Illusory superiority (the belief that you are better than average) means most people think, "it might work for them but not for me."

  • Mistake: skipping the jugular objection: Not addressing the common main doubt at the start of copy is a mistake. Start with "Even if..." to answer what readers are thinking. Illusory superiority makes them think, "it might work for them, but not for me."

  • Open with customer language (the words customers actually use): Open with their words. State their pain point directly. Reference their insecurity or deepest desire. Or make a bold benefit claim that resonates immediately.

  • Short first sentence of body copy: Keep the first sentence very short. It should be nearly incomplete and easy to read. Avoid long words and words with many syllables (beats in a word). Its only job is to make the reader read the second sentence.

  • Headline-to-first-sentence pull (print): A print ad is an ad in a magazine or newspaper. Every element (headline, subheadline (the smaller line under the headline), photo, caption (text under the picture), logo, layout) is judged by one question. Does it get the reader to read the first sentence? The first body sentence should be very short, almost incomplete, with no long words.

    • "Your inbox is a mess. Here is how to clear it in ten minutes."
    • "Your back hurts by noon. Four changes can stop that before lunch."

  • Real-life scenario: Open with the buyer inside a stressful everyday moment. Describe the feelings and pressure. Only then reveal how the product resolves it. Keep technical specs (exact details like numbers and features) for later, so the scene does the emotional work first.

    • "You are stuck in a hotel lobby, the bill is waiting, and the card reader fails; this portable terminal fixes the moment."
    • "You are on a crowded train with a phone that is about to die; this solar pack keeps you connected until you get home."
    • "The card reader fails and the line is growing behind you. This portable terminal fixes that moment."
    • "The phone is at 3 percent on a crowded train. This solar pack keeps you connected until you get home."

  • Familiar scene from daily life: Open with a scene the reader recognises. Then move to the problem the product solves, within the same scene. A familiar setting makes the reader picture himself, so the offer feels relevant.

    • "After you finish the morning school run and drop the car keys on the counter, you still have three invoices waiting to be paid."
    • "On the first cold morning of the year, the water in the shower takes nine minutes to warm, and your kids are already late for the bus."

  • News angle tied to the reader: A news angle, or an unusual and specific detail, draws attention. It can be bridged to the offer. Tie the attention to the reader's own interest in their business or life. It must not stand alone as a shock.

  • Newsworthy first, then their affairs: Businesspeople face many distractions. Give the reader something newsworthy first. Then connect it to their own affairs. Once attention is diverted, bring it back by showing how the offer serves those affairs.

  • Simple first line that is relevant: The first sentence or headline must instantly say something important, valuable and useful to the reader. An unwanted letter is treated as a pest until it proves otherwise. Gimmicks (tricks to get attention), such as fake hazard warnings, tend to fail. A genuinely relevant headline usually succeeds.

  • Sensational shout (mistake): A sensational shout (a loud, alarming word or phrase) with no link to the offer draws the wrong response. Tie every attention-grabbing element to a reason the reader cares about the offer.

  • Find the point of contact (the one thought that links the reader to what you sell): Follow these three steps to build an opening.

    1. List the reader's dominant thoughts, such as income, family, comfort, status, promotion, business survival or a current worry.
    2. Pick the thought most closely tied to what you sell.
    3. Open with a sentence about that thought and bring in the offer as its answer.

On-screen text, sound and visual hooks

2 min
  • On-screen text carries much of the effect: The on-screen text (words shown in the video) and the hook share a large part of a video's effect. One estimate puts the text alone at about 30% of the video. A weak opening can turn a good demonstration into a low-converting video (one that rarely leads to sales).
  • Text plus unexpected background: Combine an on-screen text hook with something unexpected in the background. The background element should be slightly odd. It catches viewers off guard and makes them curious.
  • Text-to-speech or short text: Text-to-speech in the opening can keep viewers engaged. Alternatively, show a few sentences of on-screen text with a quick transition. Viewers must re-watch the video to finish reading.
  • Study viral on-screen text: Viral (widely shared) product videos often succeed because of their on-screen text. Study the text and structure of viral product videos. Reuse the proven text pattern while filming your own product.
  • On-screen hook example: On a short product clip, the first second shows the hook text "Do you see yourself drinking this?" on screen. Then comes a recreated pouring sound and a text line describing the product's benefit.
  • Short, fast hooks and music: A short, fast hook is key to TikTok creatives (the ad videos). Spend effort on the first seconds, the music and the hook variations to find winning versions.
  • Thumbnails matter less for video ads: A thumbnail is the small preview picture. Feed placements (where the ad appears in a feed) often skip past thumbnails. Test the first seconds of the video (the hook) more than the thumbnail.
  • Hook formats for variations: Try a clip change or a music change. Other hook formats include a "three reasons why" opener, a "wish I knew earlier" opener, or a before-and-after opener.
  • Hook scene template: Use this template as written for the first seconds.
Hook, first seconds. Option one, spoken: tell viewers this is for them if [PET] hair has taken over their home. Shot: a slow pan across a living room buried in [PET] hair. Option two, spoken: say that [PRODUCT] changed their [SPACE] and gave them back their health. Shot: show the product, cut to a before view of a grimy, hair-covered [SPACE], then cut to the same [SPACE] spotless afterward.
  • Hook as a pattern break in the visual: Ads must interrupt, not blend in. Use an unusual image. The image is part of the pattern break that stops the scroll.

Building hook variations

1 min
  • Variations per concept: Write one script for the concept (the core idea). Then make three versions. Only the first three to five seconds change. The rest of the video stays the same.
  • Keep the hook separate for testing: The hook, meaning the first three to five seconds, decides whether viewers stay. Test it separately from the rest of the video.
  • One video, three tests: Keep the video and change only the opening hook, one of the most important parts for grabbing attention. One video can become three test videos this way.
  • Start with about six hooks: Build a list of about six hooks and make several videos for each one. Once a hook starts performing, duplicate (copy) that video and post more variations of it.
  • Hook library (a saved collection): A library of about 50 hook ideas can produce around 50 variations of the same product video. It can supply roughly three to six months of creative. Keep the library current, because hook trends change constantly.
  • Test list of 50 hooks: A video hook test list of 50 hook ideas is recommended for TikTok-style first seconds. Use it to pick which hook goes in the first position.
  • Keep notes on hooks that work: Hooks can start with a question about the audience's issue, or with a direct solution statement. They should be tested. Keep notes on hooks that work so they can be reused later. This includes TikTok ads, once organic results (free, unpaid results) fund them.

Testing hooks

1 min
  • Test systematically: Take a winning creative (an ad that already performs well). Test many hook variations until one clearly outperforms. Hook ideas should come from audience research on language, problems and context.
  • Three variations of one video: Create three variations of one video. Replace only the first 3 to 5 seconds of the clip with different openings. Run all variations inside the winning audiences (the groups that respond best).
  • Lift a weak attention rate (how many viewers keep watching): To raise a weak attention rate, take the video that works. Place several new hooks in front of it. Make sure every hook fits the marketing angle (the main selling idea) and matches the landing page copy. Keep testing new hooks continually, even when attention is already good.
  • Hook testing with the 13-pattern classification: Use the 13-pattern set from the classification procedure (a sorting system with 13 types). Label each tested hook with it, so results show which pattern type performs.
  • Hooks decide the click too: A strong opening drives views beyond the hook, into the click. Track clicks as well as watch time in the hook test.

Mistakes to avoid

1 min
  • Spending too long before the main benefit. If the ad takes 10 seconds or more to introduce the product, demonstrate the benefit within 3 to 5 seconds, then introduce the product.
  • Leading with product features in the opening. Show the problem first, then the solution.
  • Not addressing the jugular objection (the main doubt) at the start of copy.
  • Attention-grabbing content with no relation to the product, which attracts viewers who will not buy.
  • Delaying the product in a non-unboxing video (opening a product on camera). Unless it is a strong unboxing, show the product within the first five seconds.
  • Sensational shouts with no link to the offer.
  • Gimmicks such as fake hazard warnings, which tend to fail.
  • Relying on the follow-for-giveaway hook as the main sales driver.
  • A product introduction that takes around 10 seconds, which loses viewers before the benefit appears.
