Playbook › 12. Creatives and ad copy

Reading results, iterating and scaling creatives

Section 12.23 · 23 of 26 in this chapter · 24 min read

In short

  • Find the weakest step first: attention, interest, desire or action. Look at the numbers, then at the ad itself.
  • Fix one weak step at a time. Changing everything at once hides what worked.
  • Check the checkout before blaming the ad. Broken buying steps lose sales that look like an ad problem.
  • Judge an ad over several days. One bad day is not enough reason to stop it.
  • Stop an ad only after it has spent enough to break even (no money lost) and still shows no promise. Otherwise, test new versions.
More key points
  • Find the ads that sell best through separate tests, then combine their best parts.
  • Judge ads by ROAS (money earned back for each unit spent on ads) first, not by how many people saw them.
  • Reuse what already works: new openings on old videos, new versions, and offer banners.
  • During Black Friday and Cyber Monday (BFCM), make fewer new ads and add offer banners to the winners.
  • Stop Christmas ads in mid-December, because late orders will not arrive in time.

Read the data and the creative together

1 min
  • A creative is the ad itself: its video, pictures and text.
  1. Review the ad performance data, meaning the numbers that show how the ad is doing.
  2. Examine the creative: the visuals (pictures and video), the headline (the main title line), the body copy (the main text) and the hooks (the first seconds or words that grab attention).
  3. List hypotheses (possible reasons, guesses you can test) about why performance is what it is, based on both the data and the creatives.

Full troubleshooting order when an ad underperforms

1 min

The funnel is the path a buyer takes: attention, interest, desire, then action (buying).

  1. Review the ad performance metrics (the numbers) to find the weak point in the funnel: attention, interest, desire or action.
  2. Open the ads in creative view, the screen that shows each ad's visuals and text.
  3. Watch the ad journey (the path a viewer takes, from the first second to the end) and identify where it could improve.
  4. Go through the creative review checklist below.
  5. List all hypotheses about why the ad is underperforming.
  6. Rank the hypotheses and pick the most important one.
  7. Create variations: some as iterations (small changes to the working ad), some as brand-new concepts.
  8. Run the new ads and test them.

Creative review procedure

1 min
  1. Look at the ad data to find where the funnel falls.
  2. Open the creative and identify which ads worked and which did not.
  3. Check the middle section of the ad for where it loses viewers.
  4. List all hypotheses for why it falls down.
  5. Pick the most important hypothesis and test it.

Creative review after every ad set test

1 min

An ad set is a group of ads that share one audience and one budget.

  1. Use the data and visual analysis to work out why each ad performed as it did.
  2. Write down why the winning ads worked and why the losing ads did not.
  3. Create three new ads that build on the analysis, iterating on the tested ads with new ideas expected to improve them.
  4. Test a whole new creative concept alongside the iterations.

After scoring the ads, list which hook styles, message angles, formats and talent types (the people shown in the ad) appear among the winners and which among the losers. Turn these patterns into the direction for the next creative round.

Soft metrics point to the stage

2 min

Soft metrics are early signs, such as attention and clicks, that come before sales.

  • Use attention rate (share of viewers who stay past the first seconds) and CTR (click-through rate: share of people who click) to diagnose a creative. Low attention points to the hook. Low CTR points to the middle content. No sales point to the offer. Fix the weakest stage rather than changing everything at once.
  • Poor hook rate (share of viewers who stay past the hook) with good interest and desire means the opening needs better hooks. Good hook rate with poor CTR means the middle messaging is not generating interest, and the hook may be attracting the wrong audience. Strong hook and CTR with weak sales point to the offer or the landing page (the page a click goes to), not the creative.
  • Read hook rate and hold rate (share of viewers who keep watching) together. A strong hook with a weak hold means the opening works but the rest of the video loses viewers. A strong hold with a weak hook means the rest of the video works and only the opening needs a rewrite. Reuse the strong part in new creatives.
  • Judge creatives by consistency across several days, not one sale. A low hook rate or low CTR points to the creative's opening. A low conversion rate (share of visitors who buy) points to the page.
  • Judge a product's soft metrics on the same budget over several days, not one day. A single day of poor results is not a reason to cut a product that is still in its learning phase (the first days when the ad platform is still finding the right buyers).
  • Engagement rate ranking, quality ranking and conversion rate ranking rate an ad against other advertisers aiming at the same audience. Use them as optional checks for engagement, content quality and conversion quality.
  • Ad ranking order: ROAS (return on ad spend: money earned back for each unit spent) first, then CTR, then average play time (how long people watch on average). Some ads have lower CTR but strong conversion, so ROAS takes priority when it is clearly positive.
  • If two or more creatives have similar ROAS, use CTR to choose between them. If CTR is also similar, use average play time. Give a creative with a large CTR gap more time to collect data before deciding.
  • Judge a creative on hook rate, CTR and conversion rate after launch.

Video retention, in detail

1 min

Video retention means how long viewers keep watching. Impressions are the number of times an ad was shown.

  • Build extra custom metrics (your own tracked numbers) for 25%, 50% and 75% video views, each divided by impressions. With high traffic these show exactly where viewers drop off, so you know which part to fix.
  • Video length matters for hook analysis. A 15-second video that averages 8 seconds of watch time means many viewers drop out in the middle. Check this alongside the hook rate.
  • Hook-first diagnostic:
    1. Test many hooks for the first seconds of the video.
    2. Check the video attention rate. If it is low, keep testing hooks.
    3. Check the CTR. If it is low, improve the middle of the ad: story, offer timing, engagement (likes, comments, shares).
    4. Check add-to-carts (people adding the product to their cart) and purchases to confirm the funnel works.
    5. Keep the hook messaging consistent with the landing page copy (the text on the page the click goes to).
  • Test better 3-second hooks to raise CTR and lower CPC (cost per click). Weak CPC, CPM (cost per 1,000 impressions) and CTR usually point to the creative, so the first seconds must capture attention and the ad must look high quality.

The four numbers, read together

1 min
  1. Check the video attention rate to judge how well the opening hook holds viewers.
  2. Check the CTR to judge whether the middle and offer make people click.
  3. Check content views and add-to-carts as extra signals, since Facebook may not record every event.
  4. Check purchases as the final result. Read the four numbers together, not one alone.
Symptom Likely cause Action Source
Low video attention Weak opening seconds Test several new hooks in front of the same video body (the rest of the ad)
Good attention, low CTR Weak middle, offer shown too late, or irrelevant viewers Strengthen the middle, show the offer sooner, make the message more specific
Content views but few add-to-carts Landing page does not match the ad Fix relevance and copy on the landing page
Add-to-carts but no purchases Checkout problem Test the checkout, then rerun the original ad to compare

Functional problems come before creative

3 min

Functional problems are technical faults, such as a broken button, not weak ad messages.

  • Troubleshooting order by funnel stage:
    1. Check the funnel stage with the biggest drop: attention, interest, desire or action.
    2. For weak desire or action with good attention and interest, check functional problems first: broken checkout, wrong currencies, shipping charged by mistake, missing add-to-cart button, wrong pricing display.
    3. Then check price shock (buyers are surprised by the price), poor product page, lack of trust, an unclear ad that misleads, and whether the offer is good enough.
    4. For poor attention with good interest and desire, test better hooks aimed at the right audience.
    5. For good attention with poor interest, test better messaging and the middle section of the ad.
  • Clicks and add-to-carts but no sales: check the checkout first. Then rerun the original ad to find out whether the ad or the checkout caused the problem. Either sales return because the checkout is fixed, or the test shows the ad was less targeted than the current one.
  • Zero add-to-carts after a large spend may mean customers do not care about the product, which is the real reason. Creative changes alone rarely fix a product that generates no add-to-carts.
  • Make sure the creative, meaning the video and product pictures, is strong before adjusting the offer. While CTR is good, focus on making the offer convert (turn visitors into buyers).
  • A strong creative and a strong offer allow more spend per customer acquisition (the cost to win one buyer), because more qualified people enter the funnel and average order value (AOV: the average amount per order) rises through upsells (a bigger or better version offered) and cross-sells (a related extra product offered). Scaling then depends on the quality of the funnel rather than on tricks.
  • A high view count from an organic video (free, not paid) does not guarantee sales. A viral toy-type product with 1.8 million views produced only 2 orders in the observed period, so check organic reach against real order numbers.
  • Comments such as "I'll buy because you're honest" are not reliable purchase signals. Place a real test order and check the confirmation or order number to verify whether the audience converts.
  • Use CTR as a sign of interest in the product. If no one clicks, the product is not appealing enough. Keep the ad running only if CTR holds up over the test budget (the money set aside for testing).
  • Judge advertising only by whether it makes people act: ordering, requesting information or paying. An ad that people merely admire and forget has failed its purpose.
  • The purpose of a Facebook ad is to drive traffic to the store. The store must then convert visitors into customers, so the website offer deserves improvement if ads are performing.
  • Measure readership by how many people actually travel through the full copy, not by circulation (number of copies printed) or impressions. Only readers who go through the copy can buy, so an ad with a large audience that reads little is weak.
  • Plan for delayed responses. Some readers clip an ad and act months later when the need appears, so a good ad can produce sales long after it stops running.
  • Formula: readers × response rate = responses. Example: if 50 percent of 100 tear-sheet recipients (copies of a printed page sent out) read it and 10 percent of readers respond, you get 5 responses per 100 pieces. A standard letter that starts at 10 percent reading would need 50 percent of its readers to respond to match that.

Benchmarks and thresholds

3 min

Different guides give different values for the same metric. Each value is listed with the condition it applies to.

Metric Value When it applies Source
CTR Below 0.7% Replace the creative, because the video is not stopping viewers
CTR Below 1% Creative is failing; fix the hook or creative and iterate once
CTR About 1% or higher Creative works and the funnel can keep running
CTR 1 to 1.5% Workable only with a cheap CPM and CPC; otherwise it means paying more per visitor and fewer conversions. Below this range the creative has clear room for improvement
CTR 1.5% (minimum acceptable, Facebook) General floor
CTR 2% or more Excellent
CTR Above 2% Keep iterating if the landing page can be improved
CTR About 3% Judged strong on a winning creative
CPC Below $1.5 Good
CPC Below $1.4 to $1.5 (US) Acceptable. Costs are rising, so $1.5 is the current industry benchmark (standard reference point)
CPC Above $1, combined with CPM above $10 and CTR below 1.5% Ads are not attracting interest; test new, more engaging creatives rather than touching the store
CPM Above about $30 (sometimes about $35) Suggests a competitive audience or weak creative that forces people to see the ad
CPM Above $10, combined with CPC above $1 and CTR below 1.5% Creative problem, see above
  • Rough diagnostic: a creative with a CTR of 1 percent and a cost per link click of $1.52 was judged weak. A CTR of about 3 percent on a winning creative was judged strong.
  • Low CTR with cheap CPM and CPC can stay profitable. A CTR of 1 to 1.5 percent must be paired with cheap CPM and CPC to remain profitable.
  • If CTR is above 1% but CPC or CPM is high, simplify the campaign structure. A campaign is a group of ads with one goal and one budget. Reduce the number of ad sets per campaign, for example from 4-5 down to fewer, because more complexity tends to increase CPM.
  • If CTR is poor on day 1, the ads will not improve on day 2 or 3. Test new creatives. A bad first-day CTR is a signal to change the creative, not to wait.
  • Use CTR as a sign of interest. Keep the ad running only if CTR holds up over the test budget.
  • Diagnose a campaign that is not converting, in this order:
    1. Check CTR first. If CTR is below 1%, the creative is failing; fix the hook or creative and iterate once (make one round of changes).
    2. Check CPC next. A CPC below $1.5 is good.
    3. Check CPM. A CPM above about $30 (sometimes about $35) suggests a competitive audience or weak creative that forces people to see the ad.
    4. If CTR is good but CPM is high, rework the creative anyway, because Meta (the company behind Facebook and Instagram) penalizes weak ads with higher costs.
    5. If the creative still fails after one iteration, switch to the next product instead of pushing more budget into it.
  • Fix for high CPM: improve creative quality and engagement ranking. Add social proof by manually posting customer reviews and comments on ads, such as 5-star reviews and user photos. Test different interest targeting (who the ad is shown to, based on interests) to find less-competitive audiences. Let engagement such as likes and comments build up naturally. CPM typically drops from 35-40 to 20-23 within 3-4 days as engagement builds.
  • Creatives are the main deciding factor once a product is performing. By estimate, creatives drive about 90-95% of results. Test more creatives when the low-performing metric is CTR or cost-per-click is high, since better creatives lift conversion across the funnel.
  • For the first 1-2 weeks, normal creatives are enough to see whether a product sells. Better creatives are needed once you want to scale the product further (grow its spend).

Kill, keep and wait decisions

2 min
  • Kill criteria (reasons to stop): stop an ad or product if you have spent at least the break-even cost per purchase (the most you can pay per sale without losing money) and the metrics (sales, add-to-cart, attention, CTR) show no promise. Keep iterating if attention is above 40% or CTR is above 2% and the landing page can be improved.
  • Once a product fails after one creative iteration, switch to the next product rather than adding budget.
  • Do not switch off an ad that makes revenue only because its CTR is low, if it at least breaks even. Improve the middle section of the ad to raise CTR while keeping sales coming in.
  • Keep a winning ad running as long as it delivers a good ROAS. Test new creatives in parallel (at the same time) with a new angle to prevent ad fatigue (buyers getting tired of the same ad).
  • Turning off campaigns too soon, before enough frequency (how many times one person sees the ad), is a common mistake. The mere exposure effect (people remember what they see again and again) requires about 21 impressions before a brand is remembered. Do not turn off ads before they have built the repeated exposure needed to make an impression.
  • Stay on message repeatedly and consistently. Use retargeting (showing ads again to people who already visited) with increased frequency on individual ads, and do not turn them off too soon. Be the familiar barista your audience knows and prefers.
  • Facebook ads with near-zero sales (written in 2022): check reach (how many people saw the ad) in Facebook Business Manager (Meta's ad account tool). If the audience is small, the sample is too small to judge. If the audience is large and sees ads but does not buy, test different ad copy.
  • Pinterest and other secondary platforms (written in 2022): judge by ROAS, not just cost per click. If traffic is high and sales are low, the page or offer needs work. If traffic is low and sales are still low, the account has a bigger structural problem (a deeper setup fault).

Iteration loops

2 min
  • Test new creatives in an iterative loop (repeated cycles): take an ad that performed well earlier, make variations, relaunch, and keep the ones that hold their results.
  • A winning ad keeps getting sales because it sends the right message to the right audience. Keep testing new creatives and iterate on the ones that previously worked, rather than assuming the cause of a performance drop is known.
  • Build a new test set after a creative review:
    1. Create three new ads based on the analysis.
    2. Make some of them iterations of the best-performing ad.
    3. Make the rest brand-new concepts.
  • Creative testing is central to scaling. A good message and creative attract the right audience, and small spend on each ad helps identify winners quickly. Small, controlled tweaks to a creative can improve results and should not be skipped because they feel dull.
  • Set up new creatives in the existing testing campaign. When relaunching a product with new ad copy, copy your best-performing ads into the existing testing campaign and run the new copy variations on them. Do not start a fresh campaign for each copy change.
  • Loop for turning a winning message into new concepts:
    1. In Ads Manager (Meta's ad control panel), find the creative with the highest website purchases in your CBO (campaign budget optimization: one budget shared across ad sets, which goes to the best ones).
    2. In Google AI Studio (Google's AI tool, running Gemini, Google's AI model), upload the winning video and transcribe its messaging (write its words down), plus the primary text and headline from the ad.
    3. In Claude (an AI assistant), hand over the transcript and rebuild the messaging: the angle (viewpoint), the promise, and the objection it handles (the doubt it answers).
    4. Turn the winning message into new concepts in different formats, such as podcast, conference, seminar, claymation, cartoon, or UGC AI ads (AI-made ads that look like customer videos).
    5. Launch each concept for 3 days in CBO 2 or CBO 3, then read website purchases and ROAS to see whether it resonates.
    6. Fold the winners back into the main CBO and repeat the loop.
  • Process for a new product on a proven layout (an ad design that already sold):
    1. Keep a file of ad creatives that have produced sales.
    2. For a new product, replace only the product imagery in a proven ad layout instead of building new creatives from scratch.
    3. Write or check the core benefits and the offer in the copy yourself before launch, rather than handing this to a freelancer.
    4. Launch the ad and judge it by hook rate, CTR and conversion rate.
  • Example: a test used a knee anatomy image. The first output was a 3D render (a computer-made 3D picture). A follow-up asked for two cross-sectional drawings (slice-view drawings) instead, one profile view facing left and one front-facing view. The second version was closer to the target because it looked like a textbook illustration.
  • Pattern to watch: a creative is a likely winner only when it has the best CTR consistently across all winning audiences, not just in one. Replace the older creative with it once that pattern is confirmed.
  • Before scaling an audience, build a winning combination of the audience and two of the best creatives, selected for strong CTR and CPM.

Creative fatigue and rotation

2 min
  • Ads on a single audience can lose performance over time, so new creatives keep the reach alive. Test alongside the winner rather than as a replacement for it.
  • Advertising styles wear out. Verse, narrative confessions and comic-strip stories each lost believability as they became familiar. Keep the strategy and rotate the style when it grows stale.
  • Ad creatives need regular refreshing because customers get used to the same ads. Produce new photos and videos as the previous ones lose effect.
  • TikTok account-level fatigue: the same material can stop an entire account from performing after roughly three to four weeks. Pausing the account for about a week and then restarting was reported to restore reach. Rotate new content in before results decline.
  • TikTok single-creative lifespan: a creative rarely holds more than about a week. Copied creatives often perform poorly after a few days, sometimes within one day in the US.
  • The two TikTok values in and describe different scopes: one creative versus the whole account. Both apply.
  • Pinterest creatives wear out (saturate) much more slowly than TikTok creatives. A video can run for six to seven months and older videos still work, so TikTok winners can be reused directly there.
  • Video age in paid tests: a video that is 2 to 3 months old has usually been seen by many people in the target market. This tends to lower CTR and raise CPM. Prefer creatives posted within about the last two weeks, and treat older videos as a weaker option for paid tests.
  • Viral video reuse: a creative running for more than about 15 to 30 days after going viral may already be known to TikTok's algorithm (the system that picks who sees what), so reuse gives less reach. A video with views in the 20,000 to 30,000 range three days after posting may still have room to scale.
  • Run length as a performance sign: ads that ran for 68 and 79 days were winners, so keep them and reuse their hooks and structure.

Scaling winners

1 min

Scaling means spending more money on ads that already sell. A winner is an ad that sells well.

  • Scale by combining winners. Find the winning hook (the first seconds that stop people scrolling), video, concept, thumbnail (the preview picture) and ad copy (the written words of the ad) through separate tests. Then combine the best parts in the scaling campaigns (the ad plans that grow your spend).
  • Only move the best ads into one ad set (a group of ads that share one budget and audience) and let Facebook optimise (let Facebook find the best buyers on its own). Do this only after the separate tests show which ads and countries work.
  • When scaling, prepare multiple creatives (the videos or pictures of an ad) in advance. Then you can quickly test whether a new one beats the current winner.
  • Scaling through new creatives on TikTok: create new creatives every week, even if older ones stop working. Duplicate (copy) a working ad set and put in four new creatives each week to double its effective budget (the money the ad set can really use) without raising the budget.
  • Once a product shows steady sales for one to two weeks, switch to UGC (user-generated content, videos made by real customers or creators) or TikTok-style creative as soon as possible. This can multiply results. Start UGC production in parallel (at the same time) with the first winning ads.
  • Use a winning ad's CTR (click-through rate, the share of viewers who click), CPM (cost per 1,000 views) and ROAS (return on ad spend), not impressions (how many times an ad was shown), as the basis for scaling decisions.
  • Judge a creative in the scaling phase by its ROAS first, then CTR.

Repurposing winning creatives

1 min

Repurposing means reusing a proven ad in a new form.

Same-platform and cross-platform reuse

2 min

Same-platform means on the same app again. Cross-platform means on a different app.

  • Repurposing on the same platform can test a different angle (a new way of selling) on a previous video, such as a new hook, a new voice or a new call to action (the line that tells people what to do next, such as "buy now").
  • Ways to build more videos from a winning video:
    1. Make variants (changed versions) that change the hook.
    2. Shorten the duration without cutting key points, for example from 15 seconds to 11 seconds.
    3. Recreate the same video with a different product variant, such as a red version after a blue one went viral (spread very fast).
  • Turning a proven organic video (a free post, not a paid ad) into a paid ad: take the top organic videos that performed well, keep the core content, and make small adjustments to fit the ad platform. Add a call to action and scarcity (pressure from a limited time or limited stock).
  • Hook-led rebuild (building new ads around the best opening) of a winning ad:
    1. Record a set of scenes (short clips, each one shot separately) covering the hook, the use case (how people use the product), the relief (the problem it solves) and the result, about 12 scenes in total.
    2. Find the hook that is running longest and keep the best-performing clip for it.
    3. Build new ads by pairing the winning hook with different body clips (the main middle part of the video).
    4. Keep the voice and action the same and change only the camera angle or opening clip for each new version.
    5. Track how long each ad runs and keep the ones that run past 60 days.
  • Reuse user-generated video by recutting the footage (rearranging the raw recorded material), changing the narrative (the order of the story), cropping out (cutting away) unwanted text, and adding simple transitions (effects between clips). Basic editing skills are enough.
  • Use one creative for different audiences by changing only the caption (the text under the video), for example one caption for a girlfriend, another for a boyfriend, and another for family members.
  • Recycle good product visuals by reposting them every couple of months on social media. This keeps accounts active without needing new material each time (source undated).
  • Save finished ad assets (ready-made parts such as images and videos) so you can reuse them across campaigns and change only the text. Recognizable creative helps shoppers identify the event quickly.

Seasonal planning

1 min
  • Check your seasonal buying patterns (when people buy) before planning creatives. Determine whether customers buy your product only in certain seasons or all year round. The answer determines when ads and creative themes should run.
  • A seasonal angle (a way of selling tied to a season) lets a seasonal product sell where the weather is not yet right. A pre-summer sale, for example, lets summer dresses sell in countries where the weather is not yet warm.
  • For a holiday, start organic testing (free posts to test) early. For example, begin on January 1 for Valentine's Day. Test many different hooks before the peak period begins (the busiest shopping time).
  • Holiday remarketing (showing ads again to people who already visited your shop): use a gift-related image, such as gift boxes, with the current offer printed on the image. The link should go to the holiday collection page (the shop page with holiday products).
  • Stop running Christmas ads from mid-December, because products ordered then will not arrive before Christmas and customers will be disappointed.
  • Reuse Black Friday ads that still perform by changing the headline (the big title line) and ad copy and launching them as a new Christmas ad. Test new ads only if they stop performing.

Peak CPM seasons and Black Friday / Cyber Monday

1 min

Peak CPM seasons are times when ad cost (CPM) rises. BFCM means Black Friday and Cyber Monday, the big sale days in November.

Managing peak cost

2 min
  • During BFCM, when CPM is high, do not create many new creatives. Scale existing winning creatives by adding offer banners (strips of text across the image that show the deal) and testing those variations.
  • Reducing ad cost during peak CPM seasons:
    1. Test better creatives, with new ones in parallel at small budgets.
    2. Add offer banners to existing winning creatives, for example "Early BFCM Sale" or "Halloween Sale Ongoing".
    3. Banners can be static images (still pictures), animations, or continuous slideshows.
    4. Focus scaling spend on banner-enhanced versions (versions with a banner added) of proven creatives, not on building entirely new creative.
  • For Black Friday, test one or two dedicated creatives (made just for this sale) that clearly show the offer, for example with a banner, rather than producing complex productions. Keep the message simple: the offer itself is usually enough.
  • Use dedicated Black Friday creatives in these formats:
    • Square image ad (a picture with equal width and height): black background with a sales banner, three product images below, headline "Black Friday sale is now on", and text "Get up to 50% off, get yours now". Stories ad (a full-screen vertical ad): the 9×16 portrait version of the same.
    • Overlay method (placing a new layer of text or shapes on top) on proven video creatives:
      1. Take existing winning video creatives.
      2. Import them into Canva (an online design tool).
      3. Resize to 4:5 ratio (the frame shape, 4 wide by 5 tall): 1350×1920px (px means pixels, the tiny dots an image is made of).
      4. Add a black banner at the top: "Black Friday event now on".
      5. Add a black banner at the bottom: "Get up to 50% off".
      6. Export (save the final file) and run it as a video ad. This overlay method works extremely well on proven winning creatives.
    • Note: 1350×1920 px is a ratio (width divided by height) of about 0.70, not 4:5 (which is 0.80, for example 1080×1350). Check the export size before upload.
  • Black Friday approach used in one example: take the best-performing existing creatives, add a Black Friday sale banner or overlay with "up to 60% off" and the end date, and export in both 9:16 and 1:1 square. 9:16 is tall, for Stories and Reels (short vertical videos). 1:1 square is for the Facebook feed (the main scrolling page of posts). Videos were cut to 4:5 for the Facebook feed with a top and bottom banner.
  • Save finished BFCM assets so they can be reused across campaigns. Only the text changes, and recognizable creative helps shoppers identify the event quickly.
