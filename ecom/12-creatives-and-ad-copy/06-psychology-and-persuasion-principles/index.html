Playbook › 12. Creatives and ad copy

Psychology and persuasion principles

Section 12.6 · 6 of 26 in this chapter · 46 min read

In short

  • Buyers feel first and think second. Most decisions start as emotion, and logic is used afterward to justify them.
  • Learn the real pain before you write. Find what the customer fears, wants, and feels, in their own words.
  • Tie each offer to one basic human desire, such as safety, comfort, status, or belonging.
  • Fear sells only with a fix. Give every scary point a small, doable next step.
  • Before you write a word, pick one feeling the reader should have when they finish.
More key points
  • Buyers act on feelings. Logic only backs them up. Build your copy on four core emotions: New, Easy, Safe, and Big.
  • Start with the buyer's own words: 30 quotes from customer reviews.
  • Never blame the buyer for past failures. Blame the old solutions instead.
  • Test two versions of an ad before you spend your budget.

Diagnosing the Buyer's Emotional State

3 min

Build the emotional picture from customer research before you write any copy. Copy means the words you write to sell.

  • The Psychology Lens is a checklist. A checklist is a list of questions you check one by one. It has seven areas:
    1. How to find deep emotional pain under the surface complaints.
    2. How to measure desire strength from the words customers use.
    3. How to spot belief loops and doubt patterns that block a purchase. A belief loop is a thought that keeps repeating and stops the buyer from acting.
    4. How to read what customers say accurately, without adding your own assumptions.
    5. What makes someone keep searching for a solution, versus giving up.
    6. Identity-based motivations and self-image drivers. Identity means how the buyer sees themselves. Drivers are the reasons that push them.
    7. How to map transformation desires in real, human terms. Transformation means the change the buyer wants in their life.
  • Its principles come only from Breakthrough Advertising, The Bencivenga 100, Scientific Advertising, and Cashvertising.
  • Use them as a working checklist, not as old-school direct-response tone. Direct-response means ads that push for an immediate answer, like "Buy now."

A prompt is an instruction you give an AI. This is the prompt used to build the checklist:

Extract a working checklist from the core psychology frameworks (Schwartz, Bencivenga, Hopkins, Whitman) covering how to recognize emotional pain in research, gauge how intensely someone desires change, spot belief barriers and patterns of skepticism, read customer language accurately, understand what sustains search versus triggers surrender, recognize identity-based motivations, and translate transformation desires into human terms-structured as diagnostic questions and signals a writer looks for, not book summaries or copy examples.

  • The Emotional Valence Framework studies how a customer feels. Valence means how positive or negative a feeling is.

  • It reveals deep pain points, desires, guilt, shame, and fear.

  • It also reveals objections (reasons they say no) and thought patterns.

  • Use these drivers to write about what customers really care about. Do not write generic messages.

  • Emotion sets how wide attention is.

  • Positive feelings widen attention. People notice more options and small details.

  • Negative feelings narrow attention. People focus on one thing and block out the rest.

  • This is the weapon-focus effect: when scared, your eyes lock onto the threat, like staring at a weapon and missing everything else in the room.

  • Emotional core requirements. For each concept, define these five things:

    1. Name the main emotion (fear, guilt, shame, hope, relief, and so on).
    2. Name the negative self-view the ad speaks to. A self-view is how someone sees themselves.
    3. Describe the emotional story the viewer gets pulled into.
    4. Define the mirror moment. In the first 3 seconds, the viewer feels seen. They think something like "This is me," "This is my kid," or "Finally someone gets it."
    5. Define the emotional permission. This is what the viewer is allowed to feel or believe. It names which guilt or shame it removes and which hope it brings back.

  • Research attention-narrowing emotions. These are feelings that make people focus on one thing.

  • Study four sub-types of each. A sub-type is a smaller kind of the same emotion.

  • Find at least 10 sourced statements per sub-type. A sourced statement is a quote with a named source.

    • Fear: immediate, long-term, social.
    • Shame: private, public, anticipated, body.
    • Guilt: action, responsibility, comparison, caregiver.
    • Anger: systemic, personal, self-directed, chronic resentment.
    • Helplessness: circumstantial, knowledge, resource, desperation.
    • Grief: identity, relationship, opportunity, witnessing loss.

  • The Distressed Buyer Paradox: people who are very stressed are your most ready buyers.

  • Yet they respond less to happy, positive messages. Stress shrinks their attention, so positive ideas do not land.

  • Aspirational messages show a dream life. These often fail with stressed people.

  • They must first feel that you understand their bad state. Only then can they think about good outcomes.

  • Highly distressed prospects often answer weakly to positive cues. A prospect is a possible buyer.

  • Their ability to widen attention is weaker. So aspirational ads can miss the buyers most ready to buy.

  • They first need to feel understood in their bad state.

Desires, Identities and Beliefs

4 min

  • The eight primary human desires are basic drives. They are also called Life-Force 8. They are:

    1. survival and extended life;
    2. enjoyment of food and drink;
    3. freedom from fear, pain, and danger;
    4. sexual companionship;
    5. comfortable living conditions;
    6. being superior and winning, keeping up with others;
    7. care and protection of loved ones;
    8. social approval.

  • Tie each offer to at least one of them. These drives are built in. They need no learning.

  • Most advertisers never clearly speak to the eight primary desires.

  • Yet these appeals drive most purchases.

  • Before you write, check: does the ad name a need for survival, comfort, status, or belonging, in plain words?

  • The nine learned secondary wants are:

    • being informed;
    • curiosity;
    • cleanliness;
    • efficiency;
    • convenience;
    • dependability and quality;
    • expression of beauty and style;
    • economy and profit;
    • bargains.

  • "Learned" means people pick them up over time. They are not built in.

  • They are weaker than the primary desires. Use them as support, not as the main hook.

  • Desire forms in three steps: Tension → Desire → Action.

  • Tension is the discomfort of a need that is not met.

  • The ad should offer the fastest way to ease that discomfort.

  • Readers enjoy seeing how another person met a desire. They picture themselves in that story.

  • This is called vicarious fulfilment: getting satisfaction through someone else's win.

  • So case-style copy about a customer's result works well.

  • Test one product against different desires. See which one gets the response.

  • A headline or offer that names one of the strongest desires usually beats a neutral one.

  • The change can be as small as the title or hook. A hook is the first line that grabs attention.

  • Case: a mail-order book seller in the 1920s and 1930s sold over 200 million cheap books.

  • Each book cost 5 cents. There were nearly 2,000 titles.

  • When a title sold poorly, he changed only the title.

  • The new title appealed to a Life-Force 8 desire. He tracked annual sales. Results:

Title version Annual sales before Annual sales after Desire appealed to
Title 1 (Ten O'Clock) 2,000 9,000 Social approval
Title 2 (Fleece of Gold) 5,000 50,000 Sexual companionship
Title 3 (Art of Controversy) 0 30,000 Being superior and winning
Title 4 (Casanova and His Loves) 8,000 22,000 Sexual companionship
Title 5 (Apothegems) 2,000 9,000 Survival and life extension

The two strongest appeals were sexual companionship and self-improvement.

  • Hormozi's Value Model is a way to rate how valuable an offer is. It has three levers. A lever is a control you can push:

    • Move the buyer toward the wanted state, or away from pain.
    • Raise how likely they think they are to get it.
    • Lower the time, effort, and sacrifice needed to get there.

  • To raise motivation, ask "what does that mean?" again and again. Ask it about the problem and about the result you want.

  • When you show a problem, make it feel worse. To villainize it means to make it look like an enemy.

  • When you show a solution, stress the good result the customer will get.

  • Look at three parts of the prospect's mind:

    • Desires: physical, material, and sensual wants.
    • Identifications: roles and personality traits they want to show to others.
    • Beliefs: opinions, prejudices, and reasoning habits that filter new information.

  • Desires and identifications can be amplified and directed.

  • Beliefs must be worked with, not argued against.

  • Identifications are the roles and self-images a prospect wants to show. "Project" means show to others.

  • A product that fits a role they admire gives them a reason to buy beyond what it does.

  • For example: a low-calorie food for a radiant personality, or a car that signals success.

  • Show people who already live in the product's world. This makes the role easy to see.

  • Jewelry and other lifestyle products often have no clear problem. They sell on feelings:

    • looks;
    • status;
    • self-expression;
    • how the buyer feels wearing or giving them.

  • Make problem-solving claims only where a real problem exists.

  • Avoid direct insults toward the buyer.

  • Luxury goods marketing leads with how the buyer will look, feel, and be seen. This means status and experience.

  • Technical details come later, such as speed, horsepower, or safety.

  • The same order works for any product that sells on emotion first. **

  • People buy the feeling a product gives them, not the result itself.

  • Every message should raise or lower feelings like joy, fear, passion, love, anger, pain, shame, or guilt.

  • Build desire by describing what the buyer will feel and experience.

  • Do not describe how the product works.

  • Leave out the mechanics until the feeling is built.

  • Sell the benefit of the benefit. A benefit is a good result. This means the good result behind the first good result.

  • Do not say: "imagine having more leads so you could have more money and more time."

  • Say: "Imagine what more leads could mean for you and your business."

  • Let the customer connect the dots in their own mind.

  • Customers are often wrong about what they need. So challenge what they think.

  • Offer a different solution. This is the "eureka moment," the sudden "aha."

  • Then close with the benefit of the benefit, in emotional language.

  • Challenge the customer to think differently about their problem.

  • Ask yourself: "Am I challenging my customer to think differently about their problem?"

  • Good sellers challenge what prospects think they need.

  • Then they offer the right solution to the right problem.

  • Five core emotional appeals:

    • make them cry (emotional connection);
    • make them buy (action);
    • make them think (challenge their view);
    • make them feel (emotional state);
    • make them act (urgency).

Emotion Drives the Decision, Logic Confirms It

3 min

  • Most buying decisions happen subconsciously, meaning without the person noticing.

  • The subconscious sends its signal to the conscious mind as emotion.

  • Then buyers look for logical reasons to back a choice they already made.

  • They remember the reasons, not the feeling that drove them. **

  • Copy should first trigger the right emotions. Then it should give logical support.

  • That way, the buyer's conscious mind has the reason it needs to go ahead.

  • Copy with only logic tends to convert worse. To convert means to turn a visitor into a buyer.

  • Copy that mainly drives emotion tends to do better. **

  • Two routes to persuasion exist:

    • The central route uses logic, evidence, and detailed reasoning.
    • The peripheral route uses pleasant images, quick cues, and associations.

  • Choose the route that matches how much thought the buyer gives the purchase.

If the product requires Then the copy should
Central route (high-cost or high personal relevance purchases) Present facts, statistics, evidence, testimonials, studies, reports, and case histories, woven into the strongest sales argument
Peripheral route (low-cost, low-involvement purchases) Use colorful, pleasant images, popular subject matter, or celebrity sponsorship; still include the basic data buyers need, such as size, quantity, or rating

  • Attitudes built through central-route thinking last longer.

  • They also resist competitor messages better than attitudes built on images alone.

  • For a high-cost purchase, invest in reasoning and evidence, not cues.

  • Six influence cues are remembered as CLARCCS. A cue is a quick signal that the brain reacts to.

    • Comparison.
    • Liking.
    • Authority (a trusted expert).
    • Reciprocation (giving back when someone gives to you).
    • Commitment and consistency.
    • Scarcity (limited supply).

  • These cues work as mental shortcuts.

  • They work best on peripheral thinkers. So they matter less for costly or complex purchases.

  • Stec and Bernstein's three persuasion heuristics for ads. A heuristic is a quick rule the brain uses to decide.

    • Length-Implies-Strength: long copy with credible facts seems true.
    • Liking-Agreement (Balance Theory): people trust sources that share their views.
    • Consensus-Implies-Correctness: a large group's favorable opinion makes the product seem correct.

  • Build copy that triggers at least one of these.

  • For Liking-Agreement, show values the buyer already holds.

  • Evidence is any fact, testimonial (a customer quote), endorsement (someone's approval), study, chart, or video that the advertiser did not create.

  • Readers trust outside proof more.

  • So evidence is more believable than the advertiser's own claims.

  • Readers want to know what they gain before they give up money, time, or effort.

  • Answer that question early.

  • Fear of loss makes the reader's benefit the first thing to establish.

  • Length suggests strength. Long copy with many credible facts makes readers think the product must be good.

  • Many testimonials or numbered reasons work the same way.

  • Show many customer photos and testimonials, not just one.

  • Quantity alone builds perceived credibility.

  • Build copy on persuasion principles such as:

    • problem-agitate-solution (name the problem, make it hurt, then give the fix);
    • social proof (others already use it and like it);
    • authority;
    • scarcity;
    • future pacing (show the buyer living the result);
    • curiosity;
    • Hormozi's time, effort, and sacrifice equation.

  • Feed each principle to the AI as a named list with a short explanation.

  • Then ask the AI to apply the best ones to the copy.

  • Use the word "you" on purpose. It turns a one-way speech into a real conversation.

  • Confirm the reader's values and emotions.

  • This builds emotional transfer (the reader feels what you describe) and thought validation (the reader feels their thinking is right).

  • Command phrases are used because they match NLP-style language. NLP (neuro-linguistic programming) is a set of speaking and thinking techniques.

  • They trigger a mental picture automatically when read.

  • Treat this as practical testing, not as proof from psychology. **

Negative and Positive Emotional Balance

2 min

  • The Affect Heuristic is a rule the brain uses: the current feeling shapes the decision. Affect means feeling.

  • A good mood lowers perceived risk. A bad mood raises it.

  • Mix negative language (the risks of not acting) with positive language (the benefits of your offer).

  • End on a clear positive note before the call to action. A call to action tells the reader what to do next.

  • Net-positive messaging is like a Hollywood blockbuster hero film. Net-positive means the good outweighs the bad in the end.

  • Use enough drama and danger to create interest, with an uplifting ending.

  • Link negative feelings to competitors, other methods, and doing nothing.

  • Link positive feelings, ideas, and events to your offer.

  • Avoid overplaying feelings or sounding manipulative.

  • Match the intensity to how big the topic is.

  • Negative-first order is for highly distressed prospects.

  • Negative hooks hold attention on the problem, the pain, or the mechanism (how the problem works).

  • Then positive hooks widen awareness of the change and the lifestyle.

  • Meet customers where they are emotionally. Then lead them toward solutions.

  • Where sources differ on order:

    • The net-positive ending applies to general messaging.
    • Negative-first order applies when prospects are highly distressed and their attention is narrowed.

  • Use the first for ordinary buyers.

  • Use the second for buyers in acute distress. Acute means sharp and severe.

  • Use an emotional rollercoaster within one message.

  • Start with humor. Move into a vulnerable story, one where you admit something hard. Return to a clear call to action.

  • Messages that trigger more emotions in one sitting tend to move people to act more often.

  • Rule of thumb for emotional messages:

    • If the product solves a problem, make the problem worse first. Show its painful results. Then present the product as the relief. Example pattern for a cleaning brush: show the embarrassment of hair everywhere, then the brush cleans it in minutes so guests come over.
    • If the product offers a benefit but solves no problem, make the benefit better. Stress the emotional gain. Example pattern for a lamp: describe the beautiful night sky or firework look it creates in the bedroom.

  • Show enough proof that the benefits are more than double the purchase risk.

  • Also amplify the risks of not buying.

  • Give strong value to dig out of the "risk hole." This hole is the doubt every buyer starts with by default.

  • Raise the stakes without fear-mongering. Fear-mongering means scaring people with exaggeration.

  • Share real potential risks.

  • Dig for deeper issues, not unlikely surface risks.

    • For example: "unhappiness and lack of fulfillment," not "unlikely business failure."

  • Appeal to universal needs and desires.

  • Repeated association: find 3-10 things, people, or values your audience already likes. Link your offer to them.

  • Find 3-10 negative associations. Contrast them with your offer.

  • Repeat this pattern to build a strong argument: [thing they like] + [what I'm selling] + [thing they don't like] + [how my offer helps avoid that]

Fear, Stress, and Pain

1 min

  • A fear appeal is an ad that uses fear to push action. The recipe has four steps. All four must be present. Remove one, and the appeal fails.

    1. Make the threat feel real and strong enough to create stress.
    2. Give a specific recommendation for reducing the threat.
    3. Show the action works against the threat.
    4. Convince the reader they can carry out that action.

  • Fear only motivates when your product can credibly solve the feared problem.

  • If the product cannot solve the threat, no amount of scary copy will convert.

  • Too much fear can paralyze the reader, so they do nothing.

  • Pair every threat with a concrete, achievable next step.

  • Also give proof that the reader can take that step.

  • Fear appeals work best when the fear is specific and already widely known. Example: sun damage to skin.

  • Avoid trying to create a new fear few people hold.

  • That takes far more effort and works less well.

  • Fear creates stress. Stress creates a desire to act to remove it.

  • The most common stress triggers in copy are loss, personal safety, and future regret.

  • Pain-point copy pairs two things:

    • The visible symptom the buyer sees;
    • The deeper fear behind it, about their own ability.

  • So the buyer recognizes both the problem and what is at stake.

  • The fear drives the purchase. The symptom makes it concrete.

    • "The tomatoes keep dying and I'm scared I'm wasting another season of planting."
    • "The faucet drips again after my fix, and I'm scared the next wrong step floods the kitchen."

  • When a product relieves pain or discomfort, the reader imagines their morning routine.

  • They picture the reduced pain or swelling (inflammation) it could bring.

  • This imagined end state triggers the feelings they would have in it.

  • It makes them feel positive about buying. **

Nostalgia

2 min

  • Nostalgia comes from Greek: nostos (return home) and algos (pain).

  • It is a warm longing for the past, triggered by things in the present.

  • It looks back through rose-colored glasses. That means it remembers mostly the good parts, in a reshaped version of history.

  • It is personal and emotional.

  • Nostalgia weakens the desire to hold onto money.

  • It also builds social connection (Lasaleta, Sedikides & Vohs 2014).

  • It is delicate but powerful, so use it with respect.

  • Nostalgia works at two campaign levels:

    1. Demographic-specific branding: aimed at one age group. Demographic means a group defined by age or background. Use it only if you target one core generation. The risk is alienating everyone else.
    2. Campaign level: throwbacks and touchstones (things that bring back old memories). This is the most common approach for entrepreneurs.

  • At brand level, use nostalgia if the company has a long, interesting history. It also fits if the brand ties to heritage (history and tradition), values, or causes.

  • At product level, use it especially for physical products or security-focused services. Vintage design projects reliability.

  • Nostalgia tips:

    • Use "Remember when?" moments of magic that can never be fully repeated.
    • Link present, familiar, loved brands to past feelings.
    • Use visuals and music to trigger nostalgia.
    • Frame campaigns that call back to heritage, shared history, or throwbacks for one group.

  • The past feels safe and predictable.

  • So nostalgia moves people by bringing back feelings from long ago.

  • Trigger nostalgia through sensory recognition. Sensory means through the senses. Do not rely on direct talk about the past.

  • Formula (steps):

    1. Start with a sensory experience in the present (smell, sound, light, texture, or taste).
    2. Add a small sensory detail that seems like background.
    3. Let recognition build as the detail links to a deeper memory.
    4. Fully land in the emotion (belonging, safety, loss, or joy).
    5. Show that this feeling is available now, through your product.

  • Nostalgia works through emotional recognition.

  • Readers do not "remember" in their head. They viscerally (deep in the gut) feel "I know this."

  • This builds a bridge from past experience to today's decision.

    Heritage cooking service example: "You catch the scent of something-butter, or cinnamon, or the warmth of a kitchen you haven't entered in years. For a moment you're not sure where you are. Then you remember: someone you loved, moving with certainty, making something for you without being asked. That feeling of being cared for. We help you give that same gift."

    Family archive service example: "An old box surfaces in your attic with handwriting you recognize before you remember who it belongs to. The certainty in those letters, the person who wrote them believing you'd someday want to know them. A feeling of being held across decades. We help you preserve these connections so they reach forward like they reached back."

Framing Gains, Losses, and Numbers

1 min

  • Framing effect: decisions vary with whether options are presented as gains or losses. Loss is psychologically about 2x more impactful than an equivalent gain (prospect theory). A $5 discount is less impactful than avoiding a $5 surcharge, even though the net result is identical.

  • Prospect theory and loss aversion: the pain of losing registers about 2x the impact of the pleasure of gaining an equivalent amount. To emotionally "fix" a loss of $100, one needs to gain $200. People prefer sure small losses over even likely potential gains when they perceive a risk of a larger loss. It is better not to lose $5 than to find $5, illogical but psychologically true.

  • Contextual savings frame: compare your offer's cost to recognizable examples to lower perceived expense, such as "less than a cup of coffee per day" or "less than 10% of a college course price."

  • Picking the right number:

    • When the number is good, pick the larger frame: "200% growth" rather than "2x growth," "30% savings" rather than "$7 savings."
    • When the number is bad, pick the smaller frame: "losing only 1%" rather than "losing $100."
    • When perspectives differ, pick the positive one: "80% fat-free" rather than "20% fat."

  • Savings or penalty frame: highlight the available savings and also the risk of not taking them, such as "Save 20% today only! Don't miss out!" Deadline urgency is key.

Anchors and Choice Architecture

1 min

  • Anchoring bias: the first information heard sets a focal point for everything that follows. Prices discussed below the anchor seem reasonable, even if still high. Create your own anchor by comparing pricing with something familiar that sheds favorable light, such as "A full-time employee costs $50,000-$100,000, but our system costs only $2,000."

  • Use anchors: compare with competitor prices, the cost of "trying to figure it out on your own," or the opportunity cost of not acting sooner. These help readers see the value in what you sell.

  • Anchoring pattern: state a high reference value first so the actual offer looks small in comparison. Pattern: "Get [result] in a fraction of the time it takes others, [short time] instead of [long time]." Price anchoring: show a higher reference price (such as a consultation worth a large amount, or a premium option) next to your price, so the middle or real offer looks like the better choice.

    • "Clean your whole kitchen in 15 minutes instead of a full weekend."
    • "Two years of expert support, normally 2,000 USD, now 149 USD."

  • Choice overload (overchoice bias): more choices lead to decision paralysis and dissatisfaction. A grocery store study found 24 jam options attracted more people but only 3% bought, while 6 options attracted fewer people but 30% bought. Streamline configurations, offer no more than 3 pricing tiers, minimize information on forms, and present options visually.

  • Rule of Two: too many choices overwhelm, but a single option feels risky because the reader can always leave. Present two specific options to invite a conscious choice instead of a habitual exit. Example: "Sign up today" or "No, I'm not interested in learning about gardening."

  • Help readers calculate their choices: present multiple options or perspectives, then systematically reduce them to a few core ones, ideally favoring your argument. This earns trust by simplifying the decision.

Social Proof and Bandwagon

2 min

  • Social proof: people follow what others do as a shortcut. Show customer counts such as thousands of owners, viral popularity, or high ratings from existing customers.

  • Social proof pattern: point to what many people already do, so the viewer follows the group. This works best when the claim is specific and true, such as a share of users or a popular bestseller.

    • "Most people in your city switched to a lighter running jacket this season, and the sales show why."
    • "Most home bakers still buy their flour from the same store, but the bakers who switched say the loaves rise better."

  • Statement pattern with scale and emotional outcome: include a specific number of satisfied customers ("50K+", "10,000+", "25,000+") paired with an emotional descriptor of their resulting state ("relieved and relaxed", "sleeping soundly", "pain-free"). The number is concrete rather than vague like "thousands," and the emotion shows the actual benefit, not just "satisfied customers."

    • "100,000+ customers waking up refreshed."
    • "75,000+ users standing taller and confident."

  • Popularity claims: state a verified ranking or a preference by a specific group, so the reader infers that many others chose the product. Use a specific number and a named audience, such as a "most recommended by" claim tied to a real professional group. Write new claims only where the ranking or survey behind them can be documented.

  • Comparison cue: people feel pressure to conform to what friends, family, and peers choose. Show the product being chosen by a clearly defined group that the target buyer belongs to or wants to join.

  • Bandwagon effect: the rate of adoption of beliefs increases as more people adopt them. Apply it by showing the reader is not alone ("join over 10,000 happy customers"), and/or that they may be left behind if they don't act (FOMO). Use phrases such as "Most people already know," "Thousands have figured out," and "Join hundreds of happy customers." Include many testimonials. Show real-time signals such as "Richard just signed up" or "Twelve people are viewing this room."

Authority, Liking, and Reciprocity

1 min
  • Cialdini's six principles, to use in copy: reciprocity (give value or free resources first so the customer feels they owe something); commitment and consistency (ask for a small commitment first, such as an email for a discount, then build to bigger asks, and label the customer's identity so they stay consistent with it); social proof (testimonials and customer numbers); authority (credible experts, publications, awards, or endorsements); liking (similarity such as "made by dog owners for dog owners," genuine compliments, and cooperation toward the customer's goals); scarcity (limited stock or rare availability makes the product seem more valuable).
  • Reciprocity: people feel they should return a favor, so offering value first, such as free information, education, or a free sample, creates a sense of obligation to buy later.
  • Reciprocity in ads: give useful information or value first, and the viewer feels more inclined to act in return. Pair the value with a clear call to action.
  • Authority: people trust credible, knowledgeable sources. Use industry awards, magazine recognition, approval from professionals in the field (such as vets), and celebrity endorsements to transfer credibility to the product.
  • Authority cue: people accept claims from official-looking or expert figures, so a credible expert endorsement can replace much of the reader's research. Testimonials and short expert videos are common ways to use it.
  • Liking: people say yes to those they like, through three routes: similarity ("I am like you"), genuine compliments, and cooperation (showing you help the customer reach their goals). "Made by dog owners for dog owners" uses similarity and cooperation together.
  • Liking cue: people buy from those they like, including representatives, characters in ads, and other users. Attractive faces are judged more trustworthy and likable, and people tend to identify with models of their own gender.

Commitment and Consistency

1 min

  • Commitment and consistency (Four Walls), in order. In print, the chain must be built into the copy, since there is no social pressure from a live person.

    1. Ask a question the prospect will certainly agree with.
    2. Ask a second question that follows logically from the first agreement.
    3. Continue with a chain of questions that each lead to the next, so the reader has said yes several times.
    4. Finish with the purchase request, which the reader now feels bound to honor.

  • Start with a small, low-cost ask, such as an email signup in exchange for a discount, then ask for more later. Questions the reader will almost certainly answer yes to build a chain of small agreements.

  • Commitment through an agreed opening statement: start with a point the reader will almost always agree with, such as a shared goal for their pet, home, or family, then ask for a small next step. Once the reader agrees with the first statement, saying no to the next request feels inconsistent with what they just said.

    • "Of course you want your kitchen to stay grease-free between cleanings, so try the first step free for seven days."
    • "Of course you want your kids to get to school on time, so join the list for a weekly routine checklist."

  • Choice-supportive bias (commitment bias): people retroactively ascribe positive attributes to the option they chose and downplay its faults, while amplifying the faults of rejected options. Readers make a choice by engaging with your copy, so help them feel good about it, especially before high-price purchases. Use positive language near the CTA ("Good move!", "Great choice!"), after the sale ("Awesome!", "Thank you!"), and send unexpected bonuses around the refund period to turn potential refunds into happy customers.

Scarcity, Loss Aversion, and FOMO

1 min
  • Loss aversion and fear of missing out: people dislike losing something they could gain, so frame the offer as something they would lose by waiting. Limited-time discounts, limited stock, and low-stock messages make people act to avoid losing an offer. They are strong when combined with a real deadline or a real stock limit, and they link closely to the stakes shown in the story.
  • Scarcity: limited stock or rare availability makes the product seem more valuable.

Envy, Status, and Keeping Up

1 min
  • Envy and keeping up with others are strong desire triggers: people want what others have and feel uneasy about being behind. Use them carefully, since blunt versions can read as manipulative. A softer version is to show that most respected or successful people in the niche already use the product.
  • Envy and scorn: people envy those who have what they lack and look down on those who lack what they have, so this can motivate buying. Use it indirectly, because direct comparisons with other people come across as manipulative and can drive customers away.
  • Keeping up with others: people do not want to fall behind their peers or status group, so show that the people they admire already use the product. Phrasing such as "why haven't you caught up with other top owners" triggers this need.

Curiosity, Shock, and Effort

1 min
  • Curiosity, shock, and specific claims pull readers into the body copy. Curiosity can be created with a question or an open loop. Shock must be qualified so it attracts the right audience, not just any attention.
  • Shock grabs attention, but shocking statements used only for effect attract the wrong audience, who will not buy. Use shock only when it connects directly to the product's problem and the buyer you want.
  • Reduce time, effort, and sacrifice in the copy. Show how fast the result comes (for example, a few swipes instead of hours), how little effort is needed, and what the customer does not have to give up, such as time with family or other activities. Use the word "just" to make effort feel small. Ask what the customer would have to give up without the product, and make clear they no longer need to give it up.

Breakthrough Advertising Tactics

1 min
  • Intensification raises the reader's desire and need for the product by making the current pain and the wanted result feel more urgent and vivid, without adding new facts. Use it in a headline or opening line when the audience already knows the problem but has not yet acted. Use it to sharpen wording around the real benefit, not to add claims the product cannot support.
  • Identification ties the product to how the buyer wants to be seen, including status and self-image. Work out what the buyer wants to become, then frame the product as a step toward that self-image. It works best when the market has heard many similar claims, so copy speaks to who the customer is and who they want to become, not to product features. Copy that ties the product to a desired identity, such as a certain kind of owner or host, connects more strongly than copy that only lists features.
  • Gradualization starts from a belief the reader already holds and moves them step by step toward the belief you want them to adopt. Each sentence first accepts the reader's current view before shifting it, instead of stating the new belief all at once. It applies when the reader does not yet believe your core claim. For example, a pet owner who already agrees that fur cleaning is tiring will accept a claim about a faster method more easily than a bold claim with no common ground.
  • Redefinition handles objections. It reframes how the reader defines the problem or the cost so the objection stops being a reason to say no. Write down the objection the customer already has, then restate it in terms that favour the product. For example, a higher price can be framed as cost per use. List the questions a prospect is likely to ask before buying, and answer each one directly in the copy.

Self-Image, Ego, and Position

1 min
  • Illusory superiority: most people think they are better, or different, than average, so objections like "maybe for them, but not for me" are the default. Address this by validating the concern first, then reframing it positively.
  • Forer (Barnum) effect: people rate vague, generic descriptions as if they were tailored to them personally. Use Barnum statements such as "you're the kind of person who [generic positive trait]" to validate reader emotions and transfer your intended emotional state. This works because everyone identifies with well-chosen universal values.
  • Good vs. evil frame: position your brand or offer as the hero or powerful ally (the real hero). Position conflicting forces as the enemy, either internal (mindset, habits, ignorance) or external (inferior products, time, government).
  • Us vs. everyone else ("community frame"): position joining your community through purchase as taking a stand for a positive value that "everyone else" has not yet joined.
  • "Unique in a sea of the same" frame: exploit innovation bias by presenting your offer, approach, message, or brand as new and innovative. Build an argument for why the new way is better and why the old way leads to problems.
  • "Because I'm worth it" frame: position your offer in terms of the customer's entitlement to benefits. For example: "You've worked hard to build this; you deserve the peace of mind that comes with knowing it's in the right hands."
  • Pro-innovation bias: the belief that an innovation should be adopted without need for alternatives. The champion may not see its limitations but keeps promoting it. Readers have "shiny object syndrome," so polish the offer by explaining why it is innovative and new. Compare it to "the old way" or state directly why it is innovative. Use the word "innovative" in headlines, such as "Innovative New Approach Unlocks Hidden Potential." Frame even non-innovations as new to the customer, such as "Innovative new device lets you call people while in your car."

Repetition, Familiarity, and Persuasion Limits

1 min
  • Mere exposure effect: people develop preferences for things merely because they are familiar with them. Each impression of an ad or brand is rated more favorably when seen regularly. Repeated exposure to new or unfamiliar products is most helpful. Frequency + Impact + Time = winning message. It takes an average of 21 impressions before a brand is remembered.
  • Repetition builds familiarity and trust, but there is an upper limit. Research showed agreement rising after three exposures and falling sharply after five. Cap the frequency of the same message and monitor for audience fatigue. Where sources differ on frequency: 21 impressions measures brand recall, while the three-to-five exposure range measures agreement with a message. Use the first to plan awareness and the second to cap a persuasive message.
  • Use redundancy instead of plain repetition. Express the same message in different wording, format, and sources so the reader sees a fresh version. Multiple different voices making the same point build conviction faster than one voice repeated.

Handling Beliefs and Objections

2 min

  • Beliefs can be changed in two ways: by changing focus (using emotion or evidence to present a different view of reality) or by changing importance (strengthening beliefs the reader already holds). Strengthening existing beliefs with facts and examples is easier and more reliable than trying to overturn them.

  • Advertising does not win arguments with beliefs. Accept them as they are and build from them using the prospect's own kind of logic, to show that the product satisfies their desires, works, is relied on by people like them, and beats other products on the need they have.

  • Telling a reader they are wrong triggers a defensive reaction that ends the sale. Compare facts and evidence side by side instead, and let the reader reach the conclusion without feeling attacked.

  • Inoculation copy, in order:

    1. Warn the reader that a competitor or opposing view is coming.
    2. Present a weak version of that opposing argument.
    3. Encourage the reader to defend their current choice with reasons they can use.

    Competitor criticism answered this way strengthens the reader's loyalty.

  • The counter-argument in an inoculation must be weak. A strong attack can change the reader's mind against you.

  • Preemptive comparison: tell the prospect what to check when comparing offers, such as how a competitor cuts corners, then show that your method avoids the problem. Name specific, verifiable differences such as ingredient sourcing, process time, or guaranteed service time. Write new examples in this same form for your own market.

  • Comparison charts that show advantages at a glance are effective because they give peripheral thinkers a ready conclusion. Include only differences you can document, and cover benefits such as speed, ease, cleanliness, health, and price.

  • Two-sided messages persuade more than one-sided ones only when they defend the advertiser's own position while openly weighing the competitor's strengths. Acknowledge what the competitor did well in the past, explain what has changed, and then present your own advantage.

  • Naming the buyer's thought: before you pitch, name the thought your audience is already having about you, such as age, appearance, credibility, or past bad experiences with sellers. Agree that the thought is reasonable, often with light humor, then move straight to your evidence. This lowers resistance and gets people to listen.

    • "You're probably thinking this is one more brand promising overnight results, and you'd be right to be skeptical. Here's what the numbers actually show."
    • "You've likely seen a dozen stores this week shouting about huge savings, so here's what I'd check before buying anything."

Sequencing the Emotional Message

1 min
  • Emotional sequence for high-distress buyers, in order. Tag each script section [NARROWING] or [BROADENING].
    1. Open with a negative emotion that holds attention.
    2. Stay in the negative state long enough to validate it.
    3. Make the transition with permission language that gives the viewer leave to hope.
    4. Present the solution with positive emotions and identity transformation.
    5. Close with a CTA that matches the viewer's state: urgency for narrowed attention, possibility for broadened attention.
  • Sequence the message from acknowledgment to hope: negative emotional hooks hold attention on the problem; once the buyer feels understood, permission language and positive, identity-based framing can open up the transformation. Use the same order for the rollercoaster structure (humor, vulnerable story, clear CTA) when the audience is not highly distressed.

Research the buyer's emotions first

1 min
  • Collect at least 30 direct quotes from Amazon reviews (written ratings from customers). Direct quotes means their exact words.
    • Research feelings that widen the buyer's attention. Sort each feeling into four sub-types (smaller groups inside one feeling).
    • Find at least 10 sourced statements for each sub-type. Sourced means you can point to where it came from.
    • Hope: immediate, long-term, permission, rekindled (hope coming back after it was lost).
    • Relief: daily, major, social, future.
    • Pride: personal, social, identity, legacy.
    • Belonging: community, family, identity, acceptance.
    • Love or connection: self, friendship, family, and romantic where relevant.
  • For every hope statement, judge:
    • how fragile (easy to break) or robust (hard to break) it is
    • what could extinguish it (put it out)
    • what would strengthen it
    • how its strength changes with the person's distress level (how upset or stressed they are)
  • Find the emotions the customer feels about the problem. Use them in product descriptions, ads, and other copy (written sales words). This is the key input for marketing that connects with the customer.
  • A problem does not always cause bad feelings. Example: a new parent who needs a safe, portable place for the baby to sleep may feel excited and hopeful, not frustrated. Pitch your marketing to those feelings.
  • People move away from pain or toward pleasure. Write down what the customer wants to avoid. Then write copy from both the pain angle and the pleasure angle.
  • For expensive products, status is often the major problem driving the purchase. Status means how other people see the buyer. It is a very powerful problem to target.

Choose one feeling and one dominant motive

1 min

  • Before writing any copy, decide the one feeling you want the reader to have when they finish. Emotion drives action. A list of benefits without the right feeling rarely brings orders.

  • Logic convinces the reader but does not make them act. Give logical reasons as the justification. Base the real pull on a primary emotion such as pride, love, fear, or gain.

  • There are six primary buying motives (the deep reasons people buy):

    • love
    • gain
    • duty
    • pride
    • self-indulgence (treating yourself)
    • self-preservation (protecting yourself)

    Find which one the reader feels most strongly before you choose your appeal.

  • Strengthen the dominant motive with supporting motives. Example: a pride-driven purchase gets stronger when reinforced with self-indulgence, duty to family, and a measurable gain.

  • Show how repair bills and higher fuel and oil use on an old product wear away the price difference of a new one.

  • Separate the motive that creates the desire from the motive that triggers the action. Example: a reader may want to keep their money, but act to protect their credit standing (their trustworthiness with lenders). So the action needs its own motive.

  • Gain is an easy motive for readers to accept.

  • Never disguise self-interest as concern for others. Readers resent it.

  • Use a love-based appeal only when the product truly protects someone the reader cares about.

  • Readers act for two reasons. They want something strongly enough to reach for the order, or they fear losing something worth having. The close (the final call to action at the end of the sales text) should trigger one or both.

  • Quantify the waste the buyer already accepts. Example: using six ounces of oil where one is enough. A specific multiple is more persuasive than a general claim that things could be better.

Turn the surface problem into the core emotion

1 min

  • Bad feelings such as fear, shame, or frustration can make a prospect (a possible buyer) feel alone. Acknowledging the problem builds trust. Trust makes the seller credible enough to offer the solution.

  • Problem-solution pitches (sales texts that name a problem, then offer the fix) work best when they hit the core emotion. The core emotion is the deepest feeling underneath the surface problem.

  • Relief from guilt, shame, self-doubt, embarrassment, loneliness, or fear of future regret often sells better than relief from the visible problem alone.

  • The most powerful benefits touch the most universal wants and fears, because everyone shares them:

    • relief from fears, frustrations, and feelings of not being good enough
    • wanting to feel loved, special, and important
    • wanting leisure and pleasure

  • These map to the seven deadly sins (an old list of human weaknesses: lust, gluttony, greed, sloth, wrath, envy, pride) as benefit themes.

  • The strongest promise often lies in how the product makes the buyer feel about themselves and how others will see them. It is not only about what the product does.

  • Emotional promises such as relief, status, confidence, safety, inclusion, and admiration from peers sell more than functional features (what the product physically does).

Build the promise from the four core emotions

4 min

Alter ego = the partner feeling that goes with each core emotion. Signal words = the words that point to that feeling.

Core emotion Alter ego Signal words Ids
New Only New: Breakthrough, Revolutionary, Discovery, Game Changing, Disruptive, NEW, Announcing, Revealed, Breaking. Only: Exclusive, Patented, Only here, Proprietary ,,,
Easy Anybody Easy, Anybody, Simple, 1-2-3, Push-button, Automatic, Fool-proof, Step-by-step, System, All you have to do is, Simply follow the steps, You don't need ,,
Safe Predictable Certainty, risk removal, track record, testimonials, case studies, and repeating patterns from history ,,,,
Big Fast Big, Monster, Huge, Once in a lifetime; Fast, Quickly, Overnight, Weekly ,,
  • Every line of persuasive copy should carry at least one of the four core emotions: New, Easy, Safe, and Big. Each one pairs with its alter ego: New/Only, Easy/Anybody, Safe/Predictable, Big/Fast.
  • Not every line needs all four. The copy as a whole should weave in as many as possible. Lean on the two or three that fit the offer best.
  • New is the most important emotion. Prospects are skeptical (doubtful) and have short attention spans.
  • If a reader sees the message as something they have already seen, or similar to it, the copy loses them.
  • New also gives hope that a failed solution might finally work. It must feel genuinely new, not merely a better version of something that exists.
  • Words that signal New: Breakthrough, Revolutionary, Discovery, Game Changing, Disruptive, NEW, Announcing, Revealed, and Breaking.
  • Use them only if the message is truly new.
  • The power comes from the subtext (the feeling a reader gets between the lines), not from the word itself.
  • Only signals exclusivity. The offer is available only here, through this seller, or was never sold before.
  • Words that signal Only: Exclusive, Patented, Only here, and Proprietary (owned by one company).
  • Phrases such as the only document in the world that contains... do the same job.
  • Easy (partner: Anybody) tells the reader the solution requires little work. It is step-by-step or push-button, and simple enough for anyone.
  • Easy also works for people who feel insecure about their abilities.
  • Words that signal Easy: Easy, Anybody, Simple, 1-2-3, Push-button, Automatic, Fool-proof, Step-by-step, System, All you have to do is, Simply follow the steps, and You don't need.
  • Readers want Easy solutions for two reasons. Laziness: they want less work. Insecurity: they fear past failures reflect their own inadequacy.
  • Making a solution feel possible for anybody, regardless of intelligence, money, or experience, addresses both reasons.
  • "Not-statements" (saying what a thing is not) do two jobs. They build intrigue. Example: It's not muscle confusion, it's not power lifting, and it's not cardio.
  • They also show Easy/Anybody by saying what the reader does not need. Example: You don't have to be a financial genius, you don't need coding experience, you don't need much money.
  • Pair not-statements with a concrete, low-effort starting point.
  • Safe reduces skepticism and raises perceived value at the same time.
  • People who see something as less risky judge its benefits as more valuable. This holds even when a riskier option offers a higher expected return (the average payout if you could repeat the choice many times).
  • So Safe is double-acting in persuasion.
  • People are risk averse (they avoid risk) and place high value on certainty.
  • In a classic study, most participants chose a guaranteed $80 over a gamble with an 80% chance at $100 and a 20% chance at $10.
  • The gamble's expected value was $82. The guarantee still won.
  • Copy should therefore emphasize certainty and eliminating risk.
  • Predictable (Safe's partner) can be conveyed by a repeating pattern from history, a track record (proof of past results), testimonials (customer praise), or case studies (detailed stories of one customer's results).
  • Let the reader infer that the outcome will follow a known sequence. Let them draw the conclusion themselves rather than stating it outright.
  • A track record can imply Safe and Predictable without guaranteeing results.
  • Present past performance as a pattern the reader can see, not as a promise.
  • Big refers to the size of the opportunity. It says the thing is extremely important, groundbreaking, or rewarding.
  • Big demands attention and implies change, which is why readers keep reading.
  • Big pairs well with FOMO (fear of missing out). Its partner Fast means results come quickly, not over years.
  • Words that signal Big: Big, Monster, Huge, and Once in a lifetime.
  • Words that signal Fast: Fast, Quickly, Overnight, and Weekly.
  • Combining Big and Fast (a large result in seconds, days, or weeks rather than years) multiplies the effect.
  • Emotions can work together. A New discovery is usually Big.
  • You can tie it to Predictable by pointing to a past event. Example: The last time something like this happened, it was extremely disruptive.
  • Easy can be woven into Big by stating that a huge result is simple to achieve.
  • Build the big promise in the lead (the first lines of the text) from these pairs: New or Only, Easy or Anybody, Safe or Predictable, Big or Fast.
  • Choose the pairs to suit the product type.
  • A system or step-by-step product leans on Easy/Anybody and Fast.
  • A prediction or trend leans on New and Big to create FOMO.
  • Copywriting works through subtext: the emotions a reader infers rather than the literal words.
  • Judge copy by what it communicates, not by how clever or conversational its wording is.
  • Plain conversational text without emotional subtext still reads as ordinary English, not persuasive copy.
  • Copy persuades by emotion, not logic. A reader can be drawn to an opportunity even while knowing it is made up.
  • The four core emotions and the four sections are the framework that lets copy bypass a reader's rational checks.

Lead with the outcome and the feeling

1 min
  • Buyers move toward pleasure and away from pain. So the main headline (the biggest line at the top of the page) should state the big benefit or desired outcome.
  • Surface that benefit before anything else.
  • Sell emotional value and the psychological outcome (how the buyer will feel), not only the product.
  • Use the buyer's emotions as the core of the message.
  • Novelty switches on attention. People's brains react strongly to things that are completely new and ignore what is familiar.
  • Show the uniqueness of the new opportunity in the headline and at the start of the letter. Frame it as a breakthrough.
  • Novelty can take many forms: a new cure, a new way to lose weight, a new way to make money, or a new business model.
  • In financial copy it can be a new law, loophole, threat, prediction, discovery, scandal, or trading system. It works as long as the reader has not seen it before.
  • Sell the feeling and the desire first.
  • Then give the technical facts and reasons (the details that prove it works). They let the buyer explain the decision to themselves and others.
  • People buy on emotion and then justify the purchase with logic.
  • Do not mention the product too early in the lead.
  • Resistance to persuasion comes in four forms. Each is handled by a different section of the copy:
    • Reactance: the feeling that your freedom is being limited, so pushy selling backfires.
    • Distrust: handled by proof and the credibility section.
    • Scrutiny: readers want logic behind their emotional decision, handled by the mechanism explanation (how the product works).
    • Inertia: staying put is easier than acting, handled by the urgency section.
  • Even a hint of selling can make a reader leave.

Persuade without triggering resistance

1 min
  • Telling the prospect that their failure is their own fault makes people reject the copy. Examples: "you are bad with money" or "you lack discipline."
  • People do not want to take responsibility for their failures. Point the blame at the old solutions instead.
  • Choose the common enemy from beliefs the audience already holds. Do not invent one from nothing.
  • Example of a common enemy: an institution or group they already distrust or resent.
  • Blaming that enemy releases a sense of belonging. It makes the prospect feel part of a group aligned against a shared problem.
  • Facts and statistics alone push readers into an analytical mindset.
  • That mindset lowers emotional response and reduces the chance they will act.
  • The same proof inside a story produces a stronger reaction than facts alone or a mix of facts and story.
  • Make statements that are honest, believable, and already held true by the reader.
  • Ask every reader to nod in agreement early.
  • One statement the reader disputes causes them to stop reading.
  • Check each claim for truth and relevance.
  • Open paragraphs or subheads (small headings inside the text) with questions and statements the reader can easily agree with. Do this before the offer appears.
  • This builds a habit of agreement that makes the offer easier to accept.
  • Use fear, threats, or crime statistics with caution. Most buyers are repelled by them.
  • Let the buyer recognize the need first. Then show the points they care about most, such as reliability and ease of installation.

Use formulas to turn features into outcomes

2 min
  • Formula: state the behavioral benefit (what the buyer does differently). Then ask what the buyer ultimately gets from it, and lead with that deeper outcome.
  • Deeper outcomes such as financial freedom, security, or being able to care for family are more persuasive than the metric itself.
    • Example A: instead of add 20 leads a month, lead with stop worrying about where next month's clients come from.
    • Example B: instead of get 30 minutes back each day, lead with finally have evenings for the people you care about.
    • Both work because they connect the feature to the reason the buyer wants it.
  • Saying a business will grow to 10k a month is a behavioral claim.
  • Explaining that this means never worrying about money again, affording what you want, and looking after your family taps into the real reason the buyer wants it.
  • The second version is the more potent message.
  • Motive-driven ad formula: replace a measurable outcome with the freedom, security, or control it brings.
  • Buyers pay for what the outcome means to their life, not the number itself.
    • Instead of Hit 20k a month, write Stop checking your bank app with dread.
    • Instead of Lose 5 kg in six weeks, write Walk into your reunion feeling like yourself again.
  • Status-driven ad formula: show that the product lets the buyer look successful, stay current with trends, or avoid being left behind by peers.
  • People want to appear in control and up to date among the people they compare themselves to.
    • Be the person whose watch everyone in the meeting notices, without a word.
    • Stay ahead of the look your friends are already wearing this season.
  • Translate an unfamiliar effort into a reference the buyer already understands. Then they grasp the value without learning new facts.
  • A time-based comparison works this way. Example: 30 minutes of exercise equals a two mile walk.
  • Pattern: [amount of effort in familiar time] equals [familiar everyday activity]. It anchors an unknown effort to a reference the shopper already understands.
    • Ten minutes of this routine equals a 30 minute gym session, if your results back the claim.
    • Five minutes with this tool equals an hour of hand sanding.

Use social proof, authority, and urgency

1 min
  • Stating a credible role, such as a nurse, gives the speaker authority (trust based on expertise).
  • Viewers tend to follow advice from people in that role.
  • Mention the role naturally in the testimonial, not as a sales claim.
  • Social proof (showing that others already chose the product) works through three signals: five-star reviews, a product that sold out, and a long waiting time for delivery.
  • Each signal shows demand. The viewer assumes others already trust the product.
  • Social proof builds trust.
  • It can take the form of testimonials, case studies, or customer reviews.
  • It is useful in an ad, though not every creative needs it.
  • Scarcity (limited supply) and urgency (time pressure) work through FOMO.
  • People fear losing an opportunity and value what is scarce.
  • Emphasize limited availability or a time-limited offer to push action.
  • Jay Abraham's principle: if you can define a customer's problem better than the customer can, they will assume you have the solution.
  • Content that explains the problem clearly builds authority and trust.

Evidence from historical cases

2 min
  • Early in advertising history, an agency tested reason-why ads (ads that give a clear reason to buy) against the usual news-style product announcements.
  • The reason-why ads promised a clear emotional payoff.
  • Within four months, the client raised its budget with the agency from $15,000 per year to $30,000 per month.
  • Lesson for copy: find the real reason customers buy, and make the emotional core of the promise the opening.
  • In a 2004 Carnegie Mellon study (a university study) on charity donations, participants shown the story of one child in poverty gave an average of $2.38.
  • Those shown only poverty statistics gave an average of $1.14.
  • A group shown both the story and the statistics gave an average of $1.43, less than the story alone.
  • Adding facts to a story can lower emotional response and reduce the amount people give.
  • Ads that appeal to emotion and tell a vivid story outperform ads built on endorsements and factual statements.
  • In one comparison, the emotional version tripled and quadrupled the returns (the sales it brought in).
  • Use authority statements as support, not as the main message.
  • Sensational, emotional content tends to outsell sober (plain, serious) content in print.
  • A tabloid-style approach (dramatic, gossipy newspaper style) was cited as selling about ten times as many copies as a clean, well-edited paper.
  • Use this to justify a more emotional headline. But test it against your own audience.
  • Starting point: a writer had a self-development book. It sold poorly under letters and ads describing its contents.
  • The book's original publisher failed.
  • A former bookkeeper for that publisher bought the plates (metal printing plates) and remaining stock for a low price.
  • He believed the book would sell if the appeal went to the reader's ambition, not a description of contents.
  • With only 200 dollars left, he spent all of it on a mailing that appealed to ambition.
  • It brought 2,000 dollars of direct orders by mail.
  • He reinvested every dollar of that money in more mailings.
  • Over the following fifteen years, the book sold more than 2,000,000 dollars.
  • Starting point: a publisher's etiquette book (a book on manners) lay in its stock room for ten to fifteen years without demand.
  • The publisher's letters and advertisements then appealed to the fear that a reader might commit an unconscious social blunder and cause themselves embarrassment.
  • The book sold about a million copies.
  • The demand came from that fear, not from any change in public taste.

Test before committing budget

1 min
  • In a documented side-by-side test (two versions shown next to each other), a positive ad showing the desired result out-pulled its negative version. The positive one won about four to one.
  • Test positive and negative framings (two ways of wording the same idea) against each other before committing budget.
  • Keep a personal spreadsheet (a table in a computer file) of psychological copy tactics.
  • Examples of tactics: loss aversion (people feel losses more strongly than gains), future pacing (helping the reader picture life after the purchase), and social proof.
  • Apply these on top of problem, solution, and outcome copy.
  • Use the spreadsheet as a reference bank.
