Benefits, bullets and unique mechanism

Section 12.10 · 10 of 26 in this chapter · 47 min read

In short

  • Sell the result the buyer wants first. Then show the product as the answer.
  • Every feature needs a plain meaning: what changes for the buyer.
  • Write many bullet points, then keep only the strongest few.
  • Use exact numbers and proof. Skip exclamation marks.
  • Never make the reader guess what a feature means.
More key points
  • Mix short and long bullets, and mix bullets that tease with bullets that reveal. This keeps readers reading.
  • Put your strongest claims at the top and the bottom of the copy. Readers look there first.
  • Explain how the product works before you list its features. Buyers trust a promise more when they see the reason behind it.
  • Turn each feature into a result the buyer gets. Back every claim with exact numbers, and check them before you print them.
  • Your product needs one clear reason it works where other things failed. Name that one special ingredient, method or technology. Never name a competitor.
  • Tie that reason to a belief the reader already has. Then back it with proof from outside sources.
  • Every promise needs proof at least as strong as the promise. Aim for three proofs.
  • End the benefits part with three short matching words, like "Fast, quiet, and built to last."

Start from the Outcome the Customer Wants

3 min
  • Find the result the customer wants, not only the features (what the product has) or technical benefits (how the product works).
  • Example: for a gaming keyboard, customers do not mainly want a wireless phone holder.
  • They want to be sharper, beat competitors, keep a higher kill-to-death ratio (kills compared with deaths in a game), and outdo opponents.
  • Build the ad's message around these results first. Then show the product as the solution.
  • Results-first ads create desire and more conversions (sales made). Feature-first ads do not.
  • Lead with the main benefits of the product. Benefits are what the customer can do with it. Do not list features first.
  • Customers buy what a feature does for them, not the feature itself.
  • A bare feature (a feature with no explanation), such as a built-in charging station, means little until the buyer sees the result it gives them.
  • Pair every relevant feature with a matching desire. Say what it means for the customer in plain terms, such as saves time and effort or lets you stay relaxed.
  • Focus on the few features that truly set the product apart from competitors. Do not list everything.
  • Each feature in a "what it does" list needs a matching meaning line.
  • The meaning line shows what changes in the customer's home, time, or comfort.
  • A feature alone is proof. The meaning line gives the reason to buy.
  • Keep feature claims short and easy to see.
  • Always state the effort saved, such as minimal effort or quick cleaning. Customers respond to less work as much as to results.
  • Shoppers compare products by features, especially online with several tabs open. So list the real features.
  • Missing a real feature makes buyers assume the product lacks it. That makes competitors look better.
  • Never leave the meaning of a feature for the reader to work out. Spell out what it means for the buyer, in a way they can picture.
  • Keep a technical term unchanged when it is the real name of the technology.
  • Then always follow it with its effect. A technical name alone sounds impressive but means nothing to an uninformed buyer.
  • Quantified technical claims (claims with numbers) must show the result to the buyer.
  • Example, described: a carbon-ceramic (a heating material made of carbon and ceramic) low-EMF (low electromagnetic field, meaning fewer invisible energy waves) heater.
  • It gives infrared heat (heat you feel as warmth from rays) that is 5x more effective than standard infrared sources.
  • Best written as: uses low-EMF carbon-ceramic heating that gives 5x more effective infrared heat than standard sources, so you spend less time in the sauna and more time on your day.
  • The star footnote (the small * note that explains a claim) on a comparison such as 5x more effective than standard* must stay with the claim.
  • A benefit that only repeats a number, such as five times more effective, is still too abstract.
  • Add a power benefit (a benefit that changes your time, money, or daily life) that turns the number into something real. Buyers often feel they lack time.
  • Non-unique features (features competitors also have) still belong in the list.
  • Place unique features and the unique mechanism (the special method behind the product) first.
  • Then include standard features that have real value. Buyers compare across products.

Feature-to-Benefit Patterns

4 min
  • Bullet formula: Feature + Benefit + Benefit of That Benefit.
  • Example:
    • KEEP THEM ON YOUR PAGES LONGER! [Feature: longer engagement] Bounce rates indicate engagement quality, boost engagement with these engineered questions to instantly increase conversion rates! [Benefit: more conversions]
    • Terms in this example: bounce rate is the share of visitors who leave a page right away. Engagement is how much visitors read, click, or interact.
  • Interactive feature storytelling. Start with a benefit statement or what the product does.
  • Then describe features as vivid, interactive experiences the user will have.
  • Why it works: it creates sensory imagery (pictures that use the senses) and shows the product in action. This makes it tangible (easy to picture) and desirable.
    • These shoes learn your gait (the way you walk or run). Run, walk, or stand and watch them adjust cushioning (soft padding) automatically with every movement throughout your day.
    • This mug knows your temperature. Pour your coffee and feel it maintain the perfect warmth for hours, cooling just as you need it.
  • Use case lead with feature checklist. Start with the primary benefit and the emotional payoff (the good feeling the buyer gets).
  • Then state relevant use cases (situations where the product is used).
  • Then list features with checkmarks.
  • Why it works: it connects to customer needs right away. Then it proves the point with clear, scannable (easy to skim) proof.
    • Save 10 hours a week on admin work. Our scheduling software handles: ✅ Multi-timezone appointment sync ✅ Automated reminders and confirmations ✅ Integration with your calendar apps ✅ Client self-service booking.
    • Build muscle faster with precision. Our workout tracker includes: ✅ Form analysis with video feedback ✅ Personalized progression algorithms ✅ Recovery recommendations based on performance ✅ Offline workout access on mobile.
  • Benefit-feature pairing with visual indicators. List each benefit with a supporting feature or proof point. Use bullets or checkmarks.
  • Structure: benefit description + supporting detail.
  • Why it works: scannable lists let different customers find their own reason to buy. The pairing ties abstract benefits to concrete features.
    • Instant relief + cooling technology; All-day comfort + invisible design
    • Reduces pain in 7 days + clinically tested; Works with your body + no medications required
  • Effort-equivalence benefit pattern. Turn abstract benefits (vague benefits) into concrete ones.
  • Compare the effort or result of your product with a familiar, measurable activity that the audience already knows.
  • Why it works: customers grasp benefits faster when they can imagine doing something they already know.
    • Only 20 minutes with our app saves the same time as a two-hour coffee meeting.
    • Using our template takes as much time as reading three emails.
  • Dimensionalized benefit pattern: [feature] so that [practical benefit] and you can [emotional outcome].
  • It turns a feature into a practical result. Then it turns that result into a feeling the reader can picture. That feeling is what triggers emotion.
    • Double-walled steel keeps lunch warm until noon so that you can skip the canteen queue (the cafeteria line) and enjoy a relaxed break.
    • Lumbar support (support for the lower back) that adjusts to your posture so that your back stays comfortable after eight hours at the desk and you can leave work feeling fresh.
  • Body copy order under a performance headline. Body copy means the main paragraphs of sales text. A performance headline is the big title that promises a result.
  • Follow this fixed order:
    1. Ask a question that names what sets the product apart.
    2. Name the specific material or technology behind it.
    3. Give concrete equipment or spec details (specs are technical facts such as size, power, and weight) in plain language.
    4. Add one practical convenience or comfort feature.
    5. List the control options (how the buyer can adjust or run it).
  • Every claim in the body copy must be backed by evidence (proof, such as tests or data).
  • Health results are left out when the product is in a health or finance space.
  • Two new examples in the same pattern. All figures are placeholders (temporary example numbers) that need real evidence.
    • What sets the Ridgeline 20V Drill apart from cheaper cordless drills? Its brushless motor and two-speed gearbox give steady torque on long runs. The 21-piece bit set and magnetic tray keep every job organized, and a battery indicator on the handle shows when to swap packs.
    • Terms in this example: 20V means 20 volts of battery power. A brushless motor is a motor design without brushes. A gearbox has parts that set speed and power. Torque is turning force. A bit is a drill tip. A battery indicator is a light that shows how much charge is left.
    • What makes the Lumen Desk Lamp different from other desk lights? Its adjustable 3,000 K to 6,500 K color range lets you match daylight at any hour. A touch dimmer and a USB-C port add convenience without extra setup.
    • Terms in this example: K (kelvin) is a measure of light colour, from warm to cool. A touch dimmer is a control you tap to make the light brighter or darker. A USB-C port is a standard charging socket.

Power Bullets

1 min
  • Rule: State the feature first. Then follow it with the benefit.
  • Then take the benefit further. Describe a concrete picture of how it will feel in real life.
  • Power bullet pattern (a short, strong bullet). Name the feature. Then state the practical benefit it gives the buyer.
  • Then add a short emotional clause. It answers a likely fear or doubt, such as reassurance for a buyer with no prior experience. It creates relief, security, or comfort.
    • Installs in about one hour, so you can enjoy it as soon as possible, even if you have never installed one before.
    • Heats up in 10 minutes, so you can start your session without waiting around while you get ready for your day.
  • Steps to write a power bullet for a product feature.
    1. Write the feature exactly as the product has it. Keep the technical name if it is the real name of the technology.
    2. State the practical benefit this feature gives the buyer.
    3. Describe in real terms what the buyer can do or feel because of that benefit, so they can picture it.
    4. If the buyer may doubt or fear something about the feature, add a short clause. It removes that doubt and gives relief, security, or comfort.
    5. Read the bullet. Check that the buyer does not have to work out its meaning alone.
  • Example: reservations mode. A reservations mode (a setting that switches the product on at a time you pick) lets the buyer schedule it up to 36 hours ahead.
  • Written as a power bullet: it is ready at the time you choose, so you can step in after a long day without waiting for it to heat up, even if you plan the session a day and a half in advance.
  • Keep the 36-hour figure exact. A precise number makes the feature believable. It also lets the buyer picture the schedule.

Structuring the Feature List

1 min
  • Structure the feature list as bullets. Each bullet pairs a feature with a benefit and an emotional image (a picture that creates a feeling).
  • Do not use bare feature statements.
  • Put the main promise, the story, and the unique mechanism before the feature bullets.
  • A standard feature list that only names features gives the buyer nothing to picture. Examples of such features: tablet controls (control through a tablet screen), reservation modes, warranties (promises to repair or replace), and installation time.
  • It also misses the chance to explain value. Avoid publishing bare feature lists on product pages and in ads.
  • A bulleted list is the preferred layout for features and benefits. It separates them from body text and is easy to scan (skim quickly).
  • Paragraphs with subheadings (small titles above text blocks) are an acceptable option if they suit the page.
  • Bullets for technical products can be longer than usual.
  • Break long bullets into shorter paragraphs inside the bullet so they stay readable. Length itself is not a problem.
  • Extra features in a product copy's bullet list do three jobs at once:
    • Compare: they let the buyer compare the product with others on the market.
    • Reinforce: they support the power benefits.
    • Argue: they give the logical argument for the purchase.
  • Choose each extra feature for at least one of these jobs.

Role of Bullets and Fascinations

2 min
  • Fascinations are short, eye-catching points that spark curiosity. Bullets and fascinations are the main points in a sales message.
  • The core job of bullets and fascinations is to keep the reader going through the whole message.
  • If bullets bore the reader, they start to skim (glance quickly) or stop reading. That directly causes lost sales.
  • Weak links (weak points) in bullet sections are often the deadliest weakness in copy. They are easy to spot and easy to escape from.
  • Bullets and fascinations do several jobs at once. They:
    • stimulate curiosity and intrigue,
    • seduce (pull in) the prospect (the potential buyer) to keep reading,
    • build desire through revealed secrets,
    • seize and hold attention,
    • improve readability,
    • add variety to prevent monotony (boredom from sameness),
    • crush the headline block by generating dozens of headline possibilities (the headline block is the main title area),
    • create multiple selling angles on one piece of information.
  • Dull, repetitive bullets or fascinations that lack intrigue are a critical weak point.
  • They make the prospect skim and scan instead of reading fully. That is one step from the prospect putting the letter down or throwing it away.
  • Weed out weak links in bullet sections, one by one.
  • They cause loss of readership (how many people keep reading), and then loss of sales.
  • Roughly 80% of promotions (sales campaigns) for certain products and markets are built almost entirely on strong bullets and fascinations, not long body copy.
  • This is especially true for book promotions, large mail packages (sets of sales letters sent by post), and certain online promotions.
  • One direct mail control (a sales letter sent by post that is the best-performing version, used as the test benchmark) featuring roughly 80% bullets and fascinations was mailed profitably for over 5 years.
  • It beat earlier controls. This shows strong long-term performance of bullet-heavy copy.
  • When you have a series of benefits, steps in a procedure, or other copy points, group them using bullets, numbers, letters, or other formatting.
  • This adds clarity and impact to the message structure. Clayton often uses this approach to organize multiple related benefits or steps in a process.

Research and Volume

1 min
  • Gene Schwartz research approach for bullet writing.
    1. Read the source material multiple times, highlighting key points each time.
    2. Compile the notes into a comprehensive (complete) document. 60-70 pages of distilled (boiled down) information is typical.
    3. Organize the research information by theme.
    4. Begin filling in the copy structure. Create bullets and fascinations as they naturally come out of understanding the material.
  • Parris Lampropoulos selection approach.
    1. Write approximately 700 fascinations covering every shocking, fascinating, and emotionally stirring piece of information in the research.
    2. Select only the strongest approximately 100 for the final package. This is a 7:1 ratio, meaning 7 written for every 1 kept.
    3. This selection guarantees exceptional variety and quality in the bullet sections. It stops the reader from skimming because of mediocre (average) bullets.
    4. Arrange the selected fascinations in the most important positions: front cover, cover deck (the smaller line under the main headline), and back cover teasers (short hooks on the back cover).
  • Example of the 7:1 ratio. Parris Lampropoulos writes approximately 700 fascinations during research and writing. Then he selects approximately 100 for the final package.
  • This ensures only exceptional material makes it into the final copy. It creates substantial variety in the bullet sections, making them more intriguing and enjoyable to read throughout the promotion.
  • Write many more bullets than you will use. The elite strategy is to write 7x more bullets than will appear in the final draft.
  • For example, write 700 fascinations and select down to 100 for the promotion. This ensures only exceptional bullets make it into the final copy.
  • Choose from the best of the best. Do not mix exceptional and mediocre bullets.
  • Archive what works. Create an archive (a saved collection) of every bullet or fascination that performs well or gets a strong response.
  • When similar bullets are needed for different products, audiences, or platforms, check the archive first.
  • High-performing bullets often work across uses when the core benefit still matters to the audience.

Quality Checks for Each Bullet

2 min
  • The Makepeace Makeover: three-step bullet improvement (a method to make a bullet stronger).
    1. Add specificity: replace vague claims with exact numbers, mechanisms (the exact method behind the result), or credibility signals (proof you can be trusted).
    2. Add intrigue and curiosity: choose one of the 21 bullet formulas that creates tension, questions, or hidden information.
    3. Add benefit clarity: make sure the reader immediately understands why this information matters to his life or business.
  • Attack each piece of information from 2-3 different angles using different formulas. Then select the strongest version.
  • Specificity separates believable bullets from generic claims. Numbers, percentages, and specific mechanisms signal research and analysis.
  • Example: How to profit from stocks is flat and unconvincing.
  • 307% profit from gold stocks using Source B Buffett's Bull Market X-Factor includes a mechanism (the named strategy), specificity (307%, not up to 500%), and credibility (a referenced expert).
  • Name the mechanism in bullets. Reveal the precise mechanism: the exact way the prospect gets the benefit.
  • A mechanism can be a proprietary system (a system you own), a specific nutrient, a technology, a supplier relationship, or a process.
  • It is very powerful in saturated markets (markets full of similar products). It sets the claim apart by showing a unique means or system.
  • It brings benefit, credibility, and intrigue at once.
  • Emotional triggers allow less proof. An emotional trigger is a feeling that pushes action.
  • When it matches what the prospect desperately wants (sexual desire, financial security, fear of missing out), a bullet can work with significantly less proof and specificity than normal. Desire overrides skepticism (doubt).
  • Balance this with enough specificity in the other bullets of the same section.
  • Find the emotional goldmines. Identify the prospect's core emotional desires or fears, such as financial security, sexual desire, or fear of missing out.
  • Build bullets that target those emotional centers for maximum resonance (a strong emotional response).
  • Gut-level filter. Use gut-level, greed-tickling impact (a strong, instant feeling of wanting) as the filter when selecting bullets for the final copy.
  • A bullet that creates an immediate, visceral (felt instantly in the body) sense of desirability has significantly higher conversion power.
  • Use colloquial language (everyday, casual words) and strong verbs (action words).
  • They communicate more meaning in fewer words than formal language. That makes bullets more impactful and memorable.
  • No exclamation marks. Exclamation marks signal hype (too much noise) and lower response rates in bullet copy.
  • Use other punctuation, such as colons (:), which boost response by 50% or more.

Fascination Formulas

1 min

Each formula below appears with its pattern and examples. Use the formulas to write many variants of the same information, then select the strongest.

Curiosity and hidden knowledge

4 min
  • "How to" bullet. It works because prospects are naturally curious about step-by-step information, especially when it ties to their deepest desires or pain points (problems).
  • Open with the benefit-related action, not just a generic process.
    • How to source inventory without tying up working capital for 90 days, using the payment-term negotiation script that works with 8 out of 10 tier-one suppliers.
    • How to prevent competitor price undercutting by positioning your brand so customers ignore price-focused competitors entirely.
    • Terms in these examples: inventory is the products you keep in stock. Working capital is the cash you have to run the business. A payment term is how long you have to pay a supplier (the company that sells you products). Tier-one suppliers are the top-level suppliers. Undercutting means selling for less than rivals.
  • "Secret to" bullet. It reveals non-obvious information, positioned as a closely guarded secret.
  • It works because prospects believe they have found something most people do not know.
  • It must be based on genuinely uncommon information. Vary it carefully to avoid overuse.
    • The secret to 40% repeat orders from first-time buyers: it has nothing to do with product quality.
    • The little-known secret that separates $50K-per-month shops from $500K shops: it is one email sequence that reverses abandonment decisions.
    • Terms in these examples: repeat orders are orders from customers who buy again. An email sequence is a set of automatic emails sent in order. Abandonment decisions are choices to leave before buying.
  • "Why" bullet. It builds intrigue by promising to reveal the reason or cause behind something.
  • It implies that understanding why will change the prospect's life. Both the reason and the benefit of knowing it must be directly valuable to the prospect.
    • Why most dropshippers hit a $100K ceiling and never break through (and why understanding this single obstacle decides if you are stuck or scaling).
    • Why customers who receive tracking updates spend 35% more on their next order, and what you are missing if you do not tell them.
    • Terms in these examples: dropshippers are store owners who sell products that suppliers ship straight to customers. A ceiling is a limit you cannot get past. Tracking updates are messages about where a parcel is.
  • "What" bullet. It works in two ways.
  • Either it gives specific action steps to avoid a problem. Or it reveals hard-to-find valuable information that is not commonly shared.
  • Both increase perceived value while teasing the answer.
    • What you must include in supplier contracts that protects you from the 12 most common vendor betrayals.
    • What your fulfillment partner doesn't want you to know about how they handle damage claims, and how knowing this saves you $3K-8K monthly.
    • Terms in these examples: a vendor is a supplier. A fulfillment partner is the company that packs and ships your orders. A damage claim is a request for money when goods arrive broken.
  • "Gimmick" bullet. It gives a creative name to a specific idea from research.
  • It combines a benefit with specificity and memorable framing. It makes a technique feel like a discovered thing with a name, which increases perceived sophistication (a feeling of being advanced).
    • The little-known supplier health score that predicts six months in advance which vendors will fail you or degrade quality.
    • The competitive pressure point: the exact moment when your competitor will need to raise prices due to supplier costs, giving you two weeks of market advantage.
    • Terms in these examples: a health score is a rating of a supplier's reliability. Degrade means get worse.
  • "Sneaky" bullet. It reveals something hidden, guarded, or deliberately obscured by a group.
  • The information is something competitors or suppliers do not want revealed.
  • Use it selectively so it does not seem contrived (fake). It suggests a conspiracy element (a hidden plot).
    • The sneaky way suppliers hide quality degradation in cost optimizations that your factory inspector never catches, until customers start returning product.
    • Sneaky fulfillment tricks most 3PLs use that make their performance metrics look better while customer satisfaction actually drops.
    • Terms in these examples: a 3PL is a third-party logistics company, an outside firm that stores and ships your orders. Cost optimizations are cuts to spending. Performance metrics are measured results.
  • "Single" bullet. It works when you have one benefit that is greater than all others, and you have proof to back it.
  • It positions one exclusive or most powerful possibility.
    • The single highest-impact metric for predicting which customers will buy again: customer acquisition cost (what you spend to win one customer) in week 2 of purchase, which predicts repeat rate (the share of customers who return) with 87% accuracy.
    • The single most important factor in supplier selection that determines 80% of your margin variance (how much your profit changes): it is not price but response speed on urgent issues.
  • "Truth" bullet. It clears up a controversial, confusing, or commonly misunderstood issue by offering a clear answer.
  • Prospects enjoy reading about contradictions (things that seem to conflict) being cleared up.
    • The truth about influencer partnerships: micro-influencers (5K-50K followers) with 8-12% engagement deliver 3x the ROAS of mega-influencers claiming higher reach.
    • Terms in this example: micro-influencers are creators with a few thousand to tens of thousands of followers. Engagement is likes, comments, and shares. ROAS means return on ad spend, the sales you earn for each dollar spent on ads. Mega-influencers are creators with very large followings.
    • THE TRUTH ABOUT DROPSHIPPING PROFIT: most calculations hide fulfillment complexity. Here is how to measure real margin after every cost is included.

Fear and danger

1 min
  • "What NEVER" bullet. It warns about a mistake or danger. Then it promises information that protects the reader from making it.
  • It builds curiosity about the hidden mistake and triggers fear-based motivation (action driven by fear).
    • What NEVER to do with your best-performing product when your supplier suggests a minor update: one founder lost 60% of revenue by ignoring this warning.
    • What NOT to say to customers when shipping is delayed: the wrong approach loses 40% of repeat customers, while the right one loses only 8%.
  • "WARNING" bullet. It warns about a danger or threat, which triggers fear.
  • Pair the threat with proof that you have the solution.
  • Variations such as CAUTION, ALERT, or DANGER have the same effect.
    • WARNING: your supplier's quality standard changed last month, and your customers are seeing it in unboxing videos even though you have not noticed it yet.
    • ALERT: the shipping carrier you selected is about to raise rates 18% in Q2. Here is the exact timing and how to lock in today's pricing before the deadline.
    • Terms in these examples: unboxing videos show people opening their deliveries. A carrier is a shipping company. Q2 is the second quarter of the year, April to June.

Questions and challenged assumptions

2 min
  • "Right...WRONG!" bullet. It plays on the prospect's belief about something they think they understand.
  • Then it quickly shows the correct information.
  • It works because everyone wants to know they have been wrong about something, especially when a strong benefit comes with it.
    • Most dropshippers think faster shipping guarantees loyalty? Wrong. Our data shows speed barely registers. Here is what actually determines repeat buys.
    • You think supplier payment terms don't matter for 30-day inventory cycles? Wrong. Here is how to turn terms into your biggest competitive advantage.
    • Terms in these examples: loyalty means customers staying with you. Repeat buys are purchases from customers who buy again. An inventory cycle is the time from ordering stock to selling it.
  • "Are you...?" bullet. It asks the prospect about something you strongly believe he is already doing or experiencing.
  • He answers yes in his head. This builds momentum (forward energy) and agreement before you deliver the benefit.
  • It creates credibility because the prospect nods.
  • Variations: Do you...?, Does your...?, Is your...?
    • Are you pricing like a commodity seller instead of a brand, and leaving 40% margin on every sale?
    • Are you using the same supplier for products in completely different categories when one small change could cut costs 25%?
    • Terms in these examples: a commodity seller sells a plain product with no brand, competing on price. Margin is the profit left after costs.
  • "Specific Question" bullet. It opens with an intriguing question that matters to the prospect.
  • Then it offers the solution, either in the bullet itself or in the product being sold.
  • It must include credibility, benefit, intrigue, and curiosity.
    • Do you know that only 8% of dropshippers ever negotiate payment terms with suppliers, even though this decision predicts success more than product selection?
    • Have you discovered that the #1 reason for chargebacks isn't fraud but customer misalignment about product details, and this one email prevents 70% of chargebacks?
    • Terms in these examples: a chargeback is when a customer asks their bank to reverse a payment. The store loses that money.
  • "If...Then" bullet. It engages the prospect by inviting him to meet a simple requirement. Or it asks whether he is experiencing something specific.
  • He becomes interested in whether he meets it. The second half reveals the benefit that follows if he does.
    • If your supplier uses 30-day lead times, you are missing the first two weeks of every trend cycle, while competitors with 14-day vendors already have 200+ sales.
    • If you are losing customers at the cart stage, the problem isn't shipping cost. It is that you are revealing it too late in the buying journey.
    • Terms in these examples: a lead time is the time from order to delivery. A trend cycle is how long a product stays popular. The cart stage is when a shopper has items in their cart but has not paid.

Direct, comparative, and benefit-first

4 min
  • "Statement of Interest + Benefit" bullet. It opens with an exciting or intriguing fact.
  • Then right away, a strong benefit statement explains why it matters.
  • It often mixes a factual lead with a how-to or why structure.
    • Your competitors are already using this supplier. Here is how to partner directly with them at their lowest-tier pricing.
    • Facebook's algorithm just changed how it prioritizes video content. Here is exactly how to structure your ads to capture the new traffic window.
    • Terms in these examples: an algorithm is the platform's automatic system that decides what people see. Lowest-tier pricing is the cheapest price level the supplier offers. A traffic window is a short time when many visitors arrive.
  • "Direct Benefit" bullet. It makes a strong claim in the opening line. This line usually starts with an action verb (a word like STOP or ELIMINATE).
  • It promises the benefit right away. The second line deepens, proves, or adds dimensions to that benefit.
    • ELIMINATE slow-moving inventory: here is how to validate demand before ordering a single unit.
    • STOP losing customers at the upsell stage: the one change to your offer sequence that increases order value 45%.
    • Terms in these examples: the upsell stage is the moment you offer an extra or bigger product. Validate demand means checking that people really want it. Order value is the money spent in one order.
  • "PLUS" bullet. It adds greed and momentum.
  • It is one of the other formulas, combined with PLUS at the beginning. It promises additional benefits beyond the main claim.
  • It works best at the end of a bullet list, to finish with power and built-up desire.
    • PLUS how to turn returns into repeat purchases that increase customer lifetime value by 120% instead of losing the customer.
    • PLUS my complete supplier database, already vetted for quality, communication, and margin, organized by category and minimum order.
    • Terms in these examples: returns are products sent back. Customer lifetime value is the total money one customer spends over time. Vetted means checked. A minimum order is the smallest amount a supplier accepts.
  • "Number" bullet. It groups several ways of doing something, several secrets, or several reasons why something will happen.
  • It condenses related information into one statement. The reader feels more value when learning multiple solutions at once.
    • The 5 overlooked sources of repeat customer revenue that don't require building email lists.
    • Seven supplier negotiation leverage points that are in plain sight but never used, because most entrepreneurs don't know what to look for.
    • Terms in these examples: leverage points are things that give you bargaining power. Email lists are saved contact lists of people who agreed to get your emails.
  • "When" bullet. It promises a benefit at a very specific time.
  • It implies a guaranteed result if the prospect acts at that moment. The prospect wants to know exactly when he can expect the benefit.
    • When you update product photos on Friday evenings, the platform algorithm serves them with full power by Monday morning, capturing the week's traffic window.
    • When you negotiate supplier terms before inventory arrives, you control cash flow for 60 days. Waiting until after arrival costs you 30% of that control.
    • Terms in these examples: cash flow is money coming in and going out of the business.
  • "Quickest, Easiest" bullet. It reveals the fastest, easiest, or simplest way to get a benefit.
  • It taps into the desire for instant results and minimal effort.
    • The easiest way to identify trending products in your niche (a small, focused market) without paying for expensive research: use your competitor's Shopify code and social proof.
    • The quickest way to recover abandoned carts without hiring email specialists: this single 48-hour sequence reverses 40% of abandonment decisions.
    • Terms in these examples: Shopify is a platform for building online stores. Shopify code is the code behind a store's website. Social proof means signs that others already trust it, such as reviews and sales counts. Abandoned carts are shopping baskets left before checkout. A sequence is a set of messages sent in order.
  • "Better" bullet. It works when something is already seen as good, but you offer something better.
  • It slips under the prospect's doubt. It introduces your benefit while building curiosity and momentum.
  • Variations include leading with BEYOND.
    • BETTER THAN Facebook ads for cold traffic: TikTok organic reach costs zero and reaches your exact demographic six months earlier than you see it on Facebook.
    • BEYOND the standard 30-day return window: offer 60 days and watch repeat purchase rates increase 35% because customers trust you more.
    • Terms in these examples: cold traffic is visitors who have never heard of you. Organic reach is free, unpaid views. A demographic is the group of people you aim at, such as their age or location. A return window is the time a customer has to send an item back.
  • Pure benefit bullets. Use these when there is no hard-to-find information to hide.
  • They work best for simple advantages and for guarantee presentations.
  • They need strong action verbs (words like save, stop, or get), clear language, and emotional weight. They do not need curiosity-driven structure.
  • They must be readable and interesting through word choice and specific detail.

Combining, Sequencing, and Placing Bullets

2 min
  • Combine two or more formulas (ready patterns for writing a bullet) on the same information. Two formulas together have a bigger effect than one alone. For example, combine a PLUS bullet (a named bullet pattern) with a How-to bullet (a bullet that teaches a way to do something) to multiply the impact.
  • Do not overuse Sneaky bullets or Secret-to bullets. These are bullets that hint at a hidden trick.
    • Overuse makes the sales text (copy) feel forced and fake.
    • Overuse also causes too much doubt in the reader.
    • When everything is presented as a secret, each secret loses its uniqueness and intrigue.
  • Vary bullet length, structure, and formula. Variety stops boredom (dull sameness). It also stops flat benefit syndrome, where all bullets sound alike and blur together.
    • Mix long and short bullets.
    • Mix blind bullets (they tease and hide the point) with revealing bullets (they show the benefit at once).
    • Mix different formulas on the same topic.
    • This creates a roller coaster feeling (ups and downs of interest). It keeps the prospect (the possible buyer) excited about each next bullet.
  • Mix blind and revealing bullets. Blind bullets tease information without revealing it. Revealing bullets show the benefit right away. This unpredictability stops monotony. It keeps readers excited about what comes next instead of skimming (reading only the surface).
  • Theme bullets to cover one topic from several angles. Themed bullets are a group of bullets about one subject.
    • Grouping related bullets with different formulas makes the prospect feel he has complete coverage.
    • Varied themed bullets keep him engaged. He does not know which direction the next one will take.
    • Themed bullets at the beginning (front cover or early deck copy, the short text near the top) signal a thorough investigation inside.
  • Position matters: place the most powerful, intriguing claims in the most valuable spots.
    • These spots are the front and back cover, the opening headline area, and the final closing bullets.
    • Opening bullets in promotional body copy (the main sales text) should bribe the prospect into reading on.
    • Closing bullets on the back cover should make him want to open the report to find the answers.
  • Sequence bullets so they feed each other. Sequence means putting things in order. Bullets linked in order support each other instead of standing alone.
    • Each bullet does not need to do as much. The total effect does most of the work.
    • Sequencing lets you introduce a benefit in one bullet and explain why it works in the next.

Unique Mechanism: Purpose and Placement

1 min
  • The unique mechanism is the part of product copy that explains how the product delivers its main promise. A mechanism means how something works. The unique mechanism also explains why the product is different from competing products.
  • It normally comes after the story or thought-command opening (an opening that tells the reader what to think) and before the detailed features and benefits.
  • Buyers decide with their feelings. They need a logical reason to confirm the decision. The unique mechanism is where that logic enters the copy. Without it, the copy only makes promises.
  • Skeptical buyers (people who doubt) have often been over-promised before. They need to see how a product works before they believe a headline promise (the big claim at the top).
  • Explain the mechanism right after the primary promise.
  • Buyers want something different from what they have tried before. The unique mechanism separates the product from alternatives on the market.
  • The unique mechanism is the strongest single benefit to highlight in ad copy (short paid promotional text). Uniqueness encourages clicks because it promises to teach the reader something new.
  • Brands print their unique mechanism prominently on packaging. They use it in offline (shops, print) and online sales alike.
    • Once you know the concept, study competitor packaging and product listings (the product pages of rival sellers).
    • Look for the mechanisms they highlight.
  • The length of the mechanism section depends on the product.
    • Simple products may need only three or four short paragraphs.
    • Technical products need more space.
    • Explain as much as needed so the mechanism is understood.

Unique Mechanism: Writing the Mechanism Sentence

1 min
  • Before writing the mechanism, list the concerns buyers have before purchase (worries they have before buying). For example, they may never have used this type of product.
  • Then connect the mechanism to resolving that concern. This adds a benefit to the logic.
  • Formula for a benefit-linked mechanism sentence (a sentence that links how the product works to a reason to buy): name the mechanism, state a measurable advantage, then state the benefit. A measurable advantage is a difference you can count or check. It is compared with the previous or competing version (a rival product). Then state the benefit that answers a specific buyer doubt.
    • The name creates uniqueness.
    • The number makes the difference believable.
    • The benefit removes the reason to hesitate.
    • Our 3-layer Thermo-Lock lid keeps coffee hot for up to 6 hours, 40% longer than our previous model, so first-time buyers can brew at 6 am without making a second cup.
    • TrailGrip sole technology holds grip on wet stone 2x better than standard rubber soles, so hikers new to trail running stop worrying about slipping.
  • State directly what the reader should conclude. For example, he needs all components of the mechanism. Do not assume the reader will reach the conclusion you want on their own.
  • Explain each technical term. Technical terms are specialist words. Jargon (specialist words used without explanation) confuses buyers and drops them from the copy.
    • Explain each term in short, plain paragraphs.
    • Keep it short for simple products, since they need less explanation.

Unique Mechanism: Technical Mechanism

2 min
  • Formula for a technical mechanism section (a fill-in pattern for explaining complex technology). Each step below is one idea.
    • Open with the trademarked technology name (a brand name legally protected by law). Add a measurable output compared with the closest competitor.
    • Then add a second attribute that goes beyond raw power (plain strength or speed).
    • Then explain each component (each part) in simple terms.
    • Close with a firm statement of what the buyer needs.
    • The number proves superiority (that it is better).
    • The second attribute widens the advantage.
    • The plain explanation makes the technology understandable.
    • Our TerraSeal boot uses a 4-zone membrane technology (a thin layer that controls what passes through) that keeps water out 3x longer than the closest competitor. It is not just about waterproofing; the same membrane lets moisture escape, so your feet stay dry on long days. You need both functions, because a boot that only blocks water traps sweat.
    • The AeroFlow kettle heats water with a 2200 W element, 30% faster than the closest competitor. It is not just about speed; the sensor (a part that detects a condition) shuts it off automatically, so it is safe to leave unattended. You need both the speed and the auto shut-off to get a fast, safe kettle.
  • Case: infrared sauna, first claim.
    • The trademarked True Wave heaters produce 1000 W (watts, a unit of power).
    • The copy states this is 25x more than the closest competitor, so the same benefits come in less time.
    • The claim was supported by a test showing higher output than competitors.
    • A session takes about 15 minutes after the sauna preheats (heats up before use).
    • A reservation mode (a setting that starts the sauna at a chosen time) can preset the sauna to switch on up to 36 hours in advance.
  • Case: infrared sauna, second attribute. The heating is full spectrum (it uses several types of light together). Infrared is invisible heat light. Wavelengths are the sizes of light waves. Full spectrum means it uses near, mid, and far infrared together.
    • Near infrared (one band of invisible heat light) is absorbed in shallow skin tissue and linked to sweating.
    • Mid infrared reaches deeper soft tissue and is linked to oxygen and circulation (blood flow).
    • Far infrared reaches the deepest and is linked to releasing stored substances from muscle and fat.
    • The copy then says you need all three wavelengths, not just one or two as some competing saunas offer.

Unique Mechanism: Verifying Mechanism Claims

1 min
  • Case: wax strip pack. The named gel technology is the first and most visible claim on the front. Gel is a thick, jelly-like liquid. The claims below it state that:
    • The gel flows around and grips the shortest hairs;
    • it works on hairs from 1.5 mm and is easier than the previous formula;
    • it removes hairs from the root (the base under the skin) for up to 28 days of smoothness;
    • The pack holds 20 strips and 2 finishing wipes (cloths used after waxing);
    • a sensitive-skin version contains almond oil and vitamin E.
  • A spoken pitch gave 20 days for the smoothness claim. The pack prints 28 days.
  • Use the printed claim only after verifying it.

Intensification: Principles

1 min
  • Intensification is the process of building desire (a strong want) by showing new images of how the product satisfies it.
  • It works in two directions.
    • Horizontally means reaching more people.
    • Vertically means making the existing desire stronger.
  • Where space is limited, use two techniques. Limited space includes catalogs (printed product lists), small print, radio, and television.
    • Compression: boil images down to a few key words.
    • Campaign repetition: repeat those key words across ads with changing embellishments (added extra details).
    • In long-form media, such as large print ads and direct mail (letters sent to buyers), the length is limited only by how many fresh ways you can present the image without boredom.
  • The reader will remember one main image from an ad.
    • Every new and different way of presenting that image adds detail, sharpens it, and builds emotional weight (feeling strength).
    • Walk around the product and show it at each point where it enters the buyer's life.
  • Do not repeat a description. Reinforce it with a new viewpoint.
    • Each new setting for the same basic promise strengthens the descriptions before it. It makes the reader more eager to take part.
    • If the new viewpoint is not different or dramatic enough to renew interest, leave it out.

Intensification: Techniques

2 min
  • Technique 1: describe the satisfaction in complete detail. Use the result's specific numbers, such as counts, sizes, colours, and time spans (how long something lasts). The reader can then picture the outcome before reading on.
  • Technique 2: put the product in action. Walk the reader step by step through how it does what it does. Include what he sees and feels at each step, so he can imagine using it.
  • Technique 3: place the reader inside the story. Describe what will happen to him on the first day he owns the product. Use concrete scenes (specific, real moments), such as a moment with family, a traffic light, or a shop counter.
  • Technique 4: turn the demonstration into a test the reader can run himself. A demonstration means showing the product working. Give clear steps to measure the result, such as checking a number before and after, or completing a task on the first evening. The claim is then proven in his own hands.
  • Technique 5: stretch the benefits over days, weeks, and months. Show the product working in stages over time. The reader sees a continuing flow of benefits instead of a single event.
  • Technique 8: compare side by side. Compare the product with the old way or with competitors (rival products). Show the specific price or cost difference in parallel sentences (sentences with the same structure). The advantage then stands out sharply.
  • Technique 9: show the negative side. Paint the problem or the inadequate old product in full, dark detail. Then give the parallel solution item by item. The reader feels the push away from the problem and the pull toward the product together.
  • Technique 10: show how easy the product is to use. Stress how little effort, skill, or time it takes. Set that against the large benefits it delivers. The reader sees a short effort for a long-lasting return.
  • Technique 11: use metaphor, analogy, and imagination.
    • A metaphor is a comparison that says one thing is another.
    • An analogy is a comparison to something familiar.
    • Use them to make facts more dramatic when a literal (plain, word-for-word) description is dull or unbelievable.
    • To personify something means to give it human traits. Personify the product or the method if that helps the reader grasp it.
  • Technique 12: summarise before closing, using a catalog. There are two kinds.
    1. Horizontal catalog: list all applications and buyer types in one-line items to widen the market. It is a shotgun approach (a wide net) that protects against relying on one main appeal.
    2. Vertical catalog: pile up the desires in summary to deepen the desire the ad has built.
    • Use short sentences, one per item, with no further explanation.
  • Technique 13: end with the guarantee as the climax. A guarantee is a promise to refund or replace the product. The climax is the peak moment.
    • State the terms in clear numbered points. Each point has its condition and refund or replacement promise.
    • Repeat the product's main performances in the process.
    • The close becomes a summary of the product's benefits, made safe by the guarantee.

Research Benefits Before Writing Copy

1 min
  1. Read Amazon reviews of the product and of similar products. Focus on the five-star reviews (the top rating) that describe the experience after use.
  2. Note the life changes, enhancements and positive outcomes buyers describe. Similar products often solve the same problem.
  3. Search Google for "benefits of [product name]". Collect articles and blogs (websites with posts) that contain benefit points and data.
  4. Collect all points in one document. Use them in the page copy (the sales text on the product page).
  • Interest copy (sales text that builds interest) should describe the end experience, such as comfort, relief or a better daily routine.
  • It should not focus on technical specs (exact product measurements and settings) and features.
  • Most buyers care more about how the product will make them feel than about volume, functions or technical details.

Turn Features Into Benefits

2 min
  • Features are what the product has. Benefits are what it does for the buyer.
  • Identify the product benefits and the desired outcome from the features before writing any copy. Features are the proof. Benefits are what the customer buys.
  • Write each feature on its own card. Translate it into the benefit it gives the buyer. Sort the cards by importance.
  • People buy what a product does for them, not what it is. So every feature must be converted into a benefit.
  • Look beyond the obvious benefit for a hidden benefit. This is one the buyer cares about deeply but rarely states. Build the message around it.
    • Example: a product that saves work time can be pitched around the free time the buyer gets back for leisure. The buyer values that more than the savings alone.
  • Do not write landing page copy (a single web page built to sell one product) that lists features without linking them to a customer benefit.
    • The customer cannot see why the feature matters.
    • Follow each feature with a "why is it there" answer that names the result the customer gets.
  • Benefit bullet pattern: translate each feature into the outcome the customer gets, so the feature explains the benefit.
    • Pattern (a fill-in template): [Feature] keeps [surface or person] [protected outcome] without [unwanted side effect].
    • Non-slip base keeps your rug in place without damaging your floor.
    • Quick-release buckle keeps your strap secure without the hassle of fiddly fastening.
  • Building ad or landing page copy around features misses what buyers want. Features include waterproof, quick-drying, smooth writing, and no ink leakage.
    • Write about the end benefit the buyer gets, such as fun time with kids, less screen time, or children's creativity.
  • Manufacturers (the companies that make the product) write specifications (exact technical details) for technical buyers.
    • A store owner should rewrite them into customer benefits.
    • Example: easy to adjust, no tools needed instead of a thread specification (a measurement of a screw's ridges).
  • Write product descriptions as benefits, not features.
    • Features are technical specs that customers do not understand. Examples are CDMA and GSM (two types of mobile phone networks) or 110 to 240 volt switching (a device that works with the electric power of many countries).
    • Benefits state what the product does for them. Examples: works in Canada, the UK and Asia or plug it in anywhere in the world.
  • Where sources differ on how much feature content to keep, both apply in different places.
    • Product descriptions: lead with benefits and keep features as supporting detail.
    • Ads: benefits carry more weight, but features must still appear. A feature describes the product part. The benefit is the outcome that part produces.

Lead With the Outcome and the Problem

1 min
  • Sell the desired outcome first. Then list the features that support it. Features act as proof that the outcome is reachable, not as the main pitch.
  • The buyer wants to know how the outcome is achieved. So present the outcome first, then the features. Each feature serves as proof that the outcome is possible.
  • Describe the outcome with a vivid, dramatized metaphor to make the promise memorable.
    • Example: melt inches off your waist instead of lose inches.
    • Keep the tone fun and positive so exercise feels less like a chore.
  • Make the big benefit claim either the unique selling point (the one thing that sets the product apart) or the strongest benefit of the product. State it boldly.
    • A vivid metaphor can make it memorable.
    • The claim must still match the actual product benefit.
  • Persuasive benefit copy describes the customer's desired outcome.
    • Where useful, it first surfaces the customer's problem. It makes the problem feel worse before presenting the product as the solution.
    • Frame the solution as fast, instant or convenient where that is true.
    • Emphasize benefits more than features.
  • Benefit copy structure, in this order:
    1. Name the problem.
    2. State the solution.
    3. State the concrete benefit in a short form.
    • Not every page needs to surface a problem. The benefits must always be emphasized clearly.
  • Show the reader the life they could have, rather than only naming the benefit.
    • Use the word Imagine followed by a vivid scene.
    • Or drop it and state the benefit directly.
    • Example: You'll speak with confidence after attending this public speaking class.

Use Occasions and Lower the Entry Barrier

1 min
  • Occasion pattern: state the moment the product is used, then the emotional result for the buyer's loved ones. An occasion is a moment or event.
    • Formula: occasion + product's role + result for others.
Occasion Product's role Result for others Example line
Cold evenings Soft fleece blanket Whole family stays warm Keep the whole family warm on cold evenings with this soft fleece blanket.
Birthday dinner Personalized cocktail glasses Dinner feels special Make every birthday dinner special with these personalized cocktail glasses.
  • Emotional benefit pattern: replace a feature statement with a use case (a real situation where the product helps).
    • Example feature statement: high quality item, ships in two days.
    • The use case connects the product to a shared moment and the customer's loved ones.
    • Formula: person-centered situation + the product's role in it.
    • Examples: Make your weekend hikes easier with this lightweight backpack and Give your kids a calmer bedtime with this soft nightlight.
  • A line such as suitable for all ages or no experience required lowers the entry barrier (whatever stops people from starting).
    • It makes the product seem usable by anyone.
    • This removes the feeling that the buyer has to be skilled first.

Make Benefits Instant With Analogies

1 min
  • Compare the product's effect to something familiar. An analogy is a comparison to something the reader already knows, such as a common event, a sport or a household task.
    • Why it works: the reader already has a clear picture to attach the new idea to. So they understand the benefit at once.
    • Example one: Like finding a parking spot right at the front door on a busy Saturday, the new loading ramp saves the dock crew twenty minutes per shift.
    • Example two: Like having a second pair of hands on the bench, the clamp holds the frame steady while the glue sets.

Stack Benefits and Make Claims Specific

1 min
  • Benefit stacking: list the ingredients and benefits one after another in a single run. Tie each to a result the viewer wants, such as firmer, brighter, less volume loss or fewer lines.
    • One ad stacked about ten benefits in a row.
    • Stacking makes each claim feel more convincing than the last.
    • Each benefit must still match the product's real ingredients.
  • Name ingredients, such as a molecule (a tiny building block of a substance) that supports elasticity (the skin's stretch and bounce). Or a compound (a mix of substances) that works against overproduction of pigment (too much skin colour).
    • Specific names make benefits believable.
    • Link each ingredient to one concrete skin result.
  • A generic claim of lowest prices was replaced with a specific net profit figure of 3 percent. Net profit is the money kept after all costs.
    • It proved more convincing. It implied prices were already near the minimum possible.
    • Specific financial facts make a claim credible.
  • A generic purity claim was replaced with specific production details. Examples are a count of experiments or a measured process. This created a far stronger impression.
    • Turn general quality statements into exact, verifiable process facts. Verifiable means it can be checked.
  • An itemized breakdown (a list of each separate cost) of hidden costs supports a profit-margin claim better than the margin figure alone. Profit margin is the share of the price kept as profit.
    • Example: 735 dollars of hidden costs on a 1,500 dollar car.
    • Readers accept a concrete cost breakdown more readily than a bare percentage.

Build the unique mechanism

2 min

  • A unique mechanism is the one thing that makes your product different from everything the reader has tried. "Mechanism" means how the product works. Do not name competitors (other companies selling similar products). The reader works out for themselves that older solutions failed because they lacked that one ingredient, method or technology.

  • Formula: name the one specific technology, ingredient or method that sets your product apart.

    • Then imply that older solutions lacked it. Still do not name any competitor.
    • It works because the reader connects their past failures to the missing element.
    • Example 1: Most serums stop at the surface. Ours uses a sealed-release carrier that reaches the layer where the damage sits.
      • A serum is a light skin product. A carrier is the material that moves the active ingredient to where it works.
    • Example 2: Shampoo rinses away before the scalp absorbs much. This formula stays on the follicle for the full wash.
      • A follicle is the small opening in skin where a hair grows.

  • The real problem is the reason the prospect's past solutions failed. A prospect is a person you want to sell to.

    • Find the real problem by reverse-engineering the answer to the uniqueness question. Reverse-engineering means starting from your answer and working backward to the problem it solves.
    • Once revealed, the reader sees that old approaches never addressed it.
    • Your new mechanism does address it. This gives them hope.

  • Explain the mechanism by linking it to a belief the audience already holds. This makes an unusual claim feel obvious.

    • The explanation and its proof are also copy. Copy means the sales words in your message.
    • They must be engaging. Wrap them in the story structure, which is the way a story is built, such as problem first, then solution.

  • Mechanism formula, in order:

    1. Existing belief the audience already holds.
    2. Your mechanism follows logically from that belief.
    3. Third-party proof confirms the result. Third-party proof comes from someone outside your company, such as a lab or a publication.

    Why it works:

    • Readers accept a mechanism faster when it extends something they already believe.
    • Outside sources make the claim harder to dismiss.
    • Example 1: Most people already know consistent sleep drives recovery; our routine schedules the light and temperature cues that make that consistency happen, and an independent sleep lab measured the effect.
    • Example 2: Shoppers already believe fresh produce keeps its nutrients when frozen right after harvest; our delivery cold chain applies exactly that, and independent testing measured the vitamin retention.
      • A cold chain is the unbroken cold storage and transport that keeps food fresh.

  • Give proof on the first page without revealing the mechanism yet.

    • A reference such as a patent filing number (the official number of a patent application) or a named publication (a magazine or site, named by title) shows the opportunity is real.
    • The explanation of how it works comes later in the letter. A letter here means the long sales text.

Proof elements

3 min
  • Each promise needs proof at least as strong as the claim. Proof means evidence.
    • Useful proof includes testimonials (a customer's praise), short anecdotes (small true stories), and clear metaphors (comparisons that make an idea easy to picture).
    • It also includes case studies (detailed stories of one customer's results), charts, and specific statistics (exact numbers).
    • Promises that are not proven make skeptics (people who doubt) doubt the whole message.
  • Proof should come in threes.
    • One or two pieces leave the claim feeling unresolved.
    • Three is the standard target, and more is better.
    • Common proof types are case studies, demonstrations, testimonials, quotes from authorities (experts in the field), charts and graphs, official documents, and articles or headlines.
  • When a claim is too high to prove with three examples, build proof from a sequence of smaller examples that add up to the claim. A sequence is a series of steps in order.
    1. Show one example worth $5,000.
    2. Show a second example worth $7,500.
    3. Show a third example worth $10,000.
    4. State the total claim of $10,000 as proven.
  • Show proof visually where possible, such as charts and screenshots.
    • Reframe each proof point two or three times in different forms. A reframe shows the same information from a new angle.
    • Example: turn a one-time $10,000 gain into a yearly income of six figures (100,000 or more).
    • Another example: compare it to a familiar benchmark (a standard to measure against), such as an S&P 500 return over a year. The S&P 500 is a famous index of 500 large US companies.
  • Proof answers the question "how do I know this is real?" It must back up the one belief.
    • Early proof can be historical charts, testimonials, or third-party quotes (quotes from outside people).
    • In competitive markets, where skepticism is high, put proof right after the big promise.
  • Testimonials should mimic the target reader as closely as possible:
    • Show images of people who look like the ideal customer. They should reflect that customer's money situation and life situation.
    • Use royalty-free or commercially licensed images. Royalty-free means you pay once and can reuse the image. Commercially licensed means the license allows business use.
    • Never use random images from a search engine.
    • Make testimonials specific: amounts, timelines, steps followed, family reactions.
    • Specificity conveys Big, Fast, Easy, Safe and Anybody. These mean: big result, fast result, easy to do, safe to try, and works for anybody.
  • For mailings that sell a product for purchase now (a mailing is a sales message sent to people):
    • Use testimonials from happy users. They matter more than anything else for sales.
    • Use photographs rather than drawings.
    • Prove the product is easy to use through copy, photos or testimonials.
  • For mailings that sell professional services (advice or work done by people, not physical goods):
    • Factual credibility supports the offer. Examples are years in business, clients served, and sample client lists.
    • Real customer experiences, benefits achieved, and doubts resolved are more persuasive.
    • Make intangible benefits (benefits you cannot see or touch) tangible. Use before and after photos, case histories and examples.
    • Consider offering a free first consultation or free information to break down skepticism.
  • Historical example, not current data:
    • In ordinary homes of that period, about two-thirds of the heat from the furnace (the machine that heats a house) was wasted.
    • One-third was lost through windows and doors.
    • Another third was lost through uninsulated walls and roofs. Uninsulated means without insulation, the material that slows heat loss.
    • A product that stops that waste can use these figures to make its case.

Close with a bottom line

1 min
  • Close the benefits section with a bottom line, the final short summary.
    • Use three short, parallel adjectives. Parallel means the words match in form, such as all single adjectives.
    • The three words should sum up the product. Example: fun, simple, effective.
    • Three words are easy to remember and repeat.
    • The rhythm makes the claims feel complete and confident.
    • Example 1: Fast, quiet, and built to last.
    • Example 2: Light, clean, and easy to store.
