Claims, compliance and legal risk

Section 12.25 · 25 of 26 in this chapter · 16 min read

In short

  • Prove every claim before you publish it. Keep a folder of proof for each one.
  • Never promise health, money or a better life in an ad. Ad networks switch accounts off for this.
  • Never copy other sellers' ads, images, videos or reviews. Make your own.
  • Never fake urgency, stock levels or reviews. That is cheating your customers.
  • Check every ad, image and landing page against each platform's rules before you launch.
This section has 15 parts. Tap one to open it.

Ground rules for every claim

2 min
  • Never publish a statement without proof. Proof is evidence you can show.
    • Example: you claim "lose three kilos in a week." Then add a customer testimonial (a quote from a real buyer) or a data point from a clinical trial (a formal test on people).
    • If you mention dosage (how much to take) or effectiveness (how well it works), link to the study or name it.
    • Show the evidence before you ask for the purchase.
  • Check every claim for truth and relevance before you publish it. Truth means it is correct. Relevance means it matters to the reader.
    • Readers who doubt a claim, or cannot see how it relates to them, stop reading.
  • Match your ad creative (the picture, video or text of the ad) to the product's real ingredients and results.
    • You may copy a winning structure (the layout and order of parts) from a successful ad.
    • The claims must still be true for your product. Otherwise the ad fails platform review (the check that ad networks run on every ad) and buyers lose trust.
  • Match the reframe (a new way of looking at the problem), the proof and the call to action (CTA, the line or button that asks the reader to act) to the real offer.
    • A script that promises more than the product delivers makes buyers feel misled after the click.
  • Describe the product accurately.
    • Buyers who purchase it should be the ones who want to keep it.
    • Accurate pictures and descriptions help reduce returns (products sent back).
  • Imitate the form and structure of researched ads. Never copy their exact wording.
    • Copying ad text word for word is plagiarism (taking someone's work as your own).
    • It is also illegal copyright infringement (using protected work without permission).
  • Raise the stakes only with real, serious risks.
    • Do not present very unlikely risks as likely.
    • Fear-mongering (scaring people with exaggerated danger) without substance is manipulative and ethically questionable.
  • Review AI-written ad copy against Google Ads policies before you turn on any ad.
    • Policies are each platform's rulebook.
    • Google enforces strict rules on health claims, personalised content and protected categories (topics with extra rules, such as health and money).
    • Non-compliant ads are disapproved (rejected) or suspended (switched off).
  • Check the landing page too.
    • The landing page is the web page the ad sends people to.
    • Platforms review it together with the ad, not only the ad.
    • Health or financial result promises on that page can lead to disapproval or suspension of the ad account (your advertising account on the platform).
  • Add a note telling the reader to consult a tax advisor (a professional who advises on taxes) for any claim about tax deductibility (money you can subtract from your taxes).
    • Keep the claim qualified (limited by conditions).
    • Never promise a specific tax result.

Health, finance and regulated claims

3 min
  • Never make health, finance or general life-quality claims in ads.
    • Examples: "earn more money," "improve your health" or "make your life better."
    • Ad networks flag these as potentially misleading, unsupported or misaligned with the landing page.
    • They regularly lead to account suspension.
    • **
  • Claims that get health or finance products into trouble include the following.
    • A product that "cures a serious disease."
    • A guaranteed specific income within a set time.
    • Both are result promises. Platforms and regulators (government bodies that enforce consumer and money rules) review them.
    • **
  • A health promise with a fixed timeframe is high risk. Example: "lose 10 pounds in 8 weeks."
    • It is safe only unless a peer-reviewed scientific study (a study checked by other experts before publication) proves that exact result.
    • Such a study is very unlikely for most small product brands.
    • **
  • For products where health claims are not allowed, such as an infrared sauna (a heated box that uses infrared light), do not make health claims in the copy (the written text).
    • Let the reader draw health conclusions personally.
    • The aim is to send paid traffic (visitors from paid ads) to the page, not to educate on health benefits there.
    • **
  • Health claims and ingredient-harm claims (claims that an ingredient is harmful) need a verifiable source before launch. Verifiable means you can check it.
    • Launch means the moment your ads go live.
    • Check them against Meta's ad policies. Meta is the company behind Facebook and Instagram.
    • Claims about attraction, status or body image also carry policy risk.
    • Test them cautiously.
    • Keep a proof file (a folder of evidence) for each claim.
  • Avoid large claims about health or prevention (stopping illness before it starts) that tests do not support.
    • Keep the main headline (the biggest line of text) on benefits readers already want.
    • Errors in health claims can lead to rejection by media (newspapers, TV and websites that publish ads) and damage trust.
  • For health-adjacent food products (food linked to health goals), do not make medical claims.
    • Examples of these foods are low-calorie, keto (a low-carb diet) and diabetic-friendly (a term for people who watch their blood sugar).
    • Do not make medical claims about treating diabetes or obesity (a serious weight problem).
    • Do not promise guaranteed weight loss.
    • Use lifestyle wording (words about everyday habits) instead, such as:
      • "supports fitness goals,"
      • "macro-friendly" (fits daily targets for protein, fat and carbs),
      • "low-calorie alternative."
  • When copy compares a product reading against a recommended limit, the figures must come from the manufacturer's testing (testing done by the company that made the product).
    • Example: an electromagnetic field (EMF, the energy field from electrical devices) of 1 mg against a recommended maximum of 3 mg.
    • State the figures as that source's data.
    • Do not turn the comparison into a promise of health or safety outcomes.
    • **
  • For products outside health and finance, the promise can describe what the product actually does for the buyer.
    • Make it specific. Write the how into the promise.
    • Vague phrases such as "improves your life" are not enough.
    • **
  • An expert presenter (a person who speaks as an authority in the ad) makes a claim easier to accept.
    • Name the credential (a real qualification, such as a coach's certificate or a clinician's license) early in the ad.
    • Clinician means a doctor or nurse.
    • Use only credentials that are real and relevant to the claim.
  • Tax claims: see the ground rule above. Do not write tax results as promises.

Superlatives, hype and numeric claims

1 min
  • Superlative means a "the best" type word. Hype means over-the-top talk. A numeric claim is a claim built on a number.
  • Use the table below to choose words.
Claim type Avoid Use instead Source
Unsupported superlatives "number one," "highest quality" A best or highest-rated claim only with a measurable proof point next to it, such as the number of reviews
Hype "multiply your life by ten," "ten thousand percent better than everything on the market" Specific, checkable facts
Generic superlatives "best in the world," "lowest price" Specific facts that can be checked
Numeric certainty "100% certain," especially in niches where it is implausible (for example the stock market) A specific figure such as 93%, kept legally defensible
  • Generic and superlative statements are discounted by readers. They suggest exaggeration.
  • A 100% claim reads as unbelievable in many niches (small markets or specialist topics). It increases skepticism.

Urgency, scarcity and sale timing

1 min
  • Fake urgency (false pressure to buy now) must never appear in a call to action.
    • Fake sales, fake countdown timers (clocks that count down to a deadline that is not real) and false stock levels are dishonest and deceive customers.
    • Example of a false stock level: showing 5 left while 1,000 sit in the warehouse.
    • **
  • Repeating a 10% off sale that ends on Sunday and is replaced by another 10% off sale on Monday is effectively a lie, not real urgency.
    • Habitually deceiving customers also tends to spread into other areas of the business.
    • **
  • Black Friday (the big US shopping day after Thanksgiving):
    • Keep creatives (ads) and sale banners (sale signs on a site) running for a week or more after the sale ends, so the offer still appears active.
    • The source frames this as slightly shady.
    • Treat it as a judgement call and avoid misleading claims.
    • Conflict: calls a sale that is repeated back-to-back a lie. allows banners to stay up a week after a sale ends. Keep the banner only if the offer genuinely remains available. Never present it as a new deadline.
  • Aggressive "breakthrough" claims (claims of a huge new discovery), combined with urgency, can get ads and offers banned by ad networks.
    • One source reports that a cryptocurrency offer (an offer to buy digital money such as Bitcoin) written this way brought in over 100,000 new paid names (leads, people who gave their details) before Google and other networks banned it.
    • Check each network's policies before launch.
    • Keep claims defensible (able to be justified with proof).

Proof, reviews and credentials

1 min
  • Use only product reviews that the supplier (the company that makes or ships your product) has given you permission to use.
    • Never import reviews from other websites or from Amazon.
    • **
  • Amazon reviews must not be copied and pasted into ads.
    • Amazon treats them as genuine customer reviews.
    • Amazon may penalise (punish) sellers who misuse them.
    • A copied review can also send viewers to Amazon, where the product is often cheaper.
  • Ads with fake comment screenshots (pictures of invented comments) carry a risk.
    • They can be caught by platforms or competitors, with consequences.
    • A lower chance of detection does not make such practices risk-free.
    • Using copyrighted footage (video protected by copyright) or others' ads as hooks (the first seconds that grab attention) can lead to takedown demands (orders to remove content) or legal claims.
  • Celebrity or famous-name mentions draw attention. Examples: "used by a famous person" or "an expert credited the product for results."
    • Check that the link is real.
    • Check that the product is genuinely connected to the person or event.
  • Never take another person's creative work and present it as your own.
    • Creative work includes ad copy, videos, images and store text.
    • Create original material, or adapt it with clear permission.
  • Seeded reviews (invented reviews placed to look real):
    • A paste-ready template (text you can copy straight in) is not reproduced here. It is a format for invented testimonials.
    • Its structure, for reference only:
      • a quoted testimonial about one specific benefit, in plain language;
      • a five-star rating, a first name, a city or state, and "USA";
      • a photo of the product or a customer using it;
      • two or three similar comments with different names and testimonials, so the ad does not look artificial.
    • Presenting invented comments as real customer proof is deceptive and is not permitted.

Copying competitors and third-party content

1 min

Legal and platform risks

2 min
  • Copying competitor ads (ads from rival sellers) that use their own branding or custom content can get the video taken down or the product shut down.
  • Competitor video content should not be copied directly.
    • The competitor can file a copyright complaint (a formal claim that you used their protected work) and have the videos removed.
    • Short-term testing with adapted scripts is safer.
    • A shoot-your-own approach (filming your own footage) is safer too.
    • Supplier videos can be a base, but re-edit them with your own captions (on-screen text).
  • Reusing footage or layouts from other creators carries legal and platform risk.
    • Make the decision to use third-party content (anything made by someone else) on purpose.
    • Creating original footage, or licensing content (paying for the right to use it), avoids the risk.
  • Drop shippers (sellers who ship supplier products without holding stock) may send legal notices that can shut down a domain (your web address).
    • Take inspiration from competitor stores, but do not copy their pages exactly.
    • Rewrite all copy in your own words, for example with ChatGPT (an AI writing tool).
  • Do not copy the layout or wording of another ad so closely that it could be mistaken for theirs.
    • A close copy violates the rights of the original writer.
  • Do not copy competitors' product images, even when a competing listing shows good ideas.
    • Create your own images that show the same benefits in your own way.
  • Copying a competitor's product images directly can lead to a DMCA takedown (a removal order under the US law called the Digital Millennium Copyright Act) of the Shopify store (an online shop built on the Shopify platform).
    • Use your own images, or substantially edited ones.
  • Big brands have legal teams (lawyers) and can pursue action beyond DMCA notices.
    • Avoid copying their content.
  • TikTok competitor content:
    • Avoid big brands such as Adidas and Gymshark (big clothing brands). They enforce DMCA takedowns within one to two weeks.
    • Use recent content, less than one to two months old, rather than outdated videos.
    • Do not claim celebrity endorsements (a famous person's approval) or false associations.
  • Do not copy videos from large brands.
    • Skip any video in which the product or brand logo is clearly visible.
    • Copying is limited to short tests of content not owned by a major brand.
  • Once a dropshipping brand (a store selling products shipped by suppliers) has proof of concept (proof that the idea works, shown by sales) and sales, replace copied content with original content. Build a distinct brand.
    • A large, known brand that gets caught copying faces more reputational damage (harm to its reputation).
  • Original footage separates your brand from competitors and makes copying harder.
    • Copycats (people who copy successful ads) are a common problem once an ad performs well.

Test windows for competitor and third-party content

1 min

Sources give different windows. Use the one that matches your situation.

Content Window or rule When it applies Source
Competitor or shared video 3-4 days in testing Only if many people are already using the same content
Competitor video About one to two weeks, then switch to custom content Standard short test; longer runs raise takedown risk that can remove the landing page or ads
Competitor logo, product name or shot content (scenes or footage) One to two weeks at most in testing Best avoided altogether
Another store's video or an Instagram photo Test only; replace with your own creator content within one or two days Any use outside a test is not permitted
Brand face (a famous person linked to the brand), hired model or custom content DMCA notice or ad ban within about seven to eight days The risk appears within this time
  • Check whether a supplier photo is generic (a stock image used by many sellers) or custom (made for one brand) before you use it.
    1. Run a Google image search on the photo. This is a search by picture.
    2. If the same photo appears on many stores, and on AliExpress or Alibaba (big online supplier marketplaces), treat it as a generic supplier image.
    3. If other stores do not use it, or your version differs from the supplier listing, someone probably made it custom. Do not use it.
  • Using a supplier or competitor product image without edits can lead to a DMCA notice, even after months of use.
    • The site can go down for an hour or two.
    • Remove the image and republish the site (publish it again) to bring it back online.
  • Using a brand's face, hired model or custom content in your ads or store risks a DMCA notice or ad ban within about seven to eight days.

Images and product photos

1 min
  • Google Ads can reject or hide images that contain text.
    • Text overlays (words added on top of a picture) are not a ban.
    • Google may stop showing that image and show other images instead.
  • The store must not depend on images with text only.
    • Check the text on each image against Google's policies.
  • Only upload display ad images (banner-style ads on websites) that you have the legal rights to distribute.
    • Google asks advertisers to confirm this before uploading.
    • **
  • Images that require attribution (a credit line naming the creator) need a credit line.
    • Shopify product pages have no built-in place for image credits.
    • Check each image's licence (the permission terms for use) before you use it on a store.
  • Do not copy product images from other websites or from general Google results.
    • Use only images explicitly labelled as allowed for reuse.
    • **
  • Using a competitor's logo, product name or shot content in ads carries a copyright strike risk against your ad account. A copyright strike is a formal copyright penalty.
    • Avoid these creatives.
    • Or use them for one to two weeks at most in testing.
  • Reusing a competitor's logo and product name can make customers distrust your store. This happens when the landing page shows a different brand.
    • Replacing the logo and product name is quick.
    • It reduces that mismatch.
  • Copying a competitor's logo, brand name, trademark (a protected name or sign) or text branding into a remapped ad (an ad rebuilt with the same layout) is a legal and brand risk.
    • Recreate only the layout, composition (how things are arranged) and visual arrangement.
    • Replace every branded element with your own.

Reused video and platform detection

1 min

Platform detection means the ways platforms spot reused videos.

Methods that change metadata

1 min

Metadata is hidden information inside a file, such as its size, device or date.

  • Changing a video's playback speed (for example, between 1.02x and 1.05x, a tiny speed change) changes its metadata. There is no visible change.
    • Some sellers use this method.
    • It goes against the spirit of platform rules on duplicate content (the same content posted again).
    • It carries a risk of penalties.
  • Changing playback speed or clips alters metadata. This can reduce the chance that Facebook detects reused content.
    • This applies only to short tests, not to long-term use.
    • Conflict: treats speed changes as a penalty risk. treats them as a test-only tactic. Use neither for ongoing campaigns.
  • Video editing apps such as CapCut or Filmora can change a video's file metadata and aspect ratio (the shape of the frame, tall or wide).
    • This is a practical workaround (a way around a problem) that has worked for some sellers.
    • It is not a guaranteed method. Platforms can still detect reused video.
  • For tests, short clips under five seconds from supplier or stock sources (ready-made footage libraries) are considered lower risk. Sources include AliExpress, Alibaba and Fiverr (a freelance service marketplace).
    • TikTok content can also be used cross-platform (on more than one platform) in tests.
    • Facebook cannot check TikTok data.

Rules on identical or reused content

1 min
  • Do not copy-paste the same video from another store into your ad account.
    • Identical reused videos can cause problems with the ad account.
    • Download several creatives and edit them so each one is clearly different.
  • TikTok penalises accounts that run identical copies of the same video.
    • Ripping a competitor's content (copying a video off their account) is more likely to fail quickly in the US market than in Europe.
  • Controversial or offensive jokes can lead TikTok to report and remove an account.
    • Indirect wording and clearly signalled humour lower the risk.
    • Avoid direct jokes on sensitive topics.

Music, trademarks and branding

1 min
  • Music taken from a TikTok sound list (TikTok's library of songs) is a risky choice for a paid ad. Its rights may not cover advertising.
    • Use music you have the rights to, such as licensed library tracks (music you have paid for or been allowed to use).
  • Trademark (a protected brand name, logo or sign) and branded elements: see the logo and branding rules above.
    • Only layout, composition and visual arrangement may be recreated.

Mailings and offline tactics

1 min

These are known tactics for printed mail. They are listed so you know the compliance requirements. They are not recommended methods.

  • Official-looking imprints (printed marks that look official) are used to get past postal workers and company gatekeepers (the staff who sort and screen mail).
    • Examples include verified or audited delivery wording, or seal-style labels (stamp-like labels) about enclosed important documents.
    • The source says they work best in political, fundraising and business-to-business (B2B, sales between companies) mailings.
    • Do not use them to imply an official or priority item that is not one. That is deceptive.
  • Tear-sheet mailings (a printed page torn off a bigger sheet, often sent with a sticky note on top) and sneak-up tactics (ways to make a mailing look like something else at first glance) must be cleared with a printer and mailing house. They must know the laws that govern them.
    • Some large users print the word "Advertisement" in small type on both the sticky note and the tear sheet. There is no measurable loss of response.
    • The sender must not use real publication names, logos or trademarks.

Pre-launch compliance check

1 min
  1. Confirm that every claim has a proof file. Confirm that health claims have a verifiable source, checked against the relevant platform policy.
  2. Read the landing page as the reviewer does. It is reviewed together with the ad.
  3. Check each image for text overlays (words added on top) and confirm the text meets Google's policies.
  4. Confirm the rights to every image, video and piece of music, and the attribution requirements.
  5. Confirm that no competitor logo, product name, trademark or custom content appears. Confirm that third-party videos stay within their test window.
  6. Confirm that urgency and sale timing are true. Confirm that any tax reference includes the advisor note.
  7. Check the policies of each network (Google, Meta, TikTok) before launch. A rule set can differ by platform.
