Playbook › 12. Creatives and ad copy

Avatars, awareness, sophistication and angles

Section 12.3 · 3 of 26 in this chapter · 38 min read

In short

  • Every buyer is at one of five awareness stages, from not knowing they have a problem to being ready to buy.
  • Write each ad for one stage only, because each stage needs a different opening.
  • Use the stage to choose the lead type (the kind of opening), the ad angle and how direct the copy is.
  • Use the customer's exact words in your copy, not marketing language.
  • Do not use one formula for every audience.
More key points
  • Every desire needs its reason, the feeling behind it, such as fear or shame. The reason makes your message stronger.
  • Sell what the buyer wants first. Then give them what they need.
  • Check that a feeling appears in real buyers' own words before you build an ad on it.
  • Fit matters more than views. A small crowd of right buyers beats a big crowd of wrong ones.
  • One copy flow can serve many visitors. Start it at Problem Aware (the buyer knows they have a problem) instead of writing separate copy for each stage.
  • Copy that starts too late, at Product Aware (the buyer knows the products), loses visitors who are still earlier.
  • Name the pain first. Then show the solution, then lead the reader to the buy.
  • Write the avatar (a detailed picture of one typical buyer) before any copy.
  • Do not push a purchase to people who are not ready. Show each group the message that fits their stage.

Awareness stages: the five levels

2 min

Schwartz's awareness scale is a five-step ladder that shows how much a buyer knows about the problem and your product. It has five stages, counted from unaware upward:

  • Stage 1, Unaware: does not know they have a problem.

  • Stage 2, Problem-Aware: knows the problem, does not know that solutions exist.

  • Stage 3, Solution-Aware: knows solutions exist, but not all options.

  • Stage 4, Product-Aware: knows your product, unsure whether it is right.

  • Stage 5, Most Aware: knows your product, may be skeptical or comparing.

  • Default to Stage 2 for cold traffic (people who have never heard of your brand) with new products.

  • Only change this if research clearly shows something else.

The same five levels, counted from most aware downward:

  • Knows the product and wants it (most aware).
  • Knows the product but does not want it yet (product-aware).
  • Knows the desire but not the product (solution-aware).
  • Knows the need but not the solution (problem-aware).
  • Completely unaware of the desire (unaware).
  • Each level is separated from the next by a psychological wall (an invisible barrier in the mind) between indifference and strong interest.

The same levels are described by what the prospect (someone who might buy) knows, and what lead type suits them:

  • Most aware: knows the product and only needs the deal.
  • Product-aware: knows the product but doubts it fits.
  • Solution-aware: wants the result but does not know the product delivers it.
  • Problem-aware: feels the problem but not that a solution exists.
  • Completely unaware: no knowledge of the problem, product or category.
  • The awareness level decides the lead type (the kind of opening) and how direct the copy (the written sales text) should be.

The stages are also described by what each reader knows:

  • Unaware readers do not see the problem.

  • Problem-aware readers know the problem but not that a fix exists.

  • Solution-aware readers know fixes exist but not your product.

  • Product-aware readers know your product but are not yet convinced.

  • Most-aware readers only need a reason to buy now.

  • Match each ad to one of the five stages.

  • Each stage needs a different opening.

  • A reader who does not know they have a problem needs a different start than someone already looking for your product.

Judging the stage and the copy implication

3 min
  • Classify each customer on Schwartz's five stages.
  • Language about failed methods (things they tried that did not work) and competitor names (rival brands) signals later stages.
  • Pure "struggling" language signals Problem-Aware.
  • Right after classifying, write the copy implication: one strategic rule that shapes the voice (the tone and personality of the writing) and approach for that stage.
  • For example, for a Problem-Aware audience the voice must sound like a person describing how the world operates, not a polished advertiser trying to heal broken belief.
  • To judge how unaware a prospect is, ask how new the product is in the market. Also ask whether similar products already exist.
  • If similar products exist, the prospect has a frame of reference (a basis to compare against) and is not fully unaware.
  • In that case, a more direct lead can be used.
  • Use the stage to choose the ad angle (the main idea the ad is built on).
  • Buyers who do not yet recognize the problem respond better to ads that surface a need or desire.
  • Buyers already comparing options respond better to comparison ads, such as us-versus-them (ads that compare you directly with rivals).
  • Do not use one formula for every audience.
  • Unaware customers do not know they have a problem.
  • So the copy must first educate them about its consequences, for example by asking whether they have noticed a specific risk or a specific symptom.
  • Once they recognize the problem, position the product as the solution.
  • Problem-aware customers know the problem but not that a solution exists.
  • So use problem-agitate-solution (PAS), a three-step method: state the problem, make it feel worse, then introduce the product.
  • Use clear, specific wording of the frustration to make it land.
  • Solution-aware customers already know alternatives exist, such as lint rollers (sticky sheets that pick up fluff) or scrapers.
  • So surface the problem, then show a unique feature that solves it in a newer or better way than those alternatives.
  • Do not just repeat that a solution exists.
  • Product-aware customers know your product but have not bought it.
  • So highlight features that make it better than other products.
  • Also address their common objections (reasons they hesitate) to ease the purchase decision.
  • Match the copy to market awareness.
  • An audience unaware of the problem needs education.
  • An audience aware of the problem needs the solution.
  • An audience aware of solutions needs a clear reason to choose your product.
  • A crowded market (many similar sellers) needs a unique mechanism (a unique way the product works) or angle.
  • Buyers who have tried many methods and now feel skeptical are usually problem-aware and leaning toward solution-aware.
  • Lead with empathy (showing you understand how they feel) for the methods that failed.
  • Do not lead with a polished product pitch.
  • Before writing, identify where the prospect sits on Schwartz's five levels.
  • The level decides the lead type and how direct the copy should be.
  • For problem-aware prospects, find the point of maximum anxiety: the frustration, desperation, fear or anger the problem causes.
  • Opening with that emotion earns a connection before any product is named.
  • The most aware buyers usually sit on the house list (your own email list of past customers).
  • They also sit among multi-buyers, customers who have bought before and come back.
  • These groups are the easiest to sell to, so the offer can be direct and short for them.
  • Problem-aware prospects need the problem explained.
  • Solution-aware prospects know several solutions exist and compare approaches.
  • Product-aware prospects compare specific products or providers. They need differentiators (features that set you apart).
  • Buyers sit at different points of awareness.
  • Some know they have a problem but not that solutions exist. Others know a solution exists but not which one to choose.
  • Copy works best when it speaks to the stage the reader is actually in.
  • That makes the seller seem to understand them.
  • When a buyer feels the seller understands their problem better than they do, the buyer assumes the seller also has the solution.
  • Copy that speaks at the buyer's current awareness level builds this trust and makes selling easier.

Writing for each stage: the three-text pattern

1 min

Use three primary texts (main pieces of writing), one for each of three awareness stages.

  • The problem-aware text opens on the pain and hints that a solution exists.
  • The solution-aware text admits that other options fell short. It then introduces the mechanism with proof.
  • The transformation text shows life after the product. It lists what is included and makes the offer clear.
  • It works because each reader meets the message at the stage they are actually in.
  • Problem-aware example, in italics: Tired of cold draughts creeping under the door every winter? There is a simpler way to seal them this weekend.
  • Transformation example, in italics: Picture the first winter evening your living room stays warm without touching the thermostat. The full sealing kit arrives in one box.

The product-journey ladder (a different model)

1 min

This is a second five-stage ladder. It shows how familiar the buyer is with the product and the purchase.

  • It is not Schwartz's awareness scale. Do not mix the two.
  • Five stages run from not knowing the product exists, to considering it, to planning a purchase and needing details, to buying, and finally to habitual repeat use.
  • Ads can cover all five stages in a single piece of copy.
  • Or a series of ads can move the prospect from one stage to the next over time.
  • Start copy at the stage where the target audience is, not where the product is.
  • Early-stage prospects need an explanation of what the product is.
  • Late-stage buyers need reasons to choose it.

Market sophistication: what the market has already heard

1 min

Market sophistication means how much the market has already heard about similar claims. A claim is a promise the product makes, such as "cleans hair."

  • Market sophistication describes how exposed a market is to a claim.
  • It sets the type of headline. A headline is the big line at the top of an ad.
  • Market sophistication describes how many competing products and claims the audience has already seen.
  • If you are first to market, state the basic claim.
  • If you are second, but the product is new to the audience, enlarge the claim the first seller made.
  • Example: from "cleans hair" to "cleans hair quicker than ever before."
  • When the audience has heard many similar claims, lead with a new mechanism.
  • A mechanism is a unique feature that solves the problem in a better, more sophisticated way.
  • When competitors copy that mechanism, exaggerate and amplify it further. Do not invent something unrelated.

Headline approach by sophistication stage

1 min

Each stage below describes the market situation and the headline approach that fits it.

Sophistication stage Market situation Headline approach
Stage 1 First to market State the need or claim simply and directly.
Stage 2 Claim already seen, product new Enlarge the claim and outbid competitors on the big benefit.
Stage 3 Competitors have claimed everything Add a new mechanism or feature to the headline that solves the problem in a new, unique way.
Stage 4 Competitors copy the mechanism Embellish the mechanism and solve more of the problem, or find a new feature that is more believable.
Stage 5 Market no longer believes claims Shift from desire to identification, speaking to who the customer is and who they aspire to be, as large commodity brands do (big brands selling everyday goods).

Assessing sophistication for a product

1 min
  1. Pick the product and define the problem it solves.
  2. Count the competitors selling the same product.
  3. List the competitors solving the same problem with a different product (for example lint rollers or hard brushes).
  4. Decide the sophistication stage from these counts and the claims already made. Then choose the headline approach for that stage.

Headlines at sophistication stage 2

1 min
  • Formula: state the core claim, then add a measured enlargement of its size, speed or count.
  • Each new competitor tries to promise more than the last.
  • Why it works: when the market has not yet seen the bigger number, a larger claim wins attention.
  • The limit is reached when the claim becomes unbelievable.
  • Lose up to 30 pounds in 6 weeks or get your money back.
  • Get 60 hours of battery life, then 90, then 120 hours on the same device.

Headlines at sophistication stage 3

1 min

Procedure for a market that has heard every claim and every extreme:

  1. Accept that repeating or exaggerating the old claim will no longer work.
  2. Introduce a new mechanism that explains how the old promise is achieved now.
  3. Move the emphasis from what the product does to how it works.
  4. Feature the mechanism in the headline, reducing the old claim to a single short word or phrase that points to it.
  5. Once the reader accepts that a new chance exists, restate the full claim in the lead paragraph (the first paragraph under the headline) to show everything they get.
  • Formula: mechanism first, claim second.
  • The headline names the new way the product works, in a short, striking phrase.
  • The lead paragraph restates the full promise and the benefits it brings.
  • Why it works: a familiar claim becomes fresh and believable again when it is explained through a new process.
    • Melts the stubborn sweat patches out of your shirts before they set, the first laundry booster with a cooling enzyme. Lead: fresh shirts after a full day of heat, in one wash, with no scrubbing.
    • The first electric kettle that heats from the bottom plate. Lead: the full promise of boiling a litre in under two minutes.
  • Solution-aware markets: the headline should name the unique feature or mechanism, not only the problem.
    • Market awareness (Schwartz's stages) decides what the ad leads with.
    • Schwartz's scale places a solution-aware market at stage three. Buyers know the problem and know that competing solutions exist.
    • The ad should not only restate the problem. It should show a unique feature or mechanism that sets the product apart from the alternatives.

Headlines at sophistication stage 4

1 min
  • In the fourth stage, a competitor has just introduced a new mechanism that is selling.
  • Respond by elaborating on that mechanism.
  • Make it easier, quicker or surer. Let it solve more of the problem. Overcome old limitations or promise extra benefits.
  • This copy eventually loses believability.
  • So the next move is a new mechanism that the market accepts.
  • When a rival launches a new mechanism for the same claim and it sells, counter it.
  • Make the approach easier, quicker and surer. Let it solve more of the problem. Overcome its old limits and promise extra benefits.
  • This keeps you competitive in markets where promises keep escalating.

Headlines for unaware and resistant markets

2 min
  • Introducing a new product to a prospect who wants what it does but does not yet know that the product exists:
    1. Name the desire or its solution in the headline, pinpointing the vague desire that is spreading.
    2. Prove that the solution can actually be achieved.
    3. Show that the mechanism that achieves it is contained in the product.
  • Execution matters more than mechanics here.
  • Success needs analysis of the right market.
  • Success needs intuition to sense a trend early and to see when it becomes profitable.
  • Success needs verbal creativity to give a name to what people feel but have not yet expressed.
  • When the market is completely unaware or resistant, first eliminate three headline options.
    • Do not mention the price or any price reduction.
    • Do not put the product name in the headline or in prominent display type (large, eye-catching lettering).
    • Do not state directly what the product does, what desire it satisfies or what problem it solves.
    • Each of these is either meaningless or will fail at that stage.
  • At the most unaware stage, the headline must be an identification headline.
  • An identification headline names the reader's own situation or feeling.
  • It calls the market together around an emotion, attitude or situation it already feels.
  • It sells nothing directly. It exists only to get the reader into the next paragraph.
  • Each paragraph then pulls the reader into the next.
  • The order is: identification, then awareness of the problem or desire, then the realisation that a solution exists, and finally the product.
  • An identification headline must be the prospect's own headline, describing their problem and their state of mind right now.
  • It should attract only the logical prospects and repel everyone else.
  • It must not be a cute, funny or shocking line with no real headline behind it.
  • Before writing the headline, answer three questions.
  • First: what mass desire (a strong want shared by many people) drives the market?
  • Second: how much does the market already know about how the product satisfies it? This is the state of awareness.
  • Third: how many similar products has the market already seen? This is the state of sophistication.
  • The first answer gives the force.
  • The second and third give the position of the market relative to the product, and therefore the content of the headline.
  • A headline that works at one awareness level usually fails at another.
  • A headline that worked for a market will stop working once that market moves to a new level.
  • Re-check the awareness level before reusing any headline.

Avatar document

1 min

The avatar document (a made-up profile of one typical customer) has six required analysis sections. Ground every claim in verbatim research data (quoted word for word).

Section What to write
(1) Awareness stage Classify on Schwartz's 5 stages using the rule that failed-method and competitor-name language signals later stages while pure "struggling" language signals Problem-Aware, then state the copy implication.
(2) Core fear / fear direction The single dominant fear underneath the surface complaint, which direction it points (future dread vs. present shame), quoted from data.
(3) Failed methods What customers tried that didn't work, phrased as "this will never work" reasoning that copy can lean on.
(4) Emotional drivers and trigger moments The specific moment or circumstance that makes them buy.
(5) Their own words Verbatim swipe list (exact phrases customers use, copied as they are) of exact phrases customers use for the problem and desired outcome, to be echoed in copy without replacing them with marketing language.
(6) Angle map Distinct angles (each = fear + mechanism), ranked by data support.
  • In the avatar, ground every claim about the customer in verbatim data from your research.
  • If the research is thin on a point, state that explicitly.
  • Do not infer or invent customer psychology.
  • Give the avatar a name, an age and a photo.
  • Then write ad copy and emails as if speaking directly to that one person.
  • A concrete, named person makes copy more specific than a broad demographic label (a general group such as an age range).

Research documentation template

1 min 
Audience Research Documentation template: (1) List all pains and desires your audience experiences. (2) List all relevant features. (3) Define your product advantages. (4) List all problems your product solves. (5) Describe the transformation-the desired outcome and how customer life improves. Keep asking 'what does that mean?' repeatedly until you reach underlying motivations such as belonging, connection, or significance. (6) Add original angle ideas from your research.
  • Before producing any ad creative (the actual ad pieces, such as images and text), research the audience's deepest needs and desires.
  • Then map how the product's features and benefits deliver that outcome.
  • Ads that list generic benefits unrelated to the audience's needs consistently underperform.

Avatar research and buyer groups

2 min
  • Build a separate avatar for each distinct buyer group, because people with different problems, needs and buying behavior respond to different copy.
  • A single category can often be split by experience level, for example beginner, casual and advanced buyers.
  • Write each piece of copy for the avatar that the specific product is built for.
  • Copy aimed at a different group will not appeal.
  • For example, a basic entry-level running shoe should be written for first-time runners, not for marathon trainees.
  • Record the experts, creators and personalities each avatar follows.
  • This supports interest targeting (showing ads to people who like certain topics) on ad networks (ad platforms).
  • People who follow a niche expert usually share that niche's interest.
  • Study the social profiles and content of the experts an avatar follows.
  • Look at what their audience likes, shares and comments on.
  • This shows the language and interests of your customer.
  • Record the online spaces where the avatar spends time, such as Facebook groups, forums, fan pages, Reddit and Quora.
  • Team members can read or join these spaces to learn the pains and desires of the market.
  • They can also use these spaces to find product angles.
  • Record the emotions the customer feels right now, such as frustration, sadness or excitement.
  • Put yourself in their position to choose the tone for your copy.
  • The customer avatar template comes with a filled-in example sheet.
  • Use it to judge how much detail each field needs.
  • Do not copy its content, because your profile must reflect your own market.
  • A buyer of a backyard gazebo may want to host friends and be seen as a good host. So the action is entertaining guests in an outdoor space.
  • Recording this identity and action next to the product lets copy speak to the host's goal instead of only the structure.
  • Ads should be built around the audience.
  • The first question is who the product is being sold to. The message should follow from that audience's view.
  • Custom ads should be written from this audience view.

Desires, emotions and the reason behind them

1 min
  • Pair each desire with the emotional reason behind it, such as fear of being embarrassed, feeling unhygienic or being ashamed of the home. The reason, or "why", gives the copy its tension. A desire without its why makes weaker messages.
  • Sell what the buyer wants, then deliver what they need. Buyers rarely name their needs, but they describe their wants clearly.

Wants and needs sort

1 min
  1. Draw two columns on a sheet of paper. Label one "Wants" and the other "Needs".
  2. List everything the buyer says they want in the first column. List what they actually need in the second.
  3. Write your sales message around the wants. Then deliver the needs as the real solution.
  • A strong ad angle (the main argument an ad makes to the viewer) has three parts:
    • A feeling the buyer has right now, such as present self-blame rather than fear of a future problem.
    • A mechanism (the "how it works" reason) that explains why earlier attempts failed, without blaming the buyer.
    • A stated reason the angle stands apart from competitors.
  • Before you use the feeling, confirm it appears across many survey answers in the buyers' own words. Otherwise it is only the seller's assumption.
  • Competitors often speak to a symptom (the visible problem), such as a child's behavior. An angle about how the buyer privately judges themselves, for example feeling like they failed, can stand apart. This works if buyers repeat that feeling in the survey.

Emotional targeting and proxy buyers

1 min

A proxy buyer is someone who buys for another person, such as a parent buying for a child.

  • Document each emotion's intensity on a 1-10 scale, where 1 is very weak and 10 is very strong.
  • Also state how long the emotion lasts, whether other people share it, and who else it affects.
  • Distinguish shame ("I am bad") from guilt ("I did something wrong"). Each one drives different messages and different proxy-buyer behavior.

High-stakes scenario template (build 7-10 scenarios)

1 min

A scenario is one real-life situation with high stakes, meaning a lot can be lost.

  1. Describe the situation in concrete detail: who is involved and what is at stake.
  2. Name the dominant emotion (the strongest feeling) and its intensity from 1 to 10.
  3. List the secondary emotions (smaller feelings beside the main one) and how they interact.
  4. Name the identity label (how the person sees themselves) that threatens to become permanent.
  5. Describe the attention state (how focused the person is) and what they are missing.
  6. Judge whether the scenario could trigger action, such as a purchase.
  7. Write the mirror-moment reaction: what the person feels or does when they see their own situation reflected.
  8. List the permission and validation (someone telling them it is okay) they need to allow hope.
  9. Describe how the scenario could become a turning point, the moment things start to change.
  10. Attach quotes from real people and source links.

Proxy buyer profile (for each buyer relationship)

1 min
  1. Helplessness: what they cannot fix themselves, and how watching the loved one suffer affects them (5 sourced examples).
  2. Guilt: what they blame themselves for, and what they wish they had done (5 examples).
  3. Protective desperation: how urgently they want to help, and what they would sacrifice (5 examples).
  4. Witnessing grief: which version of the loved one they are losing (5 examples).
  5. Identity impact: what label they carry as a result, such as "bad parent" (5 examples).

  • Write at least 10 full proxy buyer narratives (short stories about one buyer), with links.

  • Each narrative includes the buyer's attention state, emotional entry point (what opens them), emotional sequence and purchase trigger (the feeling that makes them buy).

  • A clearly named target viewer, such as a woman in a specific age range or life stage, makes the viewer feel the ad is for them. The speaker should fit that viewer. Then the ad feels personal, not sent to everyone.

Angle research and angle banks

2 min
  • An angle is a separate layer from the format (the shape of the ad, such as a video or a photo) and the concept (the idea the ad puts across). Do not confuse angle, concept and format when you read a competitor's data.
  • Mark any angle that is backed by avatar data but already saturated (used by so many competitors that it feels worn out). These angles have emotional truth. They need a fresh mechanism or a different position to stand out. Saturation does not kill an angle. It means you need new ideas inside the same feeling.
  • When a niche's main angle is saturated, reframe the same product for a different buyer. For example, a gift bought for a partner instead of a purchase for oneself. Test the new angle as its own ad set (a group of ads tested together) before you drop the product.
  • Compare the buyer breakdown in your ad account (for example by gender) with the people shown in the creative (the ad's image or video). If the real buyers differ from the people in the ad, make new creatives aimed at the real buyer group.
  • Build a unique marketing angle by attacking the problem from different perspectives. The same product can then be marketed to different audiences or platforms. For example, Facebook for one group and TikTok for a younger group with the same problem.
  • Create ad angles that match the problem. One shows the product as protection from the cold. Another shows use with coffee and friends. A third shows the product as a gift. Men also buy it, so keep the audience open to both genders.
  • Marketing angles for zodiac jewelry (jewelry tied to star signs) included:
    • Showing off a star sign.
    • A birthday or holiday gift.
    • Self-expression.
    • Compatibility and connection between signs.
  • A lead-generation quiz (a quiz that collects a buyer's email address) can send each buyer's matches as a one-page PDF (a document file). It can unlock a discount after they finish.
  • Also target a secondary problem named in the product map (your written list of what the product fixes). Examples: convenience of using the product at home, or saving time. Keep both in the same picture.
  • List five ad angles from the collected data. Examples: comfort, sustainability, more confidence, better sleep.
  • Refine each angle with basic buying drivers (the reasons people buy): survival, enjoyment of life, life extension, enjoyment of food and drink, envy, jealousy, curiosity, expression of beauty and style, economy, profit, convenience and efficiency.
  • Pick the most promising angles by checking what customers repeat in Amazon and Reddit reviews.

Angle selection procedure

1 min
  1. List the marketing angles for the product.
  2. Check what customers repeat in Amazon and Reddit reviews. This shows which angle is most likely to land.
  3. Ask an AI chat tool like ChatGPT which audience is more likely to respond. Its data may only go back to 2021, so judge its answer against the review evidence.
  4. Produce one concept per angle, or at least the most promising one if time is short.
  • A video that mentions a product's flaw as a joke or confession can attract many comments and views. Views alone do not show buying intent (a real plan to buy).
  • Test whether the audience actually buys before you scale (spend more on what works) an honesty-style angle. This kind of angle can work well or fail badly, depending on the product.
  • A video with 100,000 views that converts well (leads to sales) can be worth more than one with one million views from an unrelated audience. Fit matters because conversion depends on how well viewers match the product.

Angle sheet and angle build

1 min

An angle sheet is a table with one column per angle. Rows are the parts of the ad.

Terms used in the sheet:

  • Feature: a part or ability of the product.
  • Agitate: make the problem feel more real and painful.
  • Social proof: other people's positive results, such as testimonials.
  • Call to action (CTA): the step you want the viewer to take, such as clicking a discount button.

Building one angle

1 min
  1. Choose the problem type. Write one overview line naming the product, the problem theme and the target customer.
  2. Write the problem or hook: the visible symptom the customer sees first.
  3. Write two to three agitate lines that make the problem concrete. Cover what it does to breathing, cleanliness, time or social life.
  4. Write one or two feeling lines describing how the customer feels because of the problem.
  5. Write the solution in one line. Name the product category and its main claim, its biggest promise.
  6. List the features in the "what it does" row. Each one is a short claim with a visible result.
  7. Write a meaning line for each key feature. Describe what this changes in the customer's home or day.
  8. Write the outcome: the emotional result the customer reaches after using the product.
  9. Write the avoid-negative-outcome row: what happens if the customer does not buy.
  10. Add social proof, such as customer testimonials that match this angle's problem.
  11. Write the call to action with a concrete offer.
  12. Start a new column for each different problem type. Keep columns separate so each one can be tested as its own angle.

Angle patterns for specific audiences

2 min
  • Gift-based angle: frame the product as a gift from a partner or friend, and describe the feeling it creates. The customer then sees a reason to buy for someone else or for themselves.
    • Example for a ring, in italics: My partner surprised me with this ring and it makes me feel seen every time I wear it.
    • Example for a keychain, in italics: My sister gave me this so I always feel safe walking home at night.
  • Cultural or identity-based angle: name a specific group or identity the product speaks to. Show how the product fits their life. This makes the creative more intimate and easier to target.
    • Example: a jewelry piece for couples in a specific culture, described as worn for a special family occasion.
    • Example: a hair brush for owners of large dogs that shed onto beds, shown with a before and after result.
  • Pattern for a niche angle: take a product that a broad audience sees as generic. Name a specific situation where it is used, and let the copy describe that situation. The viewer can picture using it right away, which sells more than a general claim.
    • Example 1: a stainless steel travel mug for early-morning commutes on cold days.
    • Example 2: a desk lamp for late-night study sessions in a shared room.
  • For products that appeal mainly to men, combine the product with a concrete situation where it is used. This narrows the message and helps a weak conversion rate (a low share of viewers who buy).
  • A broad ad for a watch case asks whether you want to keep your watches in a box. It speaks to everyone, so it stands out less. A segment-specific ad (aimed at one group) speaks to a traveler choosing the right watch for each outfit on a trip abroad.
  • Models in an ad should look like the person the viewer wants to become. Viewers are more attracted to the product when the person in the ad matches their aspiration (the person they hope to be).
  • For ads aimed at older audiences, use a younger person speaking to them. Older viewers want to look young and do not identify with ads showing people their own age.

Angle patterns for products

2 min
  • Show the target audience using the product in its real setting, such as a cyclist or snowboarder. Name the specific problem it solves. Avoid generic product shots on plain backgrounds for cold traffic (people who do not know you yet).
  • Use a gifting angle for a product that is a gift. Show it as something a buyer's family member would want. Keep the copy simple and clear about what the product is.
  • Market the same product with different angles, such as a gift for couples, a memorial keepsake (an item that keeps a loved one in mind) or a pregnancy milestone. Different angles keep the product fresh and give you unique content.
  • Same product, different angle: take a product that already sells broadly. Promote it to a specific audience with its own interest or problem. Then brand the product page (give it a name and style) to match.
    • Example: a foldable mat promoted to yoga teachers for studio use.
    • Example: a phone stand promoted to home bakers for recipe videos.
  • Angle for children's toys: position the toy as a way to reduce a child's screen time, by offering a hands-on or educational activity instead.
    • Example of your own: a building kit that replaces tablet time with a 20-minute task.
    • Example of your own: a drawing set pitched as a screen-free weekend activity for kids.
  • Family-benefit angle for educational toys: frame the product around less screen time for children, and shared quality time with parents or grandparents.
    • Example: a wooden building kit that replaces an hour of tablet time.
    • Example: a puzzle set designed for weekend time with grandchildren.
  • When a product has a technology feature, sell it as a classic or traditional look. This keeps the buyer from falling behind on modern function. The formula: name the timeless style the buyer already wants, then add the modern capability they fear missing. It works because it narrows the message to one specific audience. It also ties the product to status (how others see you) and belonging (feeling part of a group).
    • Example 1, in italics: Keep the warm look of a wooden desk lamp without working under dim, outdated light.
    • Example 2, in italics: Carry a classic leather wallet that still works with every new payment app.

Naming angles and concepts

1 min
  • An angle is the argument or approach the ad takes to the viewer. It is separate from the concept and the format.
  • Give each distinct angle a clear name.

Angle-naming prompt

1 min 
Combine the fears and angles discovered in customer research with the gaps between what competitors currently advertise and what the market isn't hearing. For each distinct angle, provide a clear name, state the underlying fear and mechanism in one line, cite the supporting evidence from customer data and market gaps, assess whether competitors are already saturating this angle or whether it remains open territory, and rank by expected strength, with well-supported customer fears that competitors ignore rising to the top.
  • To name what people feel but have not yet said, use verbal creativity (finding fresh, clear words). This is part of introducing a new product to a market that does not yet know it.
  • Renaming an old product can create new market appeal. The South Beach Diet was first called "Modified Carbohydrate Diet" and failed until it was renamed.
  • Apple's iPod was marketed as "1,000 songs in your pocket." It was not the first MP3 player (a music file player).

Angle-choice prompt

1 min 
From the angles you listed, choose the most unique one, meaning one that most competitors in this niche do not use. Explain why it is unique, how often buyers mention the underlying feeling, and which emotion and desired outcome it connects. Keep the answer as a short paragraph.

Planning a new ad (concept development)

1 min
  1. State the problem in plain words, for example that you want to sell this product.
  2. Restate the problem in a different way. For example, you want to introduce the unique aspects of the product to the prospect (a possible buyer).
  3. Restate it again in terms of the buyer's experience. For example, the product should be easy to buy and seem fun.
  4. Choose the restatement that feels closest to the ad you want to write.
  5. List several big ideas for that restatement. Pick the one or two that make the most sense.
  6. Visualize (picture) how the concept fits into the ad. Check it against the restatement.
  7. Stop and incubate: step away and let the idea settle.
  8. Write the headline (the big first line), then the subheadline (the line under it), then the first sentence.
  9. Block-diagram the finished ad, meaning sketch its parts as labeled boxes in order, to check its flow.

Unique selling points and value propositions

1 min

A value proposition is the promise of value a product makes to a buyer. A unique selling point (USP) is what makes your offer different from others.

  • Value propositions can be functional (they solve a practical need, typical for tech or electronics) or emotional (typical for kids' products and beauty products).
  • Define one value proposition per customer archetype (a typical buyer type). Base it on which reason drives the purchase.
  • List the unique selling points of best-selling products, such as manufacturing process, eco-friendly features, press coverage or charity donations. These can serve as credible marketing angles.
  • Compare products by one value-based claim that rivals cannot easily copy. Copied general claims become common and lose value.
  • Look for a unique, specific, verifiable advantage, one you can prove.
  • The USP formula from Rosser Reeves, an advertising expert, has three parts:
    • The product must actually do what the copy says it does.
    • The benefit must be original compared with competitors.
    • The promise must target a core desire the prospect already has. Desire cannot be created, only awakened (brought to the surface).
  • The unique selling proposition in the offer must make the service different. It is usually phrased as "The only service that...". A USP can be created in three ways:
    1. By genuinely unique features, like a proprietary (owned, secret) system or patented technology.
    2. By labeling a common feature as unique, because competitors do not talk about it.
    3. By giving an old thing a new name.
  • Labeling a common feature as unique works by highlighting something every competitor also has but no one mentions. Example: a manufacturer's gold-plated connectors that all rivals also use but never advertised. The reader treats the newly highlighted detail as distinctive.

Models, presenters and identity in creatives

1 min

A creative is the ad's image, video or copy. A presenter is a person who speaks in the ad.

  • Use a wide variety of avatars (buyer types, shown as people in the ad), including different genders, ages and ethnicities, to promote the same product. This variety lets you test which audience responds best.
  • Models in an ad should look like the person the viewer wants to become.
  • A well-known person can be used as an opponent or challenge rather than an endorser (someone who praises the product).
  • A challenge creates a story. An endorsement only adds a name.
  • Build the ad around the contest. Make the product the thing being challenged.
  • Get written permission before publishing any name. An unauthorized use caused a legal fight in the original case.

Copy flow across stages on multi-page stores

1 min

Points in this group are older. They date from 2021 to 2022 and are marked.

  • Long-form sales pages (one long page that sells step by step) make it easier to test different messages.
  • They also help you reach awareness segments (groups of buyers who know different amounts about their problem).
  • Traffic sources (where visitors come from, such as ads or search) can send each awareness segment to a different message.
  • When a store has many product pages, write one copy flow. Do not write separate copy for each stage.
  • Start that flow at Problem Aware, the stage where the buyer knows they have a problem.
  • The flow works for visitors at that stage and at every stage above it, up to purchase. Stages above it mean buyers who know more.
  • Copy that starts at a later stage, such as Product Aware, does not work for visitors at earlier stages.
  • Copy that starts at Unaware (the buyer does not know they have a problem) works for all stages.
  • But Unaware copy is harder to write well.
  • People at Problem Aware or above are easy to target.
  • They already know their pain (the problem that hurts) and what they want to solve.
  • One copy flow that starts with the pain is simpler than writing different copy for each awareness stage.

Copy flow procedure

1 min
  1. Name the customer's pain or problem first. Problem Aware visitors recognise it.
  2. Build the desire for a solution. Show how the solution addresses that problem.
  3. Lead the reader through the options to the point of purchase (the moment they buy), all within the same copy.
  • Example for a surfboard store selling to beginners.
    • Problem Aware: the buyer realises they have no board and cannot surf without one.
    • Solution Aware (the buyer knows a solution exists): choosing between renting and buying.
    • Product Aware: comparing beginner boards by budget, shape, style and brand.
    • Ninja Aware (the buyer knows the exact product and seller): choosing a specific brand, shape and style from a specific seller.
    • Ninja Aware is an extra stage added after Product Aware. It is not one of Schwartz's five stages (the classic five awareness levels from the book Breakthrough Advertising).
  • At the Ninja Aware stage, the solution itself creates the desire.
  • Once a buyer understands how a solution works, they want the products that deliver it.
  • So copy for late-stage buyers (buyers close to purchase) should connect the product to the solution the buyer has already accepted.

Testing, planning and checklists

1 min
  • Ads aimed at product-aware audiences (buyers who know the product) differ from ads aimed at unaware audiences.
  • The framework behind this is covered in the classic book Breakthrough Advertising.
  • Making creatives (ad images and videos) for unaware audiences is one way to grow a product that has already reached its aware market.
  • Ads that run a long time can keep working after a product is obsolete (out of date).
  • This holds as long as the core need (the basic need the product meets) and buyer motivation (the reason they buy) stay the same.
  • Work out the buyer's underlying need so the copy stays valid across product cycles (each new version of a product).

Pre-launch ad checklist

1 min
  1. Does it tap into a desire the audience already has?
  2. Does it show the desirable outcome the customer can reach with the product?
  3. Does it future-pace the viewer (help them picture life after using the product)?
  4. Does it show social proof (evidence that other people already use it)?
  5. Is there an authority signal (a sign of expertise), such as an expert endorsement, where the product category makes one credible? For example, an orthopedic surgeon backing a back-support product.
  6. Does the angle (the specific way the ad frames its message) match the buyer's journey stage (the path from first awareness to purchase)?

Avatar documents

1 min

Write the avatar before any copy. An avatar is a detailed picture of one typical buyer. Use the ten questions below to build it, then check the result against an example persona.

Ten questions about the target buyer, answered in this order:

  1. What keeps them awake at night, or what worries them?
  2. What are they afraid of?
  3. What makes them angry, and who do they blame?
  4. What are their top three daily frustrations?
  5. Which trends are changing their business or life now and later?
  6. What do they secretly want most?
  7. Does the way they make decisions have a built-in bias (a habit in how they think that pushes them one way)?
  8. Do they use their own vocabulary (their own words for things)?
  9. Who else sells something similar to them, and how?
  10. Who has tried to sell them something similar, and why it failed?

Research before writing

1 min
  • Research the buyer before writing any copy.
  • Copy only works if the writer truly understands the buyer.
  • Visualise a typical recipient's whole day (the recipient is the person who gets the letter).
  • Follow their day from arrival at work to how and when their mail reaches them.
  • Note what else takes their attention at that moment.
  • Study the reader before the product.
  • Find the features that match the reader's needs, then build the letter around them.
  • A letter that starts from the buyer's reactions and presents only the matching features is almost always effective.
  • Let the buyer's priorities drive the copy, not the seller's.
  • A sales letter gets no live feedback to correct a misunderstanding.
  • A corporate buyer (a business weighing a donation or purchase) typically asks three things:
    1. What benefit justifies the cost?
    2. Who else already chose this?
    3. Where will the money come from in the budget?

Ad angles and concept building

1 min
  • Define the ad angle before the script.
  • An ad angle is a specific take on a problem for a target audience.
  • Not every product solves a problem.
  • Some products fulfil a need, such as self-expression, identity or a hobby.
  • For these, connect the ad emotionally to the desired feeling, not to a problem.

Lateral thinking exercise for a stuck ad concept (lateral thinking means finding new ideas through unusual links):

  1. Pick a random source of words, such as a shuffled set of words or a dictionary.
  2. Pull out the first three words you see.
  3. Force a connection between those three words and the product you are selling.
  4. Build an ad concept (the main idea of the ad) from the connection, keeping the selling goal in mind.

Awareness stage and funnel messaging

1 min
  • Do not push a purchase to audiences who do not yet know a product category (a type of product) exists.
  • Do not push a purchase to audiences who do not realise they have a problem it solves.
  • Open with the symptom or desire they already feel, such as tiredness.
  • Explain the benefits and present the product as a possible answer.
  • So the first step is awareness, not a sale.
  • A buy-now offer shown to people who are not ready to buy wastes ad spend (the money paid for ads).
  • It also drives away prospects (people who may buy) who would have converted (bought) later.
  • Serve the right message at each funnel stage (the steps from first seeing an ad to buying).
  • This keeps those prospects in the funnel instead of losing them.

On-screen models

1 min
  • Use one consistent on-screen model (the person who appears on camera) across all videos.
  • In competitor videos (videos from rival brands), a consistent model increased sales, especially when the product targeted women.
  • Using her in every video can build recognition.
