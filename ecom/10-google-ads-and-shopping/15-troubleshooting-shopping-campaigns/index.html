Troubleshooting shopping campaigns

Section 10.15 · 15 of 23 in this chapter · 42 min read

In short

  • Spend the full daily budget first. If money does not get spent, you get no usable data.
  • If the budget does not spend, check the bids (the price you offer per click). Most often the real problem is that your products do not show for the words buyers type.
  • Fix the root setting (the main control) before anything else. After any change, wait 2 to 3 days, then 2 to 3 more days before judging results.
  • Only after the budget spends and clicks build up, check the 2.5 sales per 100 qualified clicks target. Then improve the product pages.
  • When sales or clicks suddenly drop, check Change History first and undo the one change that caused it.
More key points
  • If a budget does not spend, the click price is often too low, or cheap products use up the money. Fix the products before raising the price.
  • If you only ship to one country, set the location to Presence, so you don't pay for clicks from countries you can't sell to.
  • Before you judge your ads, check that the store and checkout work.
  • Review 30 days of ad spend, clicks and visits before judging the ad words.
  • Check that your daily budget is actually being spent.
  • Check that people in your target market can see your ads.
  • Check which search words bring sales, then compare with competitors.
  • About 7 in 10 failing Google Ads accounts show one of two patterns.

Diagnostic order and working principles

3 min
  1. Check whether the full daily budget (the most you allow a campaign to spend per day) is being spent. A campaign is one group of ads with its own budget and settings. If it is not spent, continue with the following steps.
  2. Check the CPC (cost per click, what you pay each time someone clicks) against the budget. This rules out bids (the price you offer per click) that are too low.
  3. Check which search terms (the exact words people type into Google) and keywords (the words your ads are set to match) your products appear for. Check whether they match the searches buyers actually make.
  4. Check the bounce rate (the share of visitors who leave right away) and the average time on site (how long visitors stay). This confirms that the traffic reaching the store is the right audience.
  5. Compare the campaign settings with the intended setup. Correct any mismatch.
  6. Apply product page SEO (making each product page easy for Google to find and rank) to all product pages. A product page is the web page for one item. The goal is that products appear for their relevant searches.
  7. Allow 2 to 3 days for Google Merchant Center (Google's tool that holds your product list) to process the changes. Then allow 2 to 3 days of relearning in Google Ads (Google's tool for paid ads), while Google adjusts again.
  8. Once the budget spends and enough clicks have built up, measure the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy). Compare it with 2.5 sales per 100 qualified clicks (clicks from people likely to buy).
  9. Only then start conversion optimization (changing product pages so more visitors buy).
  • The first goal for a campaign is to spend the full daily budget every day, for example $10 per day. Do this before optimizing ads, pages or anything else. A budget that does not spend gives no usable data.
  • When a daily budget does not spend, check the CPC first.
  • If the CPCs are reasonable for the budget, the most likely cause is that products do not appear for the search terms buyers actually use. The bids are probably not too low.
  • Fix the root setting (the main control that drives the others) in the account. Do this before chasing advanced Google Ads courses or granular optimizations (small detail tweaks).
  • When the root setting is corrected, related problems such as low conversions and high bounce rates often resolve too.
  • Check low performance in the ad account first. Look at CTR (click-through rate, the share of people who see an ad and click it), CPC, clicks, conversion rate, and ROAS (return on ad spend, sales divided by ad cost) below 10x.
  • Only after that, investigate competitors on Google Shopping (Google's product ads with photos and prices) for MAP violations (sellers pricing below the price the brand allows). These are not checked by default. (Older advice, 2024, from a source that treats 10x as the ROAS threshold.)
  • Spending on Google Ads with no sales in the first week signals a conversion problem on the store. Fix the store's conversion before raising the budget.
  • A daily budget is not the problem if it is not fully spent. Check that the campaign type and budget setting match the blueprint's Google Shopping setup before changing budgets or bids.
  • The "spend all money" option is a setting that uses the whole budget. Check it as part of that review.
  • If the budget is not being spent, review the campaign for weaknesses that stop spending.
  • Also check that the traffic reaching the store is the right kind of audience.
  • Before blaming the website or the ads for weak conversions, confirm that traffic targeting reaches the right buyer. Targeting means choosing who sees your ads.
  • If the targeting is wrong, the conversion rate stays low no matter how good the site is. So check the audience and search matches first.

Account health, feed and Merchant Center

3 min
  • The product feed (the file of product data that Google reads for Shopping ads) is held in Google Merchant Center. Errors or unclear product data in the feed are a common cause of irrelevant search matches.
  • Changes to product titles and product information only reach Google Ads after Merchant Center has processed them.
  • After you update product information, expect 2 to 3 days for Google Merchant Center to process the changes.
  • After that, expect another 2 to 3 days of relearning in Google Ads.
  • Do not judge the results of a change before this window has passed.
  • If traffic falls and nothing changed in the account, check Merchant Center for product errors or warnings.
  • A drop should not be blamed on seasonality alone (normal yearly ups and downs in sales).
  • An account alert is the question mark or exclamation icon at the top right of Google Ads. It turns red for a verification request (Google asking you to confirm who you are) or an empty account balance (no money left to spend). Check it first.
  • Unapproved products, warnings or missing required fields in Google Merchant Center or Microsoft Merchant Center stop products from showing. Microsoft Merchant Center is Microsoft's version of the same tool.
  • Clean up the feed and resubmit (send it again).
  • Feed and product-page data must be complete before any other change.
  • For a product group (a set of products that share one bid) with no or few clicks, first check the keywords it is shown for.
  • Then check the Merchant Center feed for errors or missing data.
  • If many products show no ads in Google Shopping while competitors' products do, Google may be failing to match yours.
  • Check product page data and Google Merchant Center information so Google can match your products to queries (what people type to search).
  • Missing identifiers are product codes that help Google tell items apart. They limit how often your products show.
  • Fill in SKU or MPN values from the supplier. A SKU is your own stock code. An MPN is the manufacturer's part number.
  • Judge performance only after traffic increases. (Older advice, 2020, from a case of 124 impressions and one click with Merchant Center warnings for missing identifiers.)
  • An impression is one time your ad is shown. If a Shopping product gets almost no impressions even with a high bid, Google may be misreading the product.
  • Check that the title starts with the product name, that the GTIN (a product barcode number) or MPN and brand are correct, and that the product page is optimized.
  • Then search in an incognito window (a private browser window that does not use saved history) to see where the product appears.
  • A product with high bids but almost no impressions may be misidentified by Google.
  • Check whether the product page carries identifiers such as GTIN, SKU or MPN. These help Google match it to the right search results.
  • Compare with competitor pages.
  • If a Google Shopping campaign spends about one cent with no errors, raise the default CPC by 5 cents. The default CPC is the standard bid applied to all products.
  • Then check the result (see the budget section).
  • Also check Merchant Center for product errors and make sure all products show green (no errors).

Zero impressions

4 min

Zero impressions means your ad was never shown.

Procedures

Zero impressions in a Shopping campaign:

  1. Open Google Merchant Center and check that the product feed is all green (no errors). Fix any errors.
  2. In Google Ads, check that products in each product group are approved.
  3. If both are approved, wait a couple of days for the products to enter rotation (the stage where Google starts showing them).
  4. If there are still no impressions, raise the maximum cost per click (the highest bid you allow, also called max CPC) in steps of about 0.05.

Zero impressions after launch:

  1. Confirm the products are approved and eligible (allowed to run) in the Google Ads account.
  2. Check the account for errors.
  3. Raise the maximum cost per click, for example from $0.25 to $0.35, and leave it for a few days.
  4. If impressions stay at zero, contact Google through live chat (typed support chat) and ask them to check for a misconfiguration (a wrong setup).

Zero impressions in a feed-based Shopping campaign:

  1. Open Google Merchant Center and confirm the product feed shows as approved with no errors.
  2. If the feed was approved recently, wait. Feeds can take two to three days before Google starts displaying products.
  3. If several days have passed, check the campaign settings, since a setting usually needs to be fixed.
  4. Try raising the cost per click to generate more volume while you check.

Zero impressions in Alpha or Beta campaigns (undated). Alpha and Beta are two campaign setups used in this method.

  1. Do not just switch the old campaigns back on. Recreate both campaigns from scratch using the standard setup process.
  2. Once submitted, confirm that all products are approved.
  3. If any products show errors, diagnose them in Merchant Center under feed diagnostics (Merchant Center's error report for the feed) and fix them in Symprosis.
  4. Expect impressions, clicks and ideally sales within two to three days after the errors are resolved.

Diagnosing Alpha/Beta shopping campaigns with no clicks or conversions (2024):

  1. Check whether the campaign gets any impressions at all.
  2. Check impression share (the share of possible impressions you actually won). Add it via Columns > Customize columns, the menu path to add a column in Google Ads.
  3. If impressions are very low or zero, the budget is probably not being spent. Slowly increase the target cost per click (the average bid you aim for).
  4. Do not turn back on a general website traffic campaign. Google will send traffic regardless of whether it is qualified to buy.

Zero or near-zero impressions in a Google Ads campaign with no error (older advice, 2021, for a campaign that stops spending after a pause):

  1. Open two windows side by side. One shows the current period since the ads were turned back on. The other shows the same number of days from when they last ran.
  2. Compare impressions and spend per product to find where the drop starts.
  3. If the cause is still unclear, duplicate both campaigns, turn off the old ones, and restart the new ones with a small budget. Then raise the budget step by step again, as before.

Alpha campaign stops getting impressions and clicks:

  1. Step 1: check the change history (the log of account changes) in the sidebar of the Google campaign. See if a change was made on the day it stopped. If you find a harmful change, click revert (undo it).
  2. Step 2: if there was no change, check Merchant Center for errors.
  3. Step 3: check the Google Ads account for other errors or blocks.

Product-level visibility (undated and 2025 sources):

  • Confirm that all items in the Google ad account are approved.
  • If spend is far below budget, raise the default CPC by 5 cents, for example from 30 cents to 35 cents.
  • Wait a few days so Google can relearn the bid. Then check whether spend and impressions increase.
  • Check the negative keywords (words that stop your ad from showing for those searches) for blocked terms.
  • Correct any broad match negatives. Broad match is a loose setting that matches many searches.
  • Google Shopping products appear at the default CPC, which applies to all products.
  • If that bid is too low to win the auction (the contest between sellers for each ad spot), the ads will not show.
  • If the Beta campaign gets no impressions or clicks, check its negative keywords for over-blocking (blocking too much).
  • Also review its cost per click. Beta still brings traffic and sales for general terms, even with campaign priority (the ranking that decides which campaign gets the search first) in place.

Rules and principles

  • Zero impressions after launch signal an error or a missing setup. Low but non-zero impressions are normal early on.
  • Raise the bid before you assume the product is wrong.
  • Zero impressions after two weeks in Alpha or Beta campaigns usually points to an error in the feed submission, the campaign setup, or the negative keyword list.
  • It is usually not a bid that is too low. Even a very low default cost per click should still generate some impressions.
  • A new campaign can take days to be approved.
  • If a toggle (an on/off switch) shows paused, switch it to active.
  • If the campaign is still not approved after a few days, email support or ask in the community group.
  • If Google Shopping results do not appear for you, the search itself may be affected by your browser. (Older advice, 2021. The procedure is listed in the no-clicks section.)
  • If ads get zero clicks no matter how high the cost per click is, the problem is usually missing impressions rather than the bid.
  • Audit the ad account first, meaning check everything in it.
  • If the audit is clean, the brands may lack search demand (people searching for them). Then switch to brands with stronger brand awareness (more people know them).

Impressions without clicks (low CTR)

5 min

Procedures

Diagnosing a Shopping campaign with no clicks:

  1. Check account health. In Google Ads, look at the question mark or exclamation icon in the top right. It turns red when there is an account alert, such as a verification request or an empty account balance.
  2. If there is no alert, check Google Merchant Center for product feed errors, corrupt data, or errors that stop products from being sent.
  3. If the feed is fine, check the campaign budgets. Google recommendations can move budget between campaigns without you meaning it.
  4. Check impressions for the Shopping campaign. Zero impressions points to a bigger problem.
  5. If impressions exist, find the products with the most impressions. Search for them on Google to compare competitor prices, promotions, images and positions.
  6. Check the search terms that trigger your ads. Add irrelevant or overly broad ones as negative keywords.

Checking a Shopping campaign that gets too few clicks (undated):

  1. Review impressions, clicks, cost, average CPC and the benchmark max CPC. The benchmark max CPC is the average CPC of competitors.
  2. If clicks are low while your CPC sits below that benchmark, raise bids toward the benchmark, for example moving toward about $0.91.
  3. If impressions stay low at high bids, search the main terms in Google Shopping, sort by review score, and compare product titles and images with the top competitors.

Optimizing a product with impressions but few clicks (undated):

  • If a product has low impressions while bids are already high, check whether the product is well optimized for the search terms.
  • Search the target term in Google Shopping, sort by review score, and compare product titles, product images and descriptions with the top competitors.
  • If a product gets impressions but no clicks, search for it in Google Shopping and compare its photos, price and promotions with competitors.
  • If it gets almost no impressions, the cause is low visibility. Raise the cost per click for that product.
  • Before changing your store, compare your Shopping listings (your product entries in Google Shopping) with competitors' listings for the same product.
  • Compare photos, prices and promotions. Those differences explain most clicks lost on a product.
  • A large gap between impressions and clicks means something is causing shoppers to choose competitors.
  • Search the same keywords in an incognito window in Google Chrome to see why. Look for a lower price, a MAP violation, a better promotion, a better main image, or a higher ad position.
  • If the account gets no clicks, raising the budget is usually the worst response. Extra spend goes to broad keywords or to competitors violating policies.
  • Diagnose the cause from the account data first.

Product gets impressions with a low search impression share (2024):

  • Low impressions with a low search impression share (your share of the matching searches you could win) mean the product is under-shown.
  • In that case, raise the CPC and optimize the product page, not the daily budget.
  • Raising the budget alone does not fix low impression share.
  • A low click-through rate in Shopping can come from low ranking in the results.
  • Products that match the search loosely appear lower on the page, where shoppers rarely scroll. So the ad gets fewer clicks.

Ad with high impression share but few clicks (2022):

  • When Google Shopping shows high search impression share (90%+) but only a few clicks and low spend despite budget available, the issue is not budget.
  • It is unapproved items or errors in your product feed.
  • Check Merchant Center for unapproved products, warnings or missing required fields. Then clean up the feed and resubmit.

Very cheap clicks or low CTR from the wrong audience:

  • Very cheap clicks with high traffic volume but almost no engagement (visitors doing little on the site) are not a success.
  • Check the campaign settings and the search terms that trigger the ads. The campaign is likely attracting the wrong type of shopper. (Older advice? Undated.)
  • A campaign with under 1,000 impressions per day, a 0.5% CTR and a $33 daily budget per campaign was not spending its budget.
  • The fix starts with the impression data, not with a higher budget. (Older advice, 2018, from a call.)
  • A new Google Shopping campaign for a B2B niche (selling to businesses) produced about 2,000 impressions and only 2 clicks. That is a CTR of about 0.1 percent.
  • The advice was to wait a couple of weeks before deciding anything.
  • But check right away which search terms triggered the ads.
  • Add irrelevant or overly broad search terms as negative keywords. Impressions from unrelated searches are wasted.
  • Once the terms were relevant, the next step was comparing competitor listings in incognito search to improve the click-through rate.
  • 0.1 percent is far below the 1 percent minimum.

Search-result visibility

If Google Shopping results do not appear for you (older advice, 2021, listed twice in the source with identical steps):

  1. Clear your browser cache (the saved page copies your browser keeps).
  2. Enable cookies (small files that let sites remember you).
  3. Search in an incognito window.
  4. Try a VPN (a virtual private network, a tool that changes the location your internet connection appears to come from).

If products are not appearing in Google Shopping:

  1. Open the campaign in Google Ads and check whether products receive impressions.
  2. Do not search for product names on Google Shopping at this step.
  3. If there are no impressions, open Google Merchant Center and confirm every product is approved with no warnings.
  4. Resolve all listed warnings so the products get approved and begin showing.
  5. Only then move on to SEO work.

If a search ad seems not to show for a keyword:

  1. Check whether the ad is actually running. A low-volume warning (Google saying few people search this term) does not always mean the ad is blocked.
  2. Record the search term and check for impressions.
  3. Search the exact term in Google several times, since one search may not show your ad.
  4. If your ad does not appear, broaden the targeted search terms for that product.

Bid level for visibility (2025)

  • To appear in Google Shopping results, bids must be competitive (close to what other sellers pay).
  • If products don't appear when you search for them in Google Shopping, increase bids by 5 cents and monitor for a week to see impressions increase.

Clicks without sales (conversion)

8 min

Benchmarks and checks

  • The working conversion target is 2.5 sales per 100 qualified clicks.
  • Case: 52,000 impressions, 280 clicks, $100 spend over 4 days, 0 sales.
  • At a 2.5% conversion rate, 300 clicks should yield 6-7 sales minimum.
  • It is too early to judge. Wait the 1-5 day conversion window (the time after a click when a sale can still count) before optimizing.
  • A product that gets over 100 unique visitors per month (different people, each counted once) from Google Ads and does not convert is quality traffic that should be converting.
  • Its product page is the first place to look for problems.
  • A store had 2,444 visits and one sale of $599 after 30 days.
  • Its conversion rate was 0.04%, far below the 2.5% target for Google Shopping.
  • Ads were above 1% CTR.
  • Six suppliers had been reduced to 12 advertised products from two of them, because the other four did not offer MAP pricing.
  • The advice was to check the Shopify sales funnel (the steps from visit to purchase in the Shopify store, the store platform).
  • Then separate bot and organic traffic. Bot traffic is visits from automated programs. Organic traffic is free visits from search, not from ads.
  • Then measure the Google Shopping conversion rate on its own.
  • Then review the keyword report and compare the top products with competitors.
  • A store with about 180 visitors per day, $1,200 per month on Google product listing ads, and $50 to $100 per month on Facebook retargeting (showing ads again to people who visited before) made one or two sales per day.
  • But most of those sales were low-priced add-on items (small extras bought alongside another item).
  • The niche was built on high-ticket products (expensive items).
  • The fix was to split the Google Shopping campaigns by price band, then clean up keywords and improve product pages.
  • If Shopping traffic is not converting, first check the keyword triggers (the search words that set off the ad) and the product pages, not the visitor count.
  • About 180 targeted visitors per day should produce one or two sales per day.
  • Unique visitors without purchases point to conversion problems.
  • A Google Shopping campaign produced 233,000 impressions over 45 days, a 0.69 percent click-through rate, and only two sales.
  • A follow-up count showed about 1,300 unique visits, which lowered the click-through rate further.
  • The advice was to raise click-through rate first with negative keywords and campaign segmentation (splitting campaigns into groups).
  • Then check product and cart pages for errors by running a test purchase.
  • A store that had launched a month earlier ran Google ads for four weeks with no sales, 124 impressions and one click. (See the account-health section for the identifier fix.)
  • Spending thousands of dollars on Google Ads without a single conversion typically results from budgets that are too large or campaigns left running without optimization.
  • Price changes can briefly lower click-through rate, especially when a price moves above the price most competitors charge.
  • Make pricing changes when necessary, not constantly, because each change makes Google learn again.
  • Do not raise prices just because conversion is low.
  • First analyze the campaign data, such as impressions, clicks and sales per search term and product, to find the real cause.
  • A sudden drop in conversions with steady traffic usually has one of two causes. New keywords bring lower-quality traffic, or traffic goes to product pages that convert poorly.
  • Compare product-level clicks and sales across two date ranges to show which one it is. (2025.)

Procedures for clicks without sales

Troubleshooting campaigns with no conversions (2024):

  1. Set the date range to your first 3 weeks when sales occurred.
  2. Check alpha and beta campaigns to see which products generated sales.
  3. Review ad spend distribution (how the money is split), impressions, clicks, and CTR in those first 3 weeks.
  4. Compare these metrics to the most recent 5 weeks with no sales.
  5. Identify which products changed in performance.
  6. Check which keywords drove impressions in the successful period versus now.
  7. Look at top-performing competitor product pages to spot positioning or messaging changes.
  8. Add non-converting search terms and competitor terms as negative keywords.
  9. Lower cost-per-click bids on products showing no conversions.

Troubleshooting a product with clicks but no sales (2024):

  1. In Google Ads reporting, check which search terms and keywords triggered the clicks for that product.
  2. If the triggering keywords are irrelevant, add them as negative keywords. If they are relevant, leave them as is.
  3. Search the product name in Google Shopping as a customer. Note the listing and the 1-3 competitor listings.
  4. Compare competitor product pages for features that could drive buying. Then improve your own product page accordingly.

If a Shopping product has no profitable sales yet, optimize before removing it (2024):

  1. Check which search terms and keywords produced the clicks. Add irrelevant ones as negative keywords.
  2. Compare the product page against the top two to three competing stores for that product. Close gaps in content and presentation.
  3. Optimize the product page against the on-page setup of the store.
  4. Let about 100 more clicks run.
  5. If there are still no sales after about 200 to 300 clicks on fully optimized campaigns and pages, lower the CPC sharply or remove the product.
  6. The first roughly 100 clicks are a learning phase.

Investigating weak conversions from ads (2021):

  1. Confirm the site works and that customers can complete a purchase.
  2. Break down Google Ads performance by keyword and see which searches generate impressions and clicks.
  3. Add non-buying keywords as negative keywords.
  4. Lower the default cost per click for the least profitable products, so budget shifts toward products that earn more.
  5. Use the cleaned-up data as the benchmark (the reference point), not a generic industry number.

Auditing an account with high traffic but few sales (2022):

  1. Check which keywords and ads produced the sales.
  2. Find which keywords produced many clicks and few conversions.
  3. Turn off the wasteful campaigns and reduce spend on the low-quality ones.
  4. Focus the budget on the campaigns and keywords that produced the sales.
  • If a site receives many visitors from a small ad budget, check whether the spend reached the right keywords.
  • Many visitors with few sales point to low-quality traffic.

Optimizing a Google Shopping campaign that is not converting after about three weeks (2021):

  1. Review the analytics for keywords generating impressions and clicks, and the product pages those clicks land on.
  2. Add negative keywords for the terms that do not work.
  3. For products with many impressions but no clicks, search Google Shopping for those products and compare your competitor's price, product name, and default image.
  4. Optimize those product pages.
  5. For clicks that come from buyer-intent keywords (words showing the buyer wants to buy, such as product name, brand name, or SKU), optimize the landing product pages (the pages visitors arrive on).
  6. Do not change the budget yet. Move spend to the best-performing places.

Auditing a Google Shopping campaign that spends without sales (2021):

  1. Review the search terms that triggered your ads over the past month.
  2. Add negative keywords for anything too broad or irrelevant.
  3. Check the product groups for impressions and clicks.
  4. For products with many impressions but few clicks, compare images and prices with competitors.
  5. For products with clicks but no sales, compare the product page with competitors in split screen and improve it.
  6. Check for MAP violations.
  7. Lower the budget on products that make no sense, and optimize product pages for those that do.

Side-by-side competitor analysis for a non-converting product (2022):

  1. Open your product page in a small window on the desktop.
  2. Open the same product page on the top two competitor sites.
  3. Compare them from a customer's point of view. Ask why they would buy from the competitor instead of you.
  4. Change your page to make people more likely to choose you.

Non-converting clicks and search terms:

  • If clicks come in but there are no sales, look at the search terms that triggered the clicks.
  • Judge their buying intent, meaning how clearly they show a wish to buy.
  • Add irrelevant broad terms as negative keywords.
  • Increase the cost per click on terms that convert.
  • Very low conversion with clicks on buyer-intent terms: optimize the landing product pages before changing bids.
  • A product with 100 clicks, 80% search impression share and no conversions should first have its store page checked for conversion elements (parts of the page that help a sale, such as buy buttons).
  • If it still does not convert, its max CPC should be lowered so it stops taking budget. (2024.)
  • Raising the max CPC on a product that gets clicks but no conversions wastes budget.
  • Check the store page and conversion elements first, then reduce the CPC if it still does not sell.
  • Spend on a product should be reduced if it gets clicks but no conversions, unless the store page and conversion elements are fixed first.
  • Check abandoned carts (orders started but not finished) in Shopify and call those customers to learn what stopped them.
  • Then audit the ad account to make sure money goes to products that convert.
  • Traffic bouncing with an average session of 0 seconds (a session is one visit) on Google Ads is most likely bot activity from Google. Google checks the store against the Merchant Center feed.
  • Verify by checking which keywords triggered the clicks in Google Ads campaigns.
  • If the clicks come from irrelevant search terms, add negative keywords. (2024.)

Product page checks (conversion)

  • Watch visitor recordings in Lucky Orange (a tool that records how visitors use your site). See where people click, whether they bounce, and whether anything looks broken.
  • Check the bounce rate in Google Analytics (Google's traffic tool).
  • Browse your own store as a buyer. Add an item to the cart and check every step for problems.
  • To review a stalled Alpha campaign for conversions, the store page must be checked first.

Sudden drops and change history

4 min

Procedures

Diagnosing sudden drops in Google Ads performance (older advice, 2021; two versions of the same procedure, merged here). Google Ads is Google's paid ad system. Performance means how well your ads bring clicks and sales.

  1. Go to your campaign in Google Ads. A campaign is one group of ads with one budget and one set of settings.
  2. Click "More" in the left menu, then "Change History". Change History is the log of every edit made in the account.
  3. Review all changes made to that campaign in the date range before performance dropped. The date range is the time period you are looking at.
  4. Identify which change caused the drop.
  5. Revert that change directly through Change History, without rebuilding the campaign. To revert means to undo it.

Checking change history after an unexplained traffic jump (2024):

  1. Open a campaign and set the date range to start at the change window (for example from April or May until today).
  2. Click Change history at the bottom left.
  3. Review every change made in that range.
  4. Revert changes that should not have been applied.

Checking a sudden traffic drop in Google Ads (2023):

  1. Open Google Ads and go to Change history.
  2. Choose All campaigns.
  3. Set the date range from when traffic was normal until today.
  4. Compare the performance graph (the line chart of clicks, sales, or spend over time) with the logged changes.
  5. If a change matches the drop, click Undo on it.

Troubleshooting a drop in Google Ads conversions (2025). A conversion is a sale or another goal you track, such as a purchase.

  1. In Google Ads, open the alpha and beta Shopping campaigns. Alpha and beta are the names of two campaigns. A Shopping campaign shows your products with photos and prices in Google.
  2. Set the date range in the top right to a period when sales were strong.
  3. Note which products received clicks and which products sold.
  4. Change the date range to the recent period when traffic is still coming in but sales have dropped.
  5. Compare which products received impressions and clicks then versus now. An impression is one time your ad was shown.
  6. Adjust bids so traffic returns to the product pages that converted before. A bid is the amount you offer for one click.

Diagnosing a performance drop in an underperforming alpha campaign (older advice, 2021, from the same source as the review procedure below):

  1. Review the change history for every modification made during the period before performance dropped.
  2. Identify negative changes.
  3. Revert problematic changes directly through Change History without rebuilding campaigns.

Rules and principles

  • Fix the root setting first. The root setting is the one change that caused the problem.
  • When an account changes sharply, a rebuild is less useful than finding the change. A rebuild means starting the campaign over from scratch. Advanced optimization is also less useful.
  • Pause the existing campaigns while you investigate a suspected settings problem. This stops them from spending on unqualified traffic, which means clicks from people unlikely to buy. (2025.)
  • Recreate the Alpha and Beta campaigns step by step, following the Google Shopping setup steps. Check each setting as you go. (2025.)
  • Confirm that each setting matches the reference, including location targeting. Location targeting is the list of places where your ads may show. (2025.)
  • If the same pattern of many clicks and little return appears in the first four to five days after launch, do not wait out the full learning period. The learning period is the time Google uses to learn who to show your ads to. Look for the root cause instead. (2025.)
  • A traffic jump caused by a change: Google's Invalid Click Analysis (Google's report of bad clicks) and third-party click fraud tools (outside tools that spot fake clicks) differ in accuracy. Click fraud means fake clicks made to waste ad money.
  • A monthly invalid click share of about 10 percent after a sudden traffic jump calls for an account-change audit first. An audit is a full check of the account. (2024.)
  • Avoid auto-applied recommendations from Google's AI suggestion panel. Also avoid calls from Google "ad specialists", as they tend to increase spend. (2024.)
  • If a campaign's ROAS (return on ad spend: sales earned for each dollar spent) falls while spend rises, that does not prove the country is maxed out. Maxed out means there are no more buyers left to reach.
  • First fix negative keywords. A negative keyword is a word that stops your ad from showing for that search.
  • Next, split campaigns into separate groups so your best sellers get their own budget.
  • Before you decide the budget is used up, improve conversion. Conversion means turning visitors into buyers.

Budget not spending and bid levels

5 min

Why the budget does not spend

Terms used here: the daily budget is the most you spend per day. CPC (cost per click) is what you pay each time someone clicks your ad. The CPC bid is the click price you offer.

  • Two budget factors must be checked together: the daily budget amount and the cost-per-click (CPC) bid.
  • A large daily budget with a very low CPC bid spends far less than the budget allows. The reason is that available traffic at that price is limited.
  • Example: if the daily budget is $1 million USD but the CPC bid is $0.001, the campaign only spends approximately $100 per day. The reason is that sufficient search traffic does not exist at that price, regardless of budget size.
  • A low CPC with a high daily budget will not spend, because the bid is below market competition. Market competition means what other sellers are paying.
  • Raising the CPC lets the full daily budget spend.
  • For example, a $10 daily budget with $0.01 CPC spends $0. Raising CPC to $0.15 lets the full $10 spend.
  • Cut ad spend for products that are typically backordered (out of stock with a delay) for about three weeks or more.
  • Move the budget to products that can ship.

Procedure for underspending

  1. Verify the daily budget amount is an insufficient reason.
  2. Increase the cost-per-click bid in small steps (the "inch up" method).
  3. Monitor spend to confirm the budget now runs out.
  4. Simultaneously implement negative keywords to improve relevance and CTR (click-through rate: the share of people who see the ad and click) of the remaining spend.

Procedure for a budget that is not fully spent

  1. Check whether the campaign is spending its full daily budget.
  2. If not, raise the cost per click for the product or campaign in steps. For example, from 25 cents to 40 cents, then to 60 cents, until traffic rises.
  3. If the full budget is spent but clicks are too few, increase the daily budget or lower the target cost per click. The target cost per click is the click price Google aims for.

Procedure for a campaign that spends about one cent

  • Raise the default CPC by 5 cents, wait a few days, and check the result. The default CPC is the click price used when no separate price is set.
  • If spend still does not change, raise it another 5 cents and repeat.

Optimization order for an underperforming Shopping campaign (undated, a $10 daily budget case)

  1. Exclude all products under $200 from campaigns. You can do this with the product inclusions and exclusions in your feed app (a tool that sends your product list to Google). You can also set their max CPC to $0 or $0.01 in the product groups. Max CPC is the highest click price you allow. A product group is a set of products that share one price.
  2. Optimize the product page title and meta tags with better keywords. Meta tags are hidden page text that describes the page to search engines. This is the second priority and takes less urgency than step 1.
  3. Keep the ads running for two weeks with the optimized products and the cheap products excluded.
  4. During those two weeks, do not change the max CPC.
  5. If the $10 daily budget still does not spend after two weeks, raise max CPC from $0.50 to $0.55 and let it run for one week.
  6. If the budget still does not spend, raise max CPC to $0.60 and let it run for one week.
  7. If the budget still does not spend, raise max CPC to $0.65. Keep increasing slowly until the budget spends at a profitable level. Profitable means sales earn more than they cost.
  • Raising max CPC is a later lever, meaning an option to use after the others. When the product mix sends budget to low-price items, a higher bid only buys more unprofitable clicks.
  • Fix which products are being advertised before increasing bids.
  • Raising max CPC early, while cheap products are still in the campaign, spends more money on sales that cannot reach the target ROAS.
  • This is the common error when a campaign seems to underspend and the store owner assumes the bid is the problem.
  • Case: a store ran Google Shopping campaigns for three months, named alpha and beta. It had a $10 daily budget that never spent in full.
  • Results: 3 sales in total, 2 from alpha and 1 from beta. Spend was alpha $152, beta $54, total $206, about $69 per month.
  • Cost per sale (what one sale cost in ads) was $74 for alpha and $52 for beta.
  • The store owner had stopped raising max CPC at $0.50 and was adding weekly negative keywords.
  • The sold products were priced at $67, $197 and $247, average about $170. That is far below the $740 needed for alpha's cost per sale, and the $520 needed for beta's cost per sale, to reach a 10x ROAS. A 10x ROAS means $10 of sales for each $1 spent.
  • Recommended actions: exclude all products under $200. Then optimize titles and meta tags second.
  • Next, run for two weeks with cheap products excluded. Then raise max CPC gradually from $0.50 to $0.55, $0.60 and $0.65, one week each, until the budget spends.
  • The source does not report the final outcome.
  • Raising the budget alone does not fix low impression share. Impression share is the share of possible views your ads actually got.
  • Instead, raise the CPC and optimize the product page.
  • Do not change the budget yet while many campaigns are still learning.
  • Move spend to the best-performing places.
  • Spend stop on a specific supplier: go to the Alpha and Beta campaigns and find all products from that supplier. (Undated.)
  • Set their Cost per click (CPC) to 0 cents. Google then does not spend money on them, so they stop showing. (Undated.)

Irrelevant traffic and search terms

2 min
  • If unwanted search terms appear in the medium or low priority campaigns, the cause is one of two things. Either the high priority campaign is not working correctly, or its budget is "Limited by budget". That status means the budget runs out too early.
  • Fix the budget status first. Then recheck the setup for negative keywords that were not applied across all campaigns.
  • When the funnel (the path from ad to sale) is set up correctly, the negative keyword strategy is close to foolproof.
  • Failures almost always trace back to setup errors.
  • Recheck the list and the setup before blaming the system.
  • Check the search terms that trigger your ads. Search terms are the words people type into Google.
  • Add irrelevant or overly broad ones as negative keywords.
  • To optimize Shopping campaigns, open the Google Ads account and find the keywords that triggered impressions or clicks.
  • Then check which product pages those searches landed on.
  • Optimize those product pages first, because they already receive traffic. (2025.)
  • Check your ad location settings.
  • If traffic from unwanted countries appears, select only the United States (or only the intended country) in campaign settings. This stops paid traffic from there.
  • Add broad or irrelevant terms that take most of the spend and bring visitors who do not buy as negative keywords. Broad terms are very general search words.
  • Find the keywords and products that generate sales. Move them into a separate campaign with its own higher budget.
  • Build a layered campaign structure, meaning campaigns stacked in levels by priority.
  • Do not spend on products that are sold below MAP by others. MAP (minimum advertised price) is the lowest price a brand allows sellers to show publicly. (2024.)
  • Monitor the Google product listings as well. They overlap heavily with the ad auction, the instant contest Google runs for each ad slot. (2024.)
  • A campaign gets clicks from a non-English search term when you serve only the US market. Check Locations.
  • Set it to "United States Only" rather than "All territories and countries."

Negative keyword rules

  • Negative keywords for irrelevant searches: a shopper searching for a brown leather chair when you sell grey fabric sectionals (sofas made of connected pieces) is wasted spend. Add such terms as negative keywords.
  • Add non-converting search terms as negative keywords. Non-converting means they bring no sales.
  • Add competitor terms as negative keywords.
  • If you see many clicks from non-buying keywords, check the negative keyword list for over-blocking. Over-blocking means blocking too many searches.
  • Correct broad match negatives. Broad match is a setting that matches loosely related searches.

Location and wrong-country traffic

2 min

Google changed the default location target for Shopping, Search and Display campaigns to "Presence or interest". The two options conflict in effect:

  • Google default: "Presence or interest" reaches people who live in, regularly are in, or have shown interest in the targeted locations. This suits local businesses reaching commuters.
  • Recommended for a national advertiser that ships to one country: "Presence" (reaches people in or regularly in the targeted locations).
Option Who it reaches Typical use
Presence or interest People in, regularly in, or who have shown interest in the targeted locations Local businesses that want to reach commuters into a city
Presence People in or regularly in the targeted locations National advertisers limited to one country (recommended)
Search interest People searching for the targeted locations Not recommended for national sales
  • For a national advertiser that ships to one country only, paid clicks from shoppers who cannot buy are waste. So set the location target to Presence.
  • Open the campaign, go to Settings > Locations, and expand Location options.
  • Set Target to Presence. Under Exclude, keep Presence (recommended).
  • "Presence or interest" exists for local advertisers who want to capture people who live outside a city but commute into it.
  • A business that ships nationally has no use for that reach, so it should not keep it.

Procedure for every campaign

  1. Open every campaign in the account, including Search, Shopping and Display.
  2. Go to Settings > Locations for each campaign.
  3. Expand Location options.
  4. Set Target to Presence.
  5. Set Exclude to Presence (recommended).
  6. Save, and repeat for every new campaign you create.
  • Google changed this setting retroactively, meaning it also changed campaigns built years earlier. It does not send any notice.
  • Check every existing campaign, including Search text campaigns. Do not assume an older account is unaffected.
  • Shopping campaigns targeted to one country can receive clicks from abroad, such as Europe or Asia, when the location setting is "Presence or interest".
  • In one Australian account, this traffic reached a volume where paid clicks were costing real money.
  • Case: a US-based Shopping account targeted US states and showed clicks from countries it does not ship to.
  • The location target was "Presence or interest", which Google applied by default to campaigns created before the setting existed.
  • Every campaign was switched to "Presence" for the target and "Presence (recommended)" for exclusions.
  • No further clicks from non-sale countries were expected, and wasted ad spend stopped, though the exact figure was not measured.
  • The same default change also appears in Display network campaigns (ads on partner websites and apps), most often there.
  • Check the location target on Display campaigns as well as Search and Shopping campaigns.
  • Shopping campaign settings show a separate "Country of sale" field. In the example it is set to United States, and it sits above the location targeting.
  • Confirm that Country of sale matches the one country you ship to. Then set the location target under Settings > Locations.

Supplier, MAP and stock problems

2 min

Procedures

Handling MAP violations in ads (2024). A MAP violation means a seller shows a price below the minimum advertised price.

  1. Pause the ads of SKUs that are sold below MAP. A SKU (stock keeping unit) is one product code.
  2. Resume them when the issue is fixed.
  3. Find the repeat offenders among suppliers or competitors.
  4. Report the supplier if it does not enforce its MAP policy.
  5. Move repeat offenders into separate campaigns with lower bids, or keep small budgets if the competitor has bad reviews.

Protecting margins when a competitor sells a supplier's product far below MAP (2026). Margin is the profit you keep from each sale.

  1. Exclude that supplier's products from your Google Shopping campaigns right away, so no budget goes to them.
  2. Lower the daily budget, for example from $40 to $20, while you work toward the first sale and Google learns.
  3. Re-add the supplier once prices return to normal.
  • Also tell the supplier, since a MAP violation can be serious for them.

Principles and cases

  • Pick the root cause of recurring MAP violations (one supplier or a few competitors) and remove it. Do not monitor endlessly. (2024.)
  • Competitors with bad reviews can still send you customers, so a small ad budget can stay on. (2024.)
  • Case: a store ran Google Shopping ads for two weeks with alpha and beta campaigns.
  • Results: $330 for 34,000 impressions, 430 clicks, a 1.29% CTR, and an average CPC of $0.77. Bids were raised to $1.00 for alpha and $0.50 for beta.
  • The average product price was $1,800, and there were no sales.
  • A competitor sold the same supplier's products at 40% off.
  • The recommended response was to exclude that supplier from ads.
  • Also lower the daily budget to $20, and re-add the supplier once prices normalized. (2026.)
  • A store had been running Google Ads for about three weeks. It had a priority campaign for 300 to 400 top products on a shared budget of $40 (one budget used by several campaigns).
  • It also had a separate secondary campaign with a max CPC of $3.
  • After 3 weeks it had spent about $189, received about 13,000 impressions and 583 clicks, and no sales.
  • The advice was to clean up irrelevant search terms first, leave bids and budgets unchanged for the moment, and add negatives before building Meta remarketing. Meta remarketing means showing ads on Facebook or Instagram to people who visited before.
  • The outcome was not reported. (2025.)
  • Low performance in Google Ads should be checked in the ad account first.
  • Only after that, investigate competitors on Google Shopping for MAP violations. These are not checked by default. (2024.)

Tracking and site checks

1 min
  • Confirm the site works and that customers can complete a purchase before judging ads.
  • Check the bounce rate in Google Analytics. Bounce rate is the share of visitors who leave after one page. Google Analytics is Google's free visitor-tracking tool.
  • Also check whether anything looks broken on the store.
  • Run a test purchase to check every step of the product and cart pages for errors.
  • The product should be checked in Google Shopping for identifiers (codes that identify a product, such as brand or part numbers) that match competitor pages.
  • Check the store with the ads. Site and checkout must work before paid traffic is judged.

Product-level decisions

1 min
  • If a product gets no impressions, raise its CPC first so it can get impressions. (2023.)
  • If it gets many clicks but no sales, first optimize the product page to lift the conversion rate. The conversion rate is the share of visitors who buy. (2023.)
  • If the page is well optimized and the product still is not profitable, decide between two options. Either remove it from paid campaigns, or keep it with a lower CPC and no active focus. (2023.)
  • Use the cleaned-up data as the benchmark for the least profitable products. A benchmark is a standard to compare against.
  • This moves budget toward products that earn more.
  • If many products do not show ads while competitors do, check product page data and Merchant Center information first. Merchant Center is Google's product data hub.
  • See the account-health section for more on this.
  • Products with a 1% CTR or above that still do not sell need a store check before any budget change.
  • Reason: a store can carry sound ad metrics while conversion is weak.

Procedures for common questions (stepwise)

Google Shopping campaign with no clicks, in order (see the no-clicks section for steps).

Raising CPC, the order of checks

  1. Confirm all products are approved.
  2. Raise the default CPC by 5 cents if spend is far below budget.
  3. Wait a few days for relearning.
  4. Check negative keywords for blocked terms.

Diagnostic Order

1 min
  1. Review the data before judging the ads.
  2. Review the campaign setup (how the campaign is built) and the strategy behind it.
  3. Check whether the budget is being spent.
  4. Check visibility (whether your ads can be seen) and location.
  5. Check search terms and competitors.
  6. Identify the underperformance pattern (the way the account is doing worse than expected).

Step 1: Review the Data

1 min
  • When someone asks how to improve their ads, first review 30 days of spend (money paid for ads), clicks and traffic (visits to the website) for each platform (an ad service, such as Google) before judging the ad copy (the words in the ad).
  • If you cannot access the account (log in to the ad service), screenshots (pictures of the screen) of spend, click counts, bounce rate (the share of visitors who leave after one page) and average time on site (how long visitors stay on average) are enough for a first diagnosis (finding the cause of the problem).

Step 2: Review the Campaign Setup

1 min
  • If Google Ads (Google's paid ad tool) campaigns underperform (do worse than they should), review the campaign setup from scratch and the underlying strategy (the plan behind it) is a sensible first step. **
  • The reason: platform changes may have altered how things work since the original setup. **

Step 3: Check Budget Delivery

1 min
  • Check first whether the daily budget (the most you let a campaign spend per day) is actually being spent before changing anything else.
  • Example discussed: a $33 daily budget with 1.3% CTR (click-through rate: the share of viewers who click the ad) and 50% impression share (the part of possible ad showings that your ad actually got; one showing is an impression) on a campaign called "alpha". **

1 min
