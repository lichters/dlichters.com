Playbook › 10. Google Ads and Shopping

Campaign segmentation, tiers and scaling structure

Section 10.13 · 13 of 23 in this chapter · 49 min read

In short

  • Start with one Alpha and one Beta campaign that hold all products. Split them only when your real numbers show a difference.
  • Use custom labels (tags you add to each product) to group products. Google reads these tags to sort your products.
  • After you change labels, wait a few hours before you edit campaigns. Google is slow to show new labels.
  • Exclude products that get no sales or make customers unhappy.
  • Never put one product in two segments. It splits your budget and makes your numbers wrong.
More key points
  • Give a brand that makes most of your sales its own campaign, but wait about a month before you decide.
  • Never keep the same product in two campaigns that compete with each other.
  • Show ads only where shipping still leaves you a profit.
  • Use one country for each campaign.
  • Stop advertising products that are out of stock for a long time.
  • If a supplier keeps breaking the minimum price rule after you follow up, lower that product's cost per click to one cent.
  • Raise budgets in small steps of 10 to 30 percent. A big jump can ruin your return for a month.
  • Repair a running campaign first. Rebuild it only if nothing improves after the repairs.
  • Pause products that are out of stock for a long time. Don't keep paying for clicks you can't sell.
  • Start simple. Split your campaigns into smaller groups only when the sales numbers show which products sell.

Scope and Sequencing of Segmentation

2 min
  • Start with one Alpha and one Beta campaign that hold all products. Alpha and Beta are two named campaigns for the same products. Alpha is the main one. Split them only when your data shows differences in margin (the profit left after costs), supplier (the company that ships the product), shipping region or brand.
  • Split on what the data shows, not on a fixed template. Data means your real sales and cost numbers.
  • Segment by performance. Products and search terms (the words people type into Google) have different CTRs (click-through rate: the share of people who see an ad and click it) and conversion rates (the share of clicks that buy). Bid more on the segments that convert well, and less on the weaker ones. A bid is the most you pay for one click.
  • Segment at campaign level only when products have very different performance profiles or margins. Keep related variations in the same campaign pair, and divide them further with product groups by brand and SKU. A product group is a set of products inside a campaign. A SKU (stock keeping unit) is a unique product code.
  • Products in different categories get separate Alpha-Beta pairs. Related variations, such as gas and electric lawnmowers, stay in the same pair.
  • Use a waterfall structure for product groups. A waterfall is a top-down tree, from broad to narrow: all products → product type → brand → individual SKU. Segment down to SKU for best optimisation (fine-tuning results). But do not create so many campaigns that performance cannot be managed.
  • Google does not reward campaign count directly. A structure that is easy to manage gets managed more often and to a higher standard. That tends to improve profitability.
  • A store with 500 or more products needs organisation inside Shopping campaigns. Without it, finding one product to change its bid or add negatives takes too long. Slow management leads to less management. Negatives (negative keywords) are search words your ads must not match.
  • The profitable limit of Shopping traffic is reached when extra clicks can no longer be bought at a cost that meets your target cost per sale and target ROAS. Target cost per sale is the highest cost you accept to make one sale. ROAS (return on ad spend) is the revenue you earn for each dollar spent on ads. The limit is not reached just because the pool of searches runs out. Narrow niches (small market focus areas) and small markets reach this limit at lower revenue.
  • Advanced strategies beyond Shopping Ads apply to stores doing $50,000-$100,000+ per month that have reached the scaling ceiling (the point where growth stops) at a 10× return on ad spend with Shopping campaigns.
  • Keep priority products in a separate campaign from secondary products. Then campaigns do not bid against each other for the same searches. Check for overlapping products whenever you restructure campaigns.
  • Do not place one product in more than one segment. Overlap splits your budget (the money a campaign may spend) and distorts data. Distorted data means the numbers are wrong.

Campaign Types and Core Structure Rules

2 min
  • Campaign type: When you create segmented Shopping campaigns in Google Ads (Google's advertising system), select Standard Shopping, not Performance Max. Performance Max is Google's fully automatic campaign type. Standard gives manual control over product groups and segmentation exclusions.
  • Core bidding setup: Keep profitable manual Shopping campaigns as the core. Manual means you set the bids yourself. When they produce data and profit, raise budgets step by step. Do not switch to Smart campaigns (Google's fully automated campaigns).
  • Tier structure: Campaigns can be split into tiers (levels), for example beta, alpha 1 and alpha 2. Universal negative keywords sit at campaign level, so they apply to the whole campaign. Brand or product negatives funnel traffic (steer visitors) toward the tightest tier, the most focused level.
  • Alpha 2 and Beta 2: A typical Shopping structure has a main Alpha and a Beta campaign holding product groups. Duplicating means making a copy. Duplicating them creates Alpha 2 and Beta 2. These separate lower-priced products from the main campaigns.
  • Separate budgets per pair: Create separate Alpha and Beta campaigns, each with its own budget and its own negative keyword lists.
  • Isolating a product: To test one product on its own, create a new Alpha and Beta pair used only for that product.
  • No shared overlap: Do not share one budget across three campaigns: all-products Alpha, all-products Beta, and single-product Alpha/Beta. This prevents proper bid optimisation (tuning bids for the best result).
  • Shared budget per tier: Several campaigns can run on one shared daily budget. Assign shared budgets to each tier based on your profit goals. Do not let the same products sit in overlapping campaigns.
  • Campaign per category: Split product categories into separate campaigns, not only separate ad groups. An ad group is a smaller section inside a campaign. This lets you set budgets per product or product type. It also lets you put more money behind products with high ROAS, for example $1 in and $20 back.
  • Subcategories: When your niche widens to several product types, create separate Alpha-Beta pairs for each subcategory. For example, one paddle board pair and one skateboard pair. Keep the existing structure if it already meets your return target. Splitting is better practice, but it is not urgent.
  • Add-on products: Add-on products are extra items sold with the main product. Add-ons with different keywords run in separate campaigns, for example an Alpha add-on and a Beta add-on. This way the main campaign data is not distorted.
  • Staged build-up: Start with all products in one Alpha and one Beta campaign. Optimise with negative keywords and product-level CPC (CPC is the cost per click, the bid for one click on one product). When a few products perform very well, create Alpha 2 and Beta 2 with only those products. Exclude them from the original campaign, and raise the budget on the new campaign.
  • Exclude losers: A product is a loser when it gets no sales, or when customers are unhappy with it. For example, it may come from a bronze-level supplier. Exclude such products from all campaigns. Excluded products cannot appear or spend budget anywhere. The budget then moves to winning brands.

Custom Labels: What They Are and How to Plan Them

2 min
  • Google Shopping (product ads with pictures and prices on Google) supports five custom labels: Custom Label 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4. A custom label is a tag you write on each product so you can group it. Each label is a separate column in the Shopify product CSV. Shopify is the online store software you use. A CSV is a spreadsheet file. In Google it appears as "Google Shopping / Custom Label 0" through "Custom Label 4". Numbering starts at zero, which gives five slots.
  • Labels act like tags on each product, and campaigns filter on them. Each product can carry up to five labels.
  • Custom labels are the more reliable segmentation method. Google's built-in segmentation options in Google Ads are glitchy, hard to understand, and often miss products in the feed. The feed is the product list you send to Google. **
  • Custom labels are a flexible way to split Shopping campaigns by brand, product title, product type or price. A product type label goes into any free (empty) custom label column in the same way. **
  • Common setup: brand in one label, product title in another, and price or product type in the remaining labels if needed. **
  • Decide the attributes before exporting. Export means downloading your product list from Shopify. Put each chosen attribute in its own custom label column. For example, vendor (the brand or supplier name field in Shopify) in Custom Label 0 and product title in Custom Label 1. **
  • Label 0 for margin vs. brand: Use Custom Label 0 for your first profit-margin segmentation. Dropshipping means selling products that a supplier ships for you, without storing them yourself. More advanced segmentation with additional label slots exists, but it is rarely necessary for typical dropshipping stores. Where brand is the main split, Source B places vendor in Custom Label 0 instead. ** Use the margin approach when profit tiers are your first split. Use the brand approach when brand is. You can combine both by using different label slots.
  • Putting the product title into a label lets you find individual products quickly within a Shopping campaign. Brand or vendor in one label and title in another gives you both a campaign-level view (the whole campaign) and a product-level view (one product). **
  • Once a title label exists, product group lists can show product titles instead of raw IDs. A raw ID is the system's code for each product, such as "shopify_us_" followed by two long numbers. A filter then finds one product quickly. Without titles, IDs are hard to read, and the hover tooltip (the small box that pops up when you move your mouse over an item) is cut off. **
  • Product names in the Item ID subdivision come from feed titles. A subdivision is a split of a product group into smaller groups. Clean up unclear or code-like titles, so results per product are easier to read and act on.

Timing: Pre-Labelling and Publication Delays

1 min
  • Apply custom labels to all products in the profitability tiers you plan to use, before creating segmented campaigns. Google needs about four hours or longer to publish labels to Ads after bulk edits (changing many products at once). Pre-labelling (adding labels before you need them) avoids waiting in the middle of your work.
  • Merchant Center (Google's product data hub) usually needs about two to three hours to show new custom labels. For large feeds it can take up to eight hours. **
  • Custom labels cannot be used in Google Ads campaign subdivisions until Merchant Center has recognised them. If you create or edit subdivisions before that, you see old labels or no labels. **
  • After the app sync shows as finished, wait a few hours or until the next day. Then edit subdivisions or create new campaigns with custom labels. Labels not yet in Merchant Center will not appear in the Google Ads interface (the screens you work in). **

Custom Labels: CSV Procedure (Shopify Export and Import)

3 min

Procedure A: Adding custom labels to all Shopify products via CSV **

  1. In the Shopify admin (the back office of your store), go to Products > All products.
  2. Click Export, select all products and click Export products.
  3. Shopify sends a download link by email when the file has 100 or more products. Smaller files may download directly. Download the CSV once it is ready.
  4. Open the CSV in a spreadsheet program (Numbers, Excel or similar).
  5. Scroll to the Google Shopping custom label columns (Google Shopping / Custom Label 0 to 4).
  6. Copy the source column (e.g. Vendor for Custom Label 0) and paste it into the matching custom label column for every product.
  7. Copy the Title column and paste it into Custom Label 1 (or your other chosen label).
  8. Rename each pasted column header back exactly to 'Google Shopping / Custom Label 0' (or the matching label). Without the exact header, the upload fails.
  9. Save or export the spreadsheet as CSV.
  10. In Shopify, go to Products > Import, choose the CSV, tick 'Overwrite any current products that have the same handle', and click Upload and continue. A handle is the unique name Shopify uses in a product's web address.
  11. Review the preview and click Import products. Large files can take a few minutes up to about half an hour. Shopify sends an email when the import is complete.
  12. In the Feed for Google Shopping app (a Shopify add-on that sends your product data to Google), go to Settings > Sync Settings From Shopify.
  13. Select 'Manually sync meta fields' (Re-sync meta fields). Meta fields are extra product details, such as custom labels. Tick Custom Labels, and click Yes, sync from Shopify. Sync means copying the data across.
  14. Keep the window open until the app reports that syncing has finished.
  15. Wait for Google Merchant Center to pick up the values. Then check the campaign settings in Google Ads.

Procedure B: Detailed version of the same CSV method **

  1. In the Shopify admin, go to Products and open All products.
  2. Click Export, select all products and choose Export products.
  3. Wait for the file. Stores with 100 or more products usually get an email with a download link when it is ready. Very small catalogues may download straight away.
  4. Open the CSV in a spreadsheet program such as Excel.
  5. Scroll right to the Google Shopping / Custom Label 0 to Custom Label 4 columns.
  6. Decide what each label will hold, for example brand in Custom Label 0 and product title in Custom Label 1.
  7. Copy the source column (for example Vendor for brand, or Title for product title) and paste it into the chosen label column for all products.
  8. Rename the pasted column header back to exactly Google Shopping / Custom Label 0 (or 1, 2, 3, 4 as applicable). If the header is not restored, the file will not upload properly.
  9. Repeat steps 6 to 8 for every label you use.
  10. Export the spreadsheet as CSV. The file name can be anything.
  11. In Shopify, go to Products and click Import, then add the CSV file.
  12. Tick the option to overwrite existing products that have the same handle, then click Upload and continue.
  13. Check the preview and click Import products. Large files can take several minutes to about half an hour. Shopify sends an email when the import is complete.
  14. Open the Feed for Google Shopping app and go to Settings, then Sync Settings From Shopify.
  15. Press RE-SYNC META FIELDS (Manually Sync Meta Fields). Confirm that Custom Labels is ticked, and choose to sync from Shopify. Keep the window open until the app reports that syncing has finished. Closing it can leave the sync incomplete.
  16. Wait for Google Merchant Center to pick up the labels. This usually takes about 2 to 3 hours and can take up to 8 hours, depending on feed size.
  17. Only after that, check the labels in Google Ads and build campaign subdivisions. Waiting a few hours or until the next day before editing segmentation is recommended.

App menu path (Feed for Google Shopping) **

  • Settings > Sync Settings From Shopify > Manually Sync Meta Fields > RE-SYNC META FIELDS.
  • In the pop-up "Please choose fields which needs to resync", tick Custom Labels. Select All is possible. Then click YES, SYNC FROM SHOPIFY.
  • Keep the page open until the status shows finished.

Custom Labels: Common Mistakes

1 min
  • Wrong source column: Pasting the wrong source column, such as product title where the brand belongs, sends wrong values to Google. Check the first rows of each custom label column before you export the CSV. **
  • Header check before pasting: Check the copied header before you paste. If a wrong column was copied (for example vendor instead of product title), remove it and copy again. Afterwards, set the header back to the exact custom label name, or the upload fails. **
  • Header left as copied name: If the header is left as the copied column name, the CSV upload will not work properly. Change the header back to the exact custom label name, for example 'Google Shopping / Custom Label 0'. **
  • Overwrite option: A CSV import overwrites every existing product with the same handle. Tick the overwrite option deliberately, and understand it before you upload. **

Example: Brand Group with Product Types

1 min
  • For a brand with three product types, name the ad group after the brand. It holds three product groups, one for each type. Opening the group for one type shows only the products of that type. You do not have to filter one long list of all the brand's products. **
  • Ad groups are the first level of segmentation. For high-ticket stores (stores selling expensive products), split by brand. Each brand has different product margins, and bids should reflect margin. **
  • Product type is the second most common split after brand. Ad groups can be split by product type instead of brand. Choose the split that separates products with different margins and bid needs. **

Demo Account Example (Shopping Campaign Split by Search Value)

1 min
  • In the demo account (a practice account), three Shopping campaigns were split by low, medium and high value search terms. Two campaigns showed a budget of US$300 each, and one was limited by its budget. Within a brand product group, the group level showed a max CPC (the highest bid you allow per click) of US$0.30 (enhanced). Enhanced means Google may adjust your bid a little to get more sales. Individual products were bid at US$0.20 per click. The benchmark max CPC shown was US$0.74. A benchmark is a reference figure that Google displays. **

Splitting by Margin and Profit

3 min

Principle

  • Allocate ad spend based on profit margins, not order volume (the number of orders). Adjust CPC bids the same way. Increase spend on products with higher margins. Lower the CPC bids on products with lower margins or high COGS. COGS means cost of goods sold: what the product costs you to buy.
  • Segment by intent (what the shopper wants) and margin. Uniform settings produce worse targets than group-specific ones.

Three-tier margin setup

  1. Identify high-margin, mid-margin, and low-margin product groups across all suppliers.
  2. Create Alpha High Margin (HM), Beta HM, Alpha Mid Margin (MM), Beta MM, Alpha Low Margin (LM), and Beta LM campaigns.
  3. Assign shared budgets to each tier based on profitability goals. Example: a $100 daily budget split as $60 HM, $30 MM, $10 LM.
  4. This allows budget control per margin tier, rather than one budget across all products.

Net-profit tiering

  • For net-profit tiers, use separate Alpha and Beta campaigns per profit band. Net profit is what you keep from a sale after all costs. A profit band is a range of profit. Keep products below the minimum profit out of all campaigns.
  • Example: Alpha 1 and Beta 1 contain products above a set net profit per sale. Products between two thresholds (limit values) go into Alpha 2 and Beta 2. Products below the lower threshold are excluded from all campaigns. The budget can be split, for example, 80 percent to the high-profit tier and 20 percent to the middle tier, with nothing to the lowest tier.

Step-by-step: building the first profit-tier campaign

  1. In Google Ads, create a new Shopping Campaign.
  2. Select conversion actions for tracking. A conversion is a sale or other goal you measure.
  3. Select your product feed (connected via Some Process).
  4. Choose Standard shopping campaign (not Performance Max).
  5. Click Continue.
  6. Name the campaign (e.g., "alpha high profit").
  7. Select a bidding strategy: manual CPC or enhanced CPC.
  8. Set the daily budget amount.
  9. Set campaign priority (alpha or beta).
  10. Turn off Search Network. Search Network shows your ads on partner websites outside Google search.
  11. Keep All Devices selected (if this has worked in past campaigns).
  12. Select the country where you do business.
  13. Name the ad group.
  14. Set default cost per click based on historical data (your past results) from existing campaigns, not estimates.
  15. Click Create Campaign.
  16. In the product groups section, click the plus icon to subdivide.
  17. Select Custom Label from the dropdown.
  18. Select Custom Label Zero.
  19. Enter or select the label value (e.g., "high profit").
  20. Verify the product count displayed matches your expectations.
  21. Save without editing bids.
  22. For the remaining "All Products" group, click the green button.
  23. Select Exclude to remove all non-labeled products from this campaign.
  24. The campaign now contains only the labeled product segment.

Example: Source A Merch Store had 22 products in its product feed. After analysing sales data, three t-shirts were identified as the highest-profit products. They were moved into a separate Alpha campaign with a dedicated budget. The same process would be repeated for mid-profit and low-profit segments, creating separate Alpha and Beta campaigns for each profitability tier.

Before launching profit-tier campaigns

  • Pause the original all-products Alpha and Beta campaigns in Google Ads before you launch the new profit-tier campaigns. If both old and new campaigns stay active, the same products receive budget from multiple campaigns. This wastes budget and corrupts performance data. The new segmented campaigns must be the only active campaigns for their products.

Splitting by Price Tier

2 min

Principle

  • Tiered campaigns by price follow the same logic as Alpha and Beta. The highest-priced products get top priority. Each lower price tier is separated out by priority. This is used when scaling beyond the initial two-campaign structure. **

Threshold values by source and context

Source Threshold When it applies
Source A Minimum price of $200 or $500 for paid Google ads Premium-only Google Shopping campaign
Source A Threshold X, for example $300 or $400 Duplicating campaigns when cheap items use up the budget
Source A $1,500 Comparing ROAS between price ranges
Source A High, mid and low tiers, no fixed figure Scaling beyond the initial two campaigns

Premium-only campaign **

  1. Set a minimum price point for paid Google ads, for example $200 or $500. Premium means higher-priced.
  2. Segment products in Google Merchant Center so that only products above that price are advertised on Google Shopping.
  3. Keep the lower-priced products out of those ads, since they are hard to keep profitable through paid ads.
  • Lower-priced products that already make money can stay on the same website. But keep them out of the high-price Google Shopping campaigns. Keep the paid ads focused on the higher-value items.

Splitting when cheap items use up the budget **

  1. Duplicate each main campaign to create a second one.
  2. In the original campaigns, exclude products under your chosen price threshold X, for example $300 or $400. Threshold X is the price line you pick.
  3. In the duplicated campaigns, exclude products over threshold X, so they only show lower-priced products.
  4. Lower the daily budget and the cost per click in the duplicated campaigns. Let the main campaigns spend on the higher-priced products that earn the most.

Comparing ROAS by price range **

  1. Build Alpha and Beta campaigns that include all products.
  2. Duplicate them into Alpha 2 and Beta 2, filtered to products above a price threshold, such as $1,500.
  3. Modify the original Alpha and Beta campaigns to include only products below that threshold.
  4. Compare ROAS for each price range, and move budget toward the better range.

Splitting by Brand and Supplier

1 min

Ways to split a high-ticket store (high-ticket means expensive products)

  • Shopping campaigns can be split by brand, product type, price, or a mix of these. Choose the way that fits how you want to organise your products. A Shopping campaign is a Google ad that shows your product photo and price.
  • For most high-ticket stores, split by brand first, then by single product. Brands often have different margins (the money you keep from each sale after costs). So they can deserve different bids (the most you offer for one click) even at the same price. Profit numbers per brand show which brands make more or less money.
  • Split by what your data shows: brand, category, bestseller, or price range. Pick the split that performs, not the one that is simply the default.
  • Split by brand when one brand covers many categories and search terms show other categories landing in the wrong campaign. Then split the Alpha and Beta campaigns (the paired campaigns used in this setup) further by category for that supplier. Only do this if those campaigns bring in results.

Winning Brand with Most of the Revenue

1 min
  • When one brand brings in most of the revenue, for example about 80% of the revenue from a store with around 20 brands, give it its own campaign. This makes its results easy to read.
  • Wait about a month or longer before you decide a brand is a winner. A few days of data is not enough.
  • When one brand takes most of a campaign's budget (the money you allow to be spent per day), move it into its own Alpha and Beta campaigns. Then remove its products from the all-products campaigns. Give each strong brand its own budget, so other brands can grow on their own.

Step-by-Step: Moving a Winning Brand into Its Own Campaign

1 min
  1. Create a new Alpha campaign. Name it after the brand and the target country, for example alpha, brand name, US.
  2. Add only that brand's products to the new campaign.
  3. Remove the same products from the main Alpha campaign, so they are not shown twice.
  4. Give the new campaign its own daily budget, a higher cost per click (CPC, the price you pay each time someone clicks), and Enhanced CPC (Google adjusts your bids a little to get more sales).
  • Do not keep a winning brand's products in both the main Alpha campaign and its own campaign. They compete with each other, and the results become unclear.
  • Raising the bid on a winning brand inside the main Alpha campaign also works. A separate campaign is easier to track and gets its own budget. So the separate campaign is the clearer choice.

Brands Getting No Impressions

1 min

An impression is one time your ad is shown to someone.

  1. Find the brands that get no impressions while other brands get all the traffic (visitors).
  2. Either raise the maximum CPC for the products of those brands, or
  3. Create a second Alpha and Beta set. Move only the brands with no traffic into it, and remove them from the first set.
  4. Review again after a few days.
  • Per-brand Alpha and Beta campaigns make it easier to see which brands are winning. Keep the brand names visible in the campaign name, so the split is easy to read.

Rebalancing Budget When One Brand Dominates

1 min
  1. Check last month's spend in your current Alpha and Beta campaigns. See how much of the budget the winning brand takes.
  2. Duplicate (make a copy of) the Alpha and Beta campaigns.
  3. In the original Alpha 1 and Beta 1, keep only the winning brand. It already has the conversions (sales or goals completed). Keep its budget at 40 USD per day.
  4. In Alpha 2 and Beta 2, put all remaining brands. Add 10 USD per day, split 5 USD between Alpha 2 and Beta 2, so the other products can collect data.
  5. Or, lower Alpha 1 and Beta 1 to 30 USD per day, and put 10 USD per day into Alpha 2 and Beta 2.

Supplier-Based Campaign Sets

1 min
  1. Create two separate campaign sets (Alpha and Beta) for each supplier, instead of one shared Alpha and Beta set. A supplier is the company that sends you the products.
  2. Example: Supplier A (alpha-A, beta-A), Supplier B (alpha-B, beta-B), Supplier C (alpha-C, beta-C).
  3. In the shared campaigns, exclude the supplier you want to focus on. For example, exclude Supplier C from the main Alpha and Beta.
  4. In the supplier-specific campaigns, include only that supplier's products.
  5. This spreads the daily budget across suppliers. It stops high-volume suppliers from using up the whole budget first.

Brand Lifecycle in Google Shopping

1 min
  1. Start with all brands in the campaigns, so you can see what sells.
  2. Over time, find brands that perform poorly on Google Shopping, or have a margin that is not viable (not enough profit to keep going).
  3. Pause those brands inside the campaigns. Do not delete them. Keep them on the site.
  4. Reactivate a paused brand when things change. For example: new pricing, a new owner, or a limited-time deal to clear excess stock.
  • Pause, do not delete, a brand's Shopping products. The campaign setup and its data stay intact. So you can turn the brand back on later without rebuilding everything. For example, after new pricing, a change of owner, or a limited-time deal to clear extra stock.
  • Mistake to avoid: splitting bestseller brands into their own high-priority campaign. Priority is which campaign Google uses first when a product fits more than one. The split makes the bestsellers compete with the remaining high-priority campaign for generic terms, such as "infrared sauna" (broad searches anyone might type). Medium and low priority campaigns do not compete with each other when they target different brands.
  • Tool for brand competition: when split high-priority campaigns compete, bid more on one campaign than the other. Or use negative keywords (search words that stop your ad from showing) so each campaign covers a different group of generic terms. Without this, the split can lower the performance of a campaign that was already converting.
  • Example: a store sells infrared saunas from ten brands. Three are bestsellers. The three bestsellers get their own high-priority campaign. The other seven stay in the original one. Both campaigns keep generic infrared sauna searches, so they compete for that term. Only bids or negative keywords keep them apart.

Ranking Brands for Priority

1 min
  • To set priorities in Google Shopping, sort your brand list with the best-selling brands first. Use the top brands shown by large competitor stores as a guide. Focus CPC and product page work on those brands first, because they bring in most of the revenue.

Bidding per Group and Target ROAS

2 min

ROAS (return on ad spend) is the money you earn back for each dollar spent on ads. A ROAS of 29x means $29 back for every $1 spent. A target ROAS is the ROAS you want Google to aim for.

  • Three-tier ROAS example: A client used three Shopping tiers, each with its own target ROAS. The branded tier had a very high target. The client wanted strong margins on branded traffic, reasoning that these shoppers would find them anyway. It reached 29x ROAS within two months. The beta shopping tier reached 3x against a target of 4x. The bottom tier reached 2x.
  • Cluster products for data: Automated bidding needs more data per group. So group similar products into larger product groups (clusters). For example, put bestsellers in one group and the rest in another. Aim for at least 20 conversions per month per cluster.
  • Set targets per group: Do not use one blanket setting for all products. Ask what each ad group is about, then set bids and targets for that group.
  • One target ROAS per group: One product group cannot use different target ROAS values. For items with higher margins, move them into their own ad group. Then set a separate target ROAS there.
  • Bidding type: When you create split campaigns, choose manual CPC (you set every bid yourself) or Enhanced CPC. Use Enhanced CPC for the winning-brand campaign.
  • Default CPC: Set your default cost per click from historical data of existing campaigns. Do not guess.
  • Raise bids on proven products: Raise bids on products that already convert in the low-priority campaign.
  • Shared budgets with test campaigns: Run several Google Shopping campaigns on one shared daily budget. Use one campaign for proven MAP products (products with a set minimum advertised price). Use a test campaign for new products, with bids near the minimum (about one cent). Use a low-priority campaign for product-level bidding. Google often suggests raising a shared budget. You can ignore these suggestions until performance justifies it.
  • Winning products get more traffic: A Shopping campaign that beats its target ROAS should get more traffic. Raise its bids, as long as its ROAS stays at or above the target. Raise its budget too. A well-performing campaign should almost never be limited by budget.

Bestseller Campaigns

1 min

Principle

  • Start with Google Shopping campaigns on products that have a proven sales history. Then expand. Keep long-tail products (niche products with few searches) on organic traffic (free visitors from search) until their performance is established.
  • Feeding Google only proven sellers lets the system learn what converts. It stops budget from going to products that have no buyer demand. Once bestseller campaigns are profitable, put untested products into separate testing Alpha and Beta campaigns.
  • You can list products as free Shopping listings, while only the bestseller group gets paid budget. Free listings show, over time, which non-featured products get impressions and sales.

Budget

  • Start with most of the spend on the bestseller group. For example, use 100 percent in the first month, or a 90/10 split with 10 percent for testing other products. Introduce the testing budget for the remaining products after about a month.
  • Bestseller split example:
Campaign Products Shared budget (per day)
Alpha bestsellers + Beta bestsellers Products identified as bestsellers Shared budget 1: $15
Alpha everything else + Beta everything else All other products Shared budget 2: $5

Each shared budget (one budget used by two campaigns) is attached to its two campaigns. Meta remarketing (ads to people who visited before, on Facebook and Instagram) is separate, at $5 per day.

Building Bestseller Campaigns Without Sales Data

1 min
  1. Ask suppliers which of their products sell best.
  2. Or check competitor stores (stores selling the same kind of products) in the future. Look at the products shown on their home page, at the top of their collections, and in collections sorted by best sellers.
  3. Put those products into the Alpha and Beta bestseller campaigns.
  4. Remove those same products from the Alpha and Beta everything-else campaigns.

Rebuilding Around a Few Proven Sellers

1 min
  • When a store has a few hundred products but only a small share makes sales, rebuild the Google Shopping structure. Create new campaigns named "Alpha Best Sellers" and "Beta Best Sellers." Add product groups only for products that already sell. Pause the old Alpha and Beta campaigns that were not converting.

Case: A store with about 300 products had over 100 sales on Microsoft Ads (Bing's ad platform) in one year. But 3,000 clicks on Google Ads over two months produced only one conversion. Only about 30 products made up the Microsoft sales. The advice was to pause the existing Google Alpha and Beta campaigns. Build new Best Sellers campaigns with just those 30 products. Add the remaining products later, in separate testing campaigns, once the first ones are profitable.

Discount Codes

1 min
  • When a store uses discount codes as Shopping promotions, exclude low-margin products from the new Alpha and Beta Best Sellers campaigns. Promotions do not appear in the free organic Shopping section. So low-margin products can stay there without a discount attached.

Hypothetical example: A store with 500 products sells only 20 of them again and again. About 80% of the Google budget goes to those 20. If the next year the same 80% goes to 20 different products that convert worse, check sales per product. Then refine CPCs to send the budget back to proven sellers.

Region, Country and Geo-Targeting

1 min

Geo-targeting means choosing where your ads are shown, such as a country, state, zip code, or radius (a circle around a point on the map).

Geo-targeting options and principles

  • Geo-targeting in Google Ads can target zip codes, whole states, or a radius around a city. It does not have to be a whole country. This lets you limit ads to customers near a supplier's warehouse.
  • Geo-targeting in Google Ads lets you limit ads to specific states or areas. This helps when shipping costs from a supplier make some regions more profitable than others.
  • Use geo-targeting in Google Ads campaigns to match the shipping region profiles in Shopify (the rules that set which regions your store ships to). Then ads only show in regions where products can be shipped.
  • If you only want to spend on customers in the United States, set the location targeting to the US only. Visitors from other countries can still arrive through free organic Google search, without any spend.
  • Ads for surfboards shown to people far inland, such as in the middle of Texas, will struggle to convert. The audience has no use for the product. Check that location and audience match the product before you scale spend.
  • Geotargeting for Shopping ads is only useful when you want deliveries close to a supplier's warehouse. Its effect on performance is not proven.
  • Use geotargeting only when supplier shipping costs differ sharply by region. Example: 500 USD to the far coast, versus 150 to 250 USD across the nearby region, with thin margins.
  • For products that cost a lot to ship to some regions, run ads only in states close to the supplier. Exclude distant states. This stops shipping costs from wiping out the profit on each sale.
  • Seasonal geo-targeting is done inside Google and Facebook. Choose the warmer states in the months when sales are low.

Supplier Radius Targeting

1 min
  • When shipping costs are high for one supplier, set up a separate Alpha and Beta for that supplier. Use geotargeting to show ads only within a radius around the supplier's warehouse, for example 100 miles. Only do this if orders stay profitable at that distance.

Step-by-Step: Geo-Targeting for a Supplier Whose Shipping Has Made the Margin Too Thin

1 min
  1. Find the supplier whose margin no longer covers shipping to far-away customers. For example, a warehouse in New Jersey with buyers in California.
  2. Set up a separate campaign for that supplier only. Use a product feed (the product list you send to Google) that contains only that supplier's products. You can duplicate an existing campaign and rename it after the supplier.
  3. Set the targeting to a radius around the warehouse, specific zip codes, or states where shipping is still profitable.
  4. Remove those products from your main campaigns, so they are not shown to distant customers.
  5. Repeat for other suppliers as needed.
  • Geo-targeting lets you keep selling products whose margin no longer covers shipping to far regions. The other choices are dropping them or raising prices until nobody buys. Ad spend goes to customers close enough to the warehouse for shipping to stay profitable.

Note on conflicting advice: Geo-targeting for supplier radius is recommended in the 2018, 2022 and 2023 sources, where shipping costs differ sharply. A 2021 source says its effect on performance is not proven. Use it when the shipping-cost gap is large and orders stay profitable at that distance. Otherwise, treat it as an unproven lever.

Multi-Country and Multi-Region Campaigns

1 min

Principle: one country per campaign (most recent advice)

  • Run one country per campaign. If you later want ads in a second country, build a separate Alpha and Beta pair for that country. Do not add it to the existing campaigns.
  • Keep a separate Google Shopping campaign for each country a product is sold in. If Canada is added, do not mix it into a US campaign. Use an Alpha 2 and Beta 2 that target only Canada, so budgets are not mixed together.
  • Google Shopping campaigns for different countries should not share one campaign. Keep the US Alpha and Beta campaigns separate from the UK campaigns.
  • When selling in two countries, do not add the second country to an existing Alpha or Beta campaign. Create separate campaigns per country. For example, keep the Canada campaigns, and build new US Alpha and Beta campaigns with the same products but US locations.
  • Run separate campaigns per country, such as the US, Canada, Australia and the UK.
  • For a store selling in several regions, run separate Alpha and Beta campaigns for each region. Also run separate Meta retargeting campaigns for each region. This keeps targeting, budget and results separate for each market.

Older option: one campaign with several countries

  • To target several countries, you can do one of two things. Either create one campaign and select all target countries, and let Google spread the spend. Or duplicate the campaign for each country, to control spend by country.
  • Do not mix two countries in one campaign.

Note on conflicting advice: The 2021 advice allows one campaign with several countries, when you want Google to spread the spend. Later advice from 2021 to 2024 requires one country per campaign. Use one campaign for several countries only if you accept Google's spend split across them. Use one campaign per country whenever you need to control budget by country.

Step-by-Step: Adding Canada to a US Store

1 min
  1. Keep the existing US campaigns unchanged.
  2. Duplicate each US campaign.
  3. In the settings of the copy, edit the location targeting. Remove United States and add Canada.
  4. Lower the budget of the Canadian campaign. Do not mix two countries in one campaign.

Step-by-Step: Two Countries with One Currency Each

1 min
  1. Duplicate every Alpha and Beta campaign, so the settings are identical.
  2. Set the country targeting of the first copy to Canada (and US, if applicable).
  3. Set the country targeting of the duplicate to the US only.
  4. Compare results per campaign to see which country performs.

Out-of-Stock Products

1 min

Out of stock means the supplier does not have the product now. Back order means you accept orders for it, but it ships later.

Principle

  • Keep running paid ads for products that are in stock, even during the 2020 COVID-19 period. If a supplier runs out of a product, remove that item from Google Merchant Center (Google's product data tool). Then you do not pay to advertise products you cannot ship.
  • During inventory shortages, run only a small, tight Google Shopping campaign. Use the products that have the best margins and are in stock. Stop promoting back-ordered products.

Lead-time rules (lead time means how long until new stock arrives)

Stock situation Action Source
Back in stock within about two to three weeks Keep ads running and show an ETA note
Out of stock for about four weeks and will not return (2021 rule) Stop taking orders, remove from ad campaigns, add back when close to restocking
Back order six to eight weeks or longer, or no ETA Set Google Shopping CPC to 1 cent, or stop spending until back in stock
Back order eight weeks or longer with no ETA Lower Google Shopping CPC to about 1 cent, or pause until back in stock. The product page still shows the back-order status and an ETA
Supplier runs out of product (2020) Remove from Google Merchant Center

ETA means estimated time of arrival, the date the stock is expected.

Note on conflicting thresholds: The 2021 rule removes products after about four weeks without a return. The 2024 rules keep ads running for two to three weeks. They move to a 1-cent CPC or a pause at six to eight weeks or longer. Use the 2024 thresholds for current planning. Use the four-week rule only if a product has no ETA and no restock date.

Full Procedure: Handling Out-of-Stock Products by Lead Time

1 min
  1. For stock returning within two to three weeks, keep ads running and show an ETA note.
  2. For back orders of six to eight weeks or longer, or with no ETA, set the Google Shopping CPC to 1 cent, or stop spending until the product is back in stock.
  3. Add a back-order note with the shipping ETA next to the add-to-cart button on the product page. (Add-to-cart is the button that puts an item in the shopping basket.)
  4. On popular product pages, add a "need something sooner" section. It links to in-stock similar products.

Back-Order Note and Paused Products

1 min
  • See the table above. Product pages keep the back-order status and ETA while the Shopping CPC is lowered or the product is paused.

MAP (Minimum Advertised Price, the lowest price a brand lets shops advertise) Violations

1 min
  • If a supplier (the company that ships products to you) keeps violating MAP after you follow up, lower the cost per click to one cent for that product. Cost per click (CPC) is what you pay each time someone clicks your ad. This keeps the product visible without overspending on it. **
  • Move products or suppliers with repeated problems, such as MAP violations, into their own campaign. A campaign is a group of ads with its own budget and settings. Give it lower bids or a very small budget. A bid is the most you offer for one click. **
  • If a supplier enforces MAP, but big-box retailers (very large stores) sell below your wholesale cost (what you pay the supplier), do not advertise the product in paid channels (paid ad platforms) at double the price of other stores.
  • Work with the supplier to fix the MAP violation first. **
  • Keep products with proven MAP compliance (products whose suppliers follow the rule) in a separate campaign with a shared budget, as described in the bidding section above. **

Budget Allocation and Scaling

2 min

Scaling a profitable campaign (raising its budget step by step) **

  1. Raise the budget by 10, 20 or 30 percent. The budget is the daily amount the campaign may spend.
  2. Let the campaign run until the actual return on ad spend (ROAS) shows. ROAS is the sales you make for each dollar spent on ads.
  3. Raise the budget again by a similar step and repeat.
  • A very large one-time budget increase can distort ROAS for a full month. In one example, ROAS fell from about 6.5x average to 1.5x after a sharp scale-up. (6.5x means 6.5 dollars of sales for each dollar spent.) So raise budgets in small increments. **
  • A new campaign with only 15 dollars per day, where 20 out of 100 products get all the clicks: use a higher CPC and higher daily budget only if the campaign is profitable. Otherwise, move the clicking products into their own Alpha campaign and scale that one. (Alpha is one of the two campaign names this method uses, along with Beta.) **

Budget splits used in examples

Source Budget split Context
(2025) $100 daily: $60 HM (high margin), $30 MM (mid margin), $10 LM (low margin) Margin tiers (groups by profit margin, the share of each sale kept as profit)
(2024) Bestseller $15, everything else $5, Meta (Facebook and Instagram ads) remarketing (ads for people who visited before) $5 per day Bestseller split
(2024) 100% to bestsellers in month one, or 90/10 with 10% for testing Bestseller launch
(2024) 80% to high-profit tier, 20% to middle tier, 0% to lowest Net-profit tiers (groups by profit left after all costs)

Campaign-level budget logic

  • Control the budget per margin tier, not one budget across all products.
  • Use one shared budget for each pair of campaigns. A shared budget is one budget used by linked campaigns.
  • Keep the budget of the winning brand fixed while other brands get a testing budget (money for trying new products).

Relaunching, Rebuilding and Migrating Stalled Accounts

2 min

Principle: new campaigns, not reactivated ones

  • A stalled account is one that has stopped selling. If you relaunch (start again) a store that was paused for years, create entirely new campaigns instead of reactivating (turning back on) old ones. New campaigns learn from recent market data and follow current platform best practices (the platform's latest recommended methods).
  • Repair the existing campaign first. Campaigns that have run for a long time with steady, high spend can carry some setup weaknesses. Google gives weight to account history and spend volume.
  • Rebuild or pause only if nothing moves after repairs. Rebuilding is generally slower to recover than fixing an account with history.
  • Create a new campaign only when the existing one is not moving after repair attempts. Pausing is rare.
  • In roughly 1% of cases, starting the whole account fresh may be the better choice. This is usually true in penalised niches (product areas Google has penalised, which means it limits ads there).

Relaunch procedure for a stalled or failed store **

  1. Rebuild paid ad campaigns from scratch instead of turning the old ones back on.
  2. Submit products to free Google product listings (free product results on Google) if they have not been submitted yet.
  3. Send a timely promotion to your existing email list (your list of subscribers) to re-engage (win back) past subscribers.
  4. Follow up with the remarketing audience (people who have already visited your store).
  5. Restart a regular social media schedule, for example one post per week.

Rebuilding Google Shopping spend (Google Shopping shows products with pictures and prices on Google) on an older account with sales history **

  1. In Shopify (online store software), list every product sold in the past one to two years together with its profit.
  2. Build a new product feed (the product list file sent to Google) that contains only the products that sold profitably in that period.
  3. Remove the old product feed from Google Merchant Center (Google's tool that holds your product list).
  4. Upload the new feed to Merchant Center.
  5. Spend the reduced daily budget only on these products, and add more products after they start to sell.

Changing to a new store URL (new web address) **

  1. Turn off all Google Ads (Google's advertising platform), without deleting the ad accounts.
  2. In Merchant Center, disconnect the product feed and delete the existing products.
  3. Create a new Merchant Center account for the new store and upload the feed there.
  4. Reuse the same Google Ads account, pointed at the new store.
  5. Do not turn the old redirect ads back on, because tracking will break. A redirect ad points to an old web address that forwards visitors to the new one. Tracking means measuring which ads bring sales.

Rebuilding for best-sellers **

  • See the bestseller section above for the procedure and case. A bestseller is your top-selling product.
  • The rebuild uses only proven sellers and pauses the old non-converting Alpha and Beta campaigns. Non-converting means the campaigns are not turning clicks into sales.

Campaign Structure: Start Simple, Segment on Evidence

1 min
  • To segment means to split into smaller groups.
  • Start with at least one Alpha and one Beta Shopping campaign, then segment further into brand campaigns and best-seller campaigns. Give the highest budgets to the most productive ones. **
  • Keep the structure simple in the first stage. Segmenting too early splits the budget and slows learning (the time Google needs to find out which clicks sell). So segment later, as the data supports it. **
  • Use a two-tier structure at launch, with one Alpha and one Beta campaign holding all products. **
  • In the early phase, the goal is to buy data on search terms (words people type into Google) and products that show around a 1% conversion rate (the share of clicks that become sales).
  • Spreading a small budget across hundreds or thousands of products leaves each product with too few clicks to show its own conversion rate. So concentrate budget on fewer products.
  • When many products have only one or two clicks each (for example, 500 products with two clicks each), reduce the number of products by turning off weaker brands or products.
  • Concentrate spending on the highest-margin (the most profit left per sale) or best cost-per-click items to gather data sooner.
  • Campaigns can be split by supplier or product category, for example separate Alpha and Beta campaigns per supplier or category. This shows which segments perform best. **
  • When adding a second niche (a separate area of related products), use a separate Alpha/Beta pair with its own budget and no overlapping products.
  • Mixing unrelated products into an existing campaign resets its learning phase (restarts the time Google needs to learn). It can hurt performance for weeks.

Splitting by Brand and Supplier

2 min

Brand split procedure **:

  1. Start with all products in one Alpha and one Beta campaign.
  2. Once one brand drives most sales, duplicate both campaigns (make exact copies).
  3. In the originals, remove brands two through seven.
  4. In the duplicates, remove brand one.
  5. Set budgets separately: keep the proven brand at its historical daily spend (what it spent per day before), test the others at a lower level, then optimise (improve).

Example budget split **: the proven brand keeps its $100 per day, and the other brands start at about $50 per day. Budgets are adjusted based on how each campaign performs.

  • Use the Alpha/Beta structure to isolate brands (separate one brand from the rest). Put a brand that outperforms into its own Alpha and Beta campaigns.
  • Exclude that brand's products from the main Alpha and Beta. Excluding means removing them from the campaign. The main campaign's budget then goes only to the rest of the catalogue (all the products you sell). **
  • Suppliers with low search volume (few people search for them) can still be listed. Put them in their own Alpha and Beta, or keep them in the main campaign.
  • In the main campaign, set a lower max cost per click (the highest price you allow per click) for their brand, so they do not overspend. **
  • There is no fixed golden ratio for the budget between Alpha and Beta campaigns. The right split depends on the niche, the suppliers and how many products each has. **

Feed per brand **: Create separate product feeds per brand in the Google Shopping app for Shopify (for example, a Nike feed and a Reebok feed). Link each feed to its own campaign. Do not put all brands into one campaign with many ad groups (groups inside a campaign). This prevents a product from appearing in the wrong ad group.

Weak supplier separation procedure **:

  1. Duplicate your main Alpha and Beta Shopping campaigns.
  2. In the new set, include only the weak supplier's products.
  3. Exclude that supplier from the main Alpha and Beta campaigns.
  4. Keep the budgets as they are, and raise the CPC for the new set only if traction (sales momentum) stays low.
  • Do not raise bids on a weak supplier inside the main campaign. That can pull traffic away from products that already convert. A separate campaign lets you measure that supplier's performance on its own. **

Splitting by Price, Margin and Product Tier

1 min
  • Price bands **: When the Shopping campaign brings cheap add-on sales instead of high-ticket items (expensive products), split it into price-band campaigns. For example, one for products of $500 or more and one for products under $500.
  • Give each campaign its own budget and bids, so expensive items get the budget and cheap items get cheaper clicks.
  • Accessories **: Put low-priced accessories in a collection linked from the product pages as add-ons.
  • Exclude that collection from Google Shopping campaigns, since advertising cheap accessories is rarely profitable. Accessories priced around 400 to 500 USD may be worth ads.
  • Print on demand (2025): If print on demand products (products made only when ordered) are added to a store, include only the high-ticket products in the Shopping campaigns.
  • Exclude the print on demand products. Otherwise most ad spend can go to items that earn about $10 per sale.
  • Product tiers (2024):
    • Alpha and Beta campaigns can be segmented by product tier (product ranking level), such as a bestseller group, a mid-seller group, or groups split by high, mid and low profit.
    • Add new SKUs (stock keeping units, each product's unique code) to an existing Alpha or Beta group only if that group contains the same tier of products, such as bestsellers.
    • Do not mix new, untested products into a bestseller-only group. Create a separate testing group instead.

Bestsellers, Alpha 2 and Beta 2

1 min

Concentrated sales procedure (2023): Use this when a few products dominate sales.

  1. Keep the current CPC changes first as the main step.
  2. When about 20 of 100 products make almost all the sales, duplicate the Alpha and Beta campaigns into Alpha 1, Beta 1, Alpha 2 and Beta 2.
  3. Keep only the top products in Alpha 1 and Beta 1.
  4. Move the remaining products into Alpha 2 and Beta 2 with their own budget, so they can start to spend without affecting the top products.

Source difference: The 2022 source keeps the structure at two tiers. It says to split later into Alpha 2 and Beta 2 by best sellers, a single brand or a price range, instead of adding a third tier. ** The 2023 source names the four campaigns and uses the trigger of about 20 of 100 products. Use the 2022 criteria to decide what goes into Alpha 2 and Beta 2. Use the 2023 trigger to decide when to create them.

  • Traffic volume is driven by competition, search volume and budget, not by the number of brands sold. To increase clicks, raise bids or budget on the products already listed rather than adding a fixed number of brands.
  • For products with more than about 10 competing stores on Google Shopping, paid traffic is likely to become too expensive. Keep those products on the store for organic traffic (free visits from search results).
  • Focus the paid budget on products with fewer competitors.

Custom Labels and Product Groups

1 min
  • Custom labels (extra tags you add to products to group them) are optional at launch. Use them later to manage products more finely as sales grow. For example, when moving from about 20,000 to 50,000 per month toward 100,000 or more per month.
  • Segment Shopping campaigns down to the product ID level (each product's own ID number), because only then can you set bids per product.
  • Exclude the "everything else" catch-all subgroup (a group that takes every product not assigned above it) at each level. It duplicates products already assigned above it.

Geo-Targeting and Costly-Shipping Suppliers

1 min

Geo-targeting means choosing the regions where your ads show.

  • Default position: Geo-targeting is off for almost all campaigns. Use it only for the special supplier case with heavy goods and a warehouse (the supplier's storage building). (2023.)
  • Narrow case: For a supplier with heavy or costly shipping, target only the low-shipping region for that supplier's products. This applies to a very small share of products. (2022.)
  • Heavy items: Restrict ad targeting to regions close to the supplier's warehouse to keep shipping costs manageable. The margin must still cover shipping when the customer is further away. (2022.)

Supplier far from customers procedure **:

  1. Create a new campaign named after the region.
  2. Exclude that supplier from the main campaign.
  3. Include the supplier only in the regional campaign.
  4. In Google Ads, open the campaign, then the ad set (a group inside a campaign), then the Locations field. Select the supplier's state and nearby states.

General principle **: If you are unsure whether to adjust geographic targeting, increase spend in regions with clearly good results and cut spend where it only wastes money. Decide with data, not guesses.

Controlling Products and Bids

1 min
  • Setting CPC to 0 cents only stops spending on those products. They can still be present in the campaign.
  • To remove them from Shopping campaigns completely, use the product include and exclude options in the campaign settings.
  • When a Google Shopping campaign has no winning product yet, exclude the products that do not perform and keep testing the remaining pool in batches.
  • In one example, 20 products had been excluded and about 200 were still left to test before a winner was expected to emerge.
  • When Google Ads cost per click for a broad category rises (for example, furniture), split the account into more granular sub-categories with separate campaigns or ad groups and separate bids for each. (2023.)
  • Remove traffic that brings in general or unrelated clicks. (2023.)

Scaling Budgets

1 min

Profitable scaling procedure **:

  1. Start from the current daily budget only if it is profitable, for example 15 per day.
  2. Raise the budget in steps, for example to 25 per day, and hold it there while checking results.
  3. At each level, check whether ads are still as profitable, which ads are more or less profitable, and which broad keywords (very general search words) appear that need negative keywords (search words that block your ad from showing).
  4. Move to the next level only when profitable scaling holds.
  • Do not jump from 15 to 200 per day in one step. That is too abrupt. Google then spends on broader keywords with lower conversion, so raise budgets gradually instead. **
  • Every Google Ads account has a ceiling on profitable traffic. The ceiling differs by product, niche and market. Total search demand can be researched in advance. But the actual cost per click and the share of that traffic you can win against competitors cannot be predicted accurately. The only reliable way to find the ceiling is to test in the live account. **
  • When Google Ads reaches its profitable ceiling, keep the campaigns at that level rather than pushing spend higher. Build other traffic sources to add volume alongside them. **

Moving winners to CPC (undated):

  1. Get sales and fulfil orders on time, since delivery is part of the customer experience.
  2. Identify the products that perform better than others.
  3. Move those products from Google Shopping or Performance Max (Google's automatic all-in-one campaign type) to CPC campaigns (campaigns where you pay per click). Bid per click to buy more traffic.
  4. Assess the results. Expect ROAS to fall as you scale. Make sure there is enough margin left to remain profitable.

Stalled Accounts: Relaunch and Rebuild

1 min

Stalled campaign case **: A Google Shopping campaign was profitable at about 20 return on ad spend, with roughly 75 percent impression share (the share of possible ad views you get) on a low-priority keyword. To gain more impressions, the owner raised the keyword bids. This triggered a flood of generic keywords that never converted and consumed the budget. After about a month of lowering bids and rechecking settings, the campaign was still not profitable. The recommendation was to pause the old campaign and build a fresh one with the same rules. Let it run for about a week, because restoring old settings rarely fixes this.

Relaunch with a smaller budget after no sales (2024):

  1. Use historical data to list products that actually sold at a profit.
  2. Create an Alpha/Beta campaign named Bestsellers that includes only those products.
  3. Start with a smaller budget and scale within that campaign as it proves itself.
  4. Keep the other products in separate Alpha testing and Beta testing campaigns with their own budgets.
  5. Move new winners into the Bestsellers campaign.
  6. Keep all products in the free Google product listings, and control spend through budget instead.

Both sources recommend rebuilding rather than repairing a stalled account. The 2019 advice rebuilds the whole campaign with the same rules. The 2024 advice rebuilds around proven products.

  • The alternative is entering the data per product in the Feed for Google Shopping app. Shopify's product CSV template already contains the Custom Label 0 to 4 columns.
