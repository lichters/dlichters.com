Playbook › 10. Google Ads and Shopping

Shopping campaign fundamentals and the alpha-Beta structure

Section 10.9 · 9 of 23 in this chapter · 52 min read

In short

  • Shopping ads show your product photo, price and store name. You do not pick keywords. Google reads your product list and chooses the searches.
  • Use Standard Shopping. Skip Performance Max and Smart Shopping, because they give you little control.
  • Set the shared daily budget to $5 to $10 in total. Start Alpha at $0.40 per click and Beta at $0.20 per click.
  • Turn off Search Partners. Add negative keywords to block searches that never buy.
  • Some older advice in this text disagrees with the newer advice. Check the newest number first.
More key points
  • Google checks your campaigns in priority order: high first, then medium, then low. This order only applies between your own campaigns.
  • Set Beta to High and Alpha to Low. This sounds backwards, but it sends weak searches to Beta first and lets strong searches fall through to Alpha.
  • Alpha is for your strongest searches, such as brand names and exact product codes. Beta is for weaker, broader searches. Put strong search words in Beta as blocked words so they move to Alpha.
  • Turn on the Item ID split so each product can get its own bid. Do not skip this step.
  • Do not move keywords by hand. Change the bid instead.
  • Turn on Google Shopping ads only after Google approves your products.
  • Judge ads by two numbers together: how often visitors buy, and how much money comes back per dollar spent.
  • Run two campaigns: Alpha for strong searches with higher prices per click, Beta for broad searches with lower prices.
  • Use negative keywords (blocked search words) to keep each search in the right campaign.
  • Give approval time: it can take 30 minutes to a few days.
  • Shopping ads are made from your product name, price and store name. You do not need to write ad text.
  • Start with two campaigns: alpha and beta. Alpha blocks weak searches, and beta receives them.
  • Launch with all your products. Split them one by one only after you have data.
  • Wait for data before you change bids or block many searches.
  • If Google Shopping sells better than Google Search, scale Shopping first.

How Shopping Ads Work

3 min
  • Shopping ads are paid ads. They show the product image, price and store name.
  • They appear on Google's front page and at the top of Shopping results.
  • Shopping ads give more control over traffic volume and scaling than any other Google traffic method.
  • Once you create and get approval for them, Shopping ads show your products at the top of Google Search results.
  • Paid Shopping ads appear in two places:
    • The top carousel (a row of product boxes) on the main results page, marked Sponsored
    • The top section of the Google Shopping tab, marked Sponsored
  • Anything marked Sponsored is a paid ad.
  • Below the paid ads are free listings that are not sponsored. They come from Google Merchant Center, Google's tool that holds your product data.
  • Shopping campaigns have no keyword bidding. A campaign is one group of ads with its own settings and budget.
  • You improve results in the Google Merchant Center product feed. A feed is your product list file, with one row per product.
  • The only text control in Google Ads is negative keywords. These are words that stop your ad from showing.
  • Titles, attributes (product details like size or colour) and images belong in Merchant Center.
  • Shopping ads show for product names, SKU numbers (a stock code for one product) and brand names from the feed. They also show the image, store name and price.
  • Searchers already know what they want, so these clicks carry strong buying intent. Intent means how close someone is to buying. This works only if negative keywords are set up correctly.

Feed-Driven Matching

  • The advertiser does not choose keywords.
  • All products go to Google Merchant Center. Google reads the product data (titles, descriptions, brand, product names, SKUs) to decide which searches show your ad.
  • Accurate product page data therefore drives matching.
  • Google does not allow you to choose keywords by hand. Matching search terms come from the product feed automatically. So bid control has to come from campaign structure and negative keywords.
  • Google matches products to searches using feed data and its own algorithms (automatic rules). You control only two things: the product data and the exclusions.
  • Google picks the triggering searches from your product data. This saves time on writing ads and choosing keywords.
  • But Google often shows products for weakly related searches. It does not judge search intent reliably. So you have to manage this yourself.
  • ** Google Shopping has no direct keyword targeting.
  • ** The only controls are the search terms that trigger ads and the negative keywords you add.
  • ** Searches that are too vague or broad usually produce few clicks.
  • Shopping campaigns do not use keywords you choose, the way Search campaigns do.
  • Titles, descriptions, brand, product names and SKUs from your Shopify store are matched through Merchant Center. Shopify is a store-building platform.
  • Google picks the search terms itself. So one campaign cannot bid higher on good terms and lower on weak terms.

Why Product Listing Ads Work

  • Product Listing Ads (PLAs) are Shopping ads with products, images and prices. They take up a very large share of the results page for buying searches. This gives them strong visibility.
  • PLAs show products visually in the results. Images, prices and seller information appear directly.
  • PLAs work because they reach people who are ready to buy a specific product. These people are in buy mode, not just researching or browsing.
  • PLA campaigns need no manual keyword research. Google's product feed system matches products to buying searches using the product data.
  • Google likes PLAs for buying searches. They give a good experience: relevant products, clear prices and good images.

Performance Max Not used. Many clicks but very low conversions

  • Google promotes Smart Campaigns to spend more money fast. It is not focused on performance. Google earns from ad volume, so the ROI (profit return) of each campaign matters less to it.
  • To beat competitors, use manual campaigns. You control strategy, targeting and bids. Simplified automated campaigns do not allow this.
  • For a new store with few orders, choose Standard Shopping, not Smart. The authors were still testing Smart when this was written.
  • Smart Shopping gives no control over keywords or negative keywords. It cannot build the funnel (the step-by-step path buyers take through your campaigns).
  • Its spend can overshoot or undershoot. This makes it unsuitable for high-ticket products (expensive items). **
  • ** Automated types such as Smart Shopping rarely work for high-ticket dropshipping. Dropshipping means you sell without holding stock; the supplier ships.
  • ** These types can still work for other physical products.
  • ** Keep high-ticket Shopping budgets for the shopping funnel structure.
  • Do not use "smart" bidding settings that remove control over campaign parameters.
  • Those modes stop you from splitting high-converting and low-converting terms into campaigns with different bid strategies. A conversion is a counted sale or action.
  • When creating a campaign, select Standard, not goal-optimized. This keeps control over each setting.
  • Do not use Performance Max.
  • At the subtype step, choose Standard Shopping. You cannot change the subtype later.

Campaign Creation Without Guidance

6 min

Choosing the Setup Path

  • About 90% or more of campaigns are created without goals guidance. This gives more bid strategy options and more manual control.
  • Google's guided setup pushes one campaign type. To build an Alpha-Beta structure, choose create a campaign without goals guidance.
  • The Alpha-Beta structure uses two campaigns at two price levels. Alpha is the main one and pays more per click. Beta pays less.
  • Choose Create a campaign without goals guidance for a new campaign.
  • Campaign goals only help once the store and Google account have enough sales data. This guide puts that at six figures or more per month, or several hundred thousand dollars in sales.
  • In Google Ads, go to Campaigns, then All campaigns. Filter to Shopping campaigns.
  • The "Go to" button at the top lets you type "campaign" to jump to creation or viewing.

Conversion Goals

  • A conversion goal is an action Google counts, such as a sale.
  • Keep all conversion goals under "Use these conversion goals for campaign performance optimization" switched on.
  • The conversions must already be set up in Google Ads beforehand.
  • Leave all conversion goals enabled.

Alpha Campaign Build (Standard Shopping)

  1. In Google Ads, click Campaigns.
  2. Click New Campaign.
  3. Choose Create a campaign without guidance.
  4. Select campaign type: Shopping.
  5. Leave all conversion goals enabled.
  6. Select your connected Google Merchant Center account.
  7. Choose Standard Shopping, not Performance Max.
  8. Name it Alpha, [Store Name].
  9. Set an initial daily budget. Start at $5-$10 per day total across both campaigns. The maximum early spend is about $30 per day.
  10. Set bid strategy: Manual CPC. CPC means cost per click. Manual CPC means you set the price per click yourself.
  11. Leave Customer acquisition on default. This bids equally for new and existing customers.
  12. Set Alpha campaign priority: Low. Priority decides which campaign gets money first.
  13. Find the Networks setting. It may be under "More settings." Turn OFF Include Google Search Partners. Search Partners are other websites that show Google ads. Conversion rates are best on Google itself. This step is very important.
  14. Create one ad group named Alpha. An ad group is a folder of ads inside a campaign.
  15. Set the CPC bid to $0.40 as a starting point.
  16. Keep Products set to All products included.
  17. Click Publish campaign.
  18. After publishing, split products by Item ID (product code) inside the Alpha ad group. This lets you control bids per product later. It is optional but recommended. Splitting is called subdivision.

Beta Campaign Build (Standard Shopping)

  1. Repeat the campaign creation steps. Choose create without guidance, select Shopping, select your Merchant Center account, and choose Standard Shopping.
  2. Name it Beta, [Store Name].
  3. Set bid strategy: Manual CPC.
  4. Set the CPC bid to $0.20 as a starting point. This is about half of Alpha.
  5. Set Beta campaign priority: High.
  6. Find the Networks setting. Turn OFF Include Google Search Partners.
  7. Create one ad group named Beta.
  8. Keep Products set to All products included.
  9. Click Publish campaign.

(No year given) Beta build:

  1. Repeat steps 1-3 from the alpha campaign creation.
  2. Name it Beta Shopping [Category] [LowerROAS].
  3. Set bidding strategy to Manual CPC.
  4. Set campaign priority to High (opposite of Alpha).
  5. Set starting bid to $0.20 (lower than Alpha).
  6. Set target ROAS lower than Alpha (e.g., 2.5x vs 3.3x). ROAS means sales earned per dollar of ad spend. Target ROAS is the return you aim for.
  7. Add negative keywords for branded terms (e.g., "lifestyles" as phrase match). Branded terms are searches with your brand name. Phrase match blocks the words in that order. This sends those searches to Alpha instead.
  8. Add universal negative keywords.
  9. Save the campaign.

** Full build in the order used in this guide, Alpha and Beta:

  1. In Google Ads, go to Campaigns and click the blue New campaign button.
  2. Select Create a campaign without goals guidance, then select Shopping.
  3. Select the linked Merchant Center account and the one country you sell to (for a US store, United States).
  4. Select Standard Shopping campaign and click Continue.
  5. Name the Beta campaign (for example beta all products shopping). Leave Additional settings at their defaults.
  6. Under Bidding and budget, select Manual CPC and uncheck Enhanced CPC. Enhanced CPC lets Google change your bid automatically.
  7. Enter the daily budget. Use the same amount for both campaigns, for example $15 or about $20.
  8. Set Campaign priority to High for Beta.
  9. Under Targeting, keep the Google search network. Uncheck Include search partners, YouTube, Gmail and Discover. Set the location to the single country.
  10. Set the start date to as soon as possible. Leave the end date empty. Click Save and continue.
  11. Keep the ad group type on Product shopping. Name the ad group beta all products.
  12. Enter the bid per click for Beta, for example $0.20.
  13. Click Save. On the ad group page, click the plus next to Add subdivision. Choose Item ID. Tick the box to select all products. Click Continue to edit bids, then Save.
  14. Create the Alpha campaign the same way. Name it alpha all products shopping. Use Manual CPC with Enhanced CPC unchecked. Use the same daily budget. Set Campaign priority to Low. Use the same targeting, location and dates.
  15. For the Alpha ad group, name it alpha all products. Enter a bid of $0.40 per click, which is double Beta's bid. Click Save, then subdivide by Item ID the same way.
  16. In the Alpha campaign, open Keywords in the sidebar. Click the plus next to Negative keywords. Add only universal negatives, terms that should never match. For a merchandise store, an example is "course" and "videos." Then save.
  17. In the Beta campaign, open Keywords. Click the plus next to Negative keywords. Add brand names, SKU numbers, popular product names, the strong product-name variants and the same universal negatives. Then save.
  18. Wait about one day for approval. Then open Ads and extensions to check the approval status. Impressions (times your ad was shown) and clicks can take a few days to start.

Setup Order Summary

  1. Create the Alpha campaign. Use: campaign without guidance, Shopping, Standard Shopping, a name like alpha... Source A merch, Low priority, Manual CPC, $0.40 CPC, Search Partners off, and Item ID subdivision on.
  2. Create the Beta campaign the same way. Use the same conversion goals, Standard Shopping, High priority, Manual CPC, $0.20 CPC, Search Partners off, and country and locations as needed.
  3. Create a shared budget under Tools > Budgets and bidding > Shared budgets. Add both campaigns. Set the daily budget to $5 to $10. Save. A shared budget is one daily money limit for several campaigns.
  4. Add negative keywords to the Beta campaign.
  5. Wait for the campaigns to be approved.

Bidding Strategy and Starting Bids

2 min

Bid Strategy

  • Manual CPC is the bid strategy in the Alpha and Beta build steps.
  • Alpha is also described with the Maximize Revenue bid strategy. This means Google tries to make the most sales. It uses aggressive bids, Low priority, negative keywords that block weak-converting terms, and universal negatives. This differs from the Manual CPC steps above.
  • Do not use Enhanced CPC. Uncheck it under Bidding and budget.

Starting Bids

Campaign Starting bid Source
Alpha $0.40 per click
Alpha (product shopping ad group) $0.50
Beta $0.20 per click (about half of Alpha)
Beta (lower than Alpha) $0.20
  • The starting manual CPC for Alpha is $0.40 (40 cents). Adjust it over time.
  • The starting CPC for Beta is half of Alpha: $0.20 (20 cents).
  • The Beta bid is always the lower of the two.
  • Alpha gets the higher bid because Alpha traffic is worth more. It is acceptable to pay more for it.
  • Give every product group the same starting bid.
  • Change bids only after performance data builds up. Bids set on guesses before data exists tend to be wrong.
  • The ad group bid starts at the campaign's starting CPC.

Target ROAS

  • Alpha example: typically produces ROAS around 15x. So start with a CPC bid of $0.40.
  • Beta example: typically produces ROAS around 8x. So start with a CPC bid of $0.20.
  • Target ROAS in alternative builds: Alpha 3.3x, Beta 2.5x (lower than Alpha).
  • Target ROAS example in naming: 3.6 for a shopping campaign for cold traffic in the USA. Cold traffic means people who do not know your store yet.

Customer Acquisition and Local Settings

  • Leave Customer acquisition on the default. It bids equally for new and existing customers.
  • Keep the default "bid equally for new and existing customers" setting. Returning customers who search for products are bid on equally.
  • Leave local products off unless the business has a physical retail location.
  • Leave EU political ads off when they do not apply. These are ads about politics in Europe.

Why Two Bid Levels

  • One budget and one campaign for all keywords means equal bids for weak and strong terms. This lowers profit.
  • Splitting into two campaigns lets most of the budget go to the higher-converting terms.
  • With a limited budget, spend goes first to the campaign with the highest return per dollar. That is Alpha.
  • Beta should still be profitable. It gets less money because its traffic is less valuable.
  • Shopping's main limit is that you cannot pick keywords or set keyword-level bids.
  • The Alpha-Beta structure works around this. You split campaigns and use negatives to funnel searches. This creates high-bid and low-bid groups, even though Google picks which searches trigger your ads.

Campaign Priority and the Alpha-Beta Logic

1 min

Priority Rules

1 min
  • Campaign priority (the order in which Google checks your campaigns) has three levels: high, medium and low. It only applies between your own campaigns, not against competitors.
  • Google checks high priority first, then medium, then low, for each search query across your account.
  • Google checks higher-priority campaigns first. Beta is set to High so that broad searches are checked first.
  • Alpha is set to Low so that searches not blocked by Beta fall through to it.
  • This is counterintuitive, because Beta is the less valuable campaign.
  • Set the Beta campaign to High priority and keep Alpha on Low (the default).
  • In a new Shopping campaign (an ad type that shows your products on Google), the Campaign priority field defaults to Low.
  • Beta must be changed to High, while Alpha stays at Low, so the intended order of checking takes effect.
  • Set the alpha campaign to low priority so Google checks it second.
  • Set the beta campaign to high priority so Google checks it first.
  • When a search matches negatives (blocked words, explained below) in the high-priority beta campaign, it funnels to the low-priority alpha campaign.
  • Set your alpha campaign priority to low and your beta campaign priority to high.
  • This counterintuitive setting ensures beta (low-converting) terms are checked first. A "low-converting" term is one that rarely turns into a sale.
  • When blocked by negatives, traffic funnels to alpha (high-converting) terms at the correct priority level. A "high-converting" term is one that often turns into a sale.
  • ** Set the campaign priority to Low for Alpha and High for Beta.
  • ** Google checks the High-priority campaign first for matching searches.

Alpha-Beta Logic

2 min
  • ** When a search query matches both campaigns, Google checks Beta first, because it has higher priority.
  • ** If the query is blocked as a negative in Beta, Google moves to Alpha.
  • ** If the query matches Alpha and is not blocked there, the ad shows with Alpha's higher bid. A bid is the most you pay for one click.
  • ** This prevents budget waste on weak keywords by reserving high-bid spend for strong keywords. A keyword is a word or phrase that triggers your ad.
  • Negative keywords (words that block your ad from showing for a search) in Beta push searches for high-converting terms into Alpha.
  • Beta is checked first as high priority and blocks the negatives.
  • Alpha, on low priority, picks up the same searches at its higher bid.
  • Negative keywords in the Beta campaign act as a filter.
  • When a search matches one of them, Google skips Beta and passes the search to the lower-priority Alpha campaign.
  • Beta negatives should be the terms most relevant to the store's own products: brand names, SKU numbers and popular product names.
  • A SKU (stock keeping unit) is a store code for one exact product.
  • These are the searches Alpha should win.
  • The inverted priority structure forces budget toward high-converting searches.
  • This blocks those searches in the high-priority Beta campaign, so Google routes them to Alpha, where you bid higher.
  • Priority of the alpha-beta structure: Beta, High, checked first; Alpha, Low, checked second.
  • Alpha holds the high-traffic campaigns.
  • Alpha blocks weak search terms with negative keywords.
  • Beta holds the keywords that still convert but are weaker.
  • Always keep a separate alpha and beta split in Shopping campaigns.
  • If only an "all brands" beta campaign exists with no alpha campaign, the setup is incorrect and must be configured properly.
  • Every product not explicitly filtered is caught by a catch-all product group at each level. A product group is a set of products you bid on together.
  • Check your product group inheritance structure carefully. Inheritance means lower groups pick up the settings of the groups above them.
  • Products you intended to separate must not be grouped together by accident.

Campaign Roles: Alpha and Beta

1 min

Alpha

1 min
  • Alpha campaigns target high-converting, branded or commercial terms, with aggressive bidding to maximize revenue and visibility.
  • A "branded" term is a search for your brand name. A "commercial" term is a search from someone ready to buy.
  • Use negative keywords to block weak or generic terms from appearing in alpha campaigns. A "generic" term is a broad word such as "umbrella".
  • Alpha targets high-intent searches: brand names, product names, and SKU/MPN numbers. High intent means the shopper is close to buying.
  • An MPN (manufacturer part number) is the code the product's maker gives it.
  • Alpha campaigns hold the high-traffic campaigns.
  • For Campaign Alpha, targeting high-converting terms: set bid strategy to Maximize Revenue (Google tries to get the most sales value for your budget).
  • For Campaign Alpha, targeting high-converting terms: apply high bids.
  • For Campaign Alpha, targeting high-converting terms: set Shopping Campaign Priority to Low.
  • For Campaign Alpha, targeting high-converting terms: add negative keywords to block low-converting terms and universal negatives. Universal negatives are words that should never match, in any campaign.
  • Alpha's Shopping campaign contains the full product set.
  • ** Alpha campaign setup:
    1. Set campaign priority to Low.
    2. Set daily budget to a higher amount (e.g., $30).
    3. Set cost-per-click bids to a higher amount. Cost per click (CPC) is what you pay each time someone clicks.
    4. Populate the campaign with only high-converting, long-tail keywords. Long-tail keywords are long, specific phrases, such as specific product model numbers or brand plus descriptor keywords.
    5. Add weak and generic terms as negative keywords to prevent budget waste.

Beta

1 min
  • Beta campaigns target low-converting, generic or informational search terms, with low bidding to capture the remaining relevant traffic economically.
  • Use negative keywords to block high-converting branded terms so they funnel to the alpha campaign instead.
  • Beta targets broader, lower-intent searches. Lower intent means the shopper is still browsing.
  • For example, general niche terms such as surfboards.
  • Beta holds the keywords that still convert but are weaker.
  • For Campaign Beta, targeting low-converting terms: apply low bids (lower than Campaign Alpha).
  • For Campaign Beta, targeting low-converting terms: set Shopping Campaign Priority to High.
  • For Campaign Beta, targeting low-converting terms: add negative keywords to block high-converting terms from Campaign Alpha and universal negatives.
  • ** Beta setup:
    1. Set campaign priority to High (Google checks this campaign first for matching searches).
    2. Set daily budget to a lower amount (e.g., $17).
    3. Set cost-per-click bids to lower amounts.
    4. Include weak but still relevant keywords that might convert (e.g., broader product category keywords, variations).
    5. Add strong and high-converting keywords as negative keywords so they are captured by the Alpha campaign instead.

Worked Example (2019)

1 min
  • ** For standup desks, Hampton blue umbrella SKU 123 is a strong keyword (Alpha).
  • ** The generic umbrella is blocked in Alpha as a negative keyword.
  • ** In Beta, the generic term is allowed with a low bid.
  • ** If someone searches umbrella, Beta captures it at low cost-per-click.
  • ** If they search the exact Hampton blue umbrella SKU 123, it is blocked in Beta and goes to Alpha with the high bid.

Alpha-Beta Procedure (2020)

1 min

**

  1. Create an alpha campaign with your broad product feed. A product feed is the file listing your products that you send to Google.
  2. Create a separate beta campaign with high-priority, high-intent keywords.
  3. Set negative keywords at the ad group level (not the campaign level) to eliminate irrelevant searches from the alpha. An ad group is a folder inside a campaign that holds products and bids.
  4. Beta is checked first by Google before alpha routes traffic.
  5. Refine the alpha over time as you identify underperforming keywords.

Splitting Proven and Untested Products (2020)

1 min
  • ** Use alpha campaigns for products that already sell and beta campaigns for testing.
  • ** Exclude the proven products from beta so new products get their own budget.
  • Split Shopping campaigns into an alpha campaign for proven products and search terms that convert well, and a beta campaign for weaker or untested segments.
  • Bid higher in the alpha campaign and lower in the beta campaign.

Negative Keywords and Routing

1 min

Routing Rules

1 min
  • Do not manually move keywords between alpha and beta campaigns.

The Waterfall Structure

1 min
  • A new Google Shopping campaign starts with all products in one group at the same bid.
  • This is a problem once the catalog (your full product list) holds many products, so the group should be split.
  • The waterfall keeps the structure in layers: all products, then brand, then item ID.
  • Each lower layer can receive its own bid once enough data exists for that product.
  • So bidding can be tuned down to the individual SKU.

Waterfall Procedure

1 min
  1. Open the shopping campaign, where all products sit in one group with the same bid.
  2. Click the plus sign to add a subdivision. A subdivision splits a product group into smaller groups.
  3. Choose the split dimension: product category, brand, item ID or custom label. A custom label is a tag you add yourself to a product.
  4. Split by brand first, selecting the brands you want, and then split further by item ID if needed.
  5. Set the same starting bid for every group.

  7. As data arrives for each product, adjust the bid per SKU.

The Google Shopping Funnel: Overview

2 min

What the Funnel Is

  • For high-ticket products (expensive items), the only Google Shopping structure described as consistently effective is search query level bidding. This is also called the shopping funnel. It means setting bids on the actual words people type. **
  • Other Shopping approaches are generally built for lower-ticket (cheaper) products. **
  • Search query level bidding also goes by the names alpha beta and inverted funnel. These are informal labels for the same strategy. **
  • Use the official name when you search for or compare sources. **
  • Standard Google Shopping ads promote one product at a time. **
  • So broad searches at the top of the funnel (people still exploring) are rarely ready to consider a single product. **
  • Standard Shopping ads suit only middle and bottom of funnel searches (people closer to buying). **
  • The alpha-beta structure is the answer to Shopping's main limit. You cannot pick keywords or set keyword-level bids.
  • By splitting campaigns and using negatives to steer searches, you create high-bid and low-bid keyword groups. Google still picks which searches trigger your ads.
  • Use two Shopping campaigns.
  • Alpha is for high-converting terms: brand names, product names, and SKU numbers (your product's own stock code).
  • Beta is for broad, generic, lower-converting terms.
  • So you can set bids differently for each group of keywords.
  • Use two Shopping campaigns that both contain the full product set.
  • Alpha catches the highest-converting searches: brand names, product names, SKU numbers. Beta catches broader, lower-converting searches.
  • Products are not split between them.
  • Only the search terms each campaign catches differ. Negative keywords enforce that split.
  • Create two campaigns, Alpha and Beta.
  • Google Shopping ads use product feed data (titles, descriptions and site content), not positive keywords (words you choose to trigger your ads).
  • Google decides which searches show your ads.
  • The two-campaign structure lets you control bids indirectly, with negative keywords.
  • High-intent searches (people ready to buy) go into Alpha, with higher bids.
  • Broad searches go into Beta, with lower bids.
  • Shopping campaigns are split into two parts to separate high and low bids.
  • This is the core of the funnel.

The Funnel in Sequence

  1. Google reads the product feed sent through Merchant Center.
  2. Beta (High priority) is checked first. It blocks searches that match its negatives.
  3. Searches blocked by Beta fall through to Alpha (Low priority), where the higher bid applies.
  4. Searches that match neither negative stay in Beta at the lower bid.
  5. Converting search terms are moved into Alpha by negative exact keywords (negative keywords that block one exact phrase).
  6. Judge each campaign on conversion rate and ROAS together.

Alpha-Beta Example

  • Alpha example: high-intent searches (brand names, product names, SKU/MPN numbers) produce ROAS around 15x. MPN means manufacturer part number, the brand's own product code.
  • So start Alpha with a CPC bid of $0.40.
  • Beta example: broader, lower-intent searches (general niche terms such as surfboards) produce ROAS around 8x.
  • So start Beta with a CPC bid of $0.20, about half of Alpha.
  • Beta negatives push high-intent terms to Alpha.
  • Example: a store that sells several brands lists each brand name, SKU number and bestselling product name as Beta negatives.

2 min

  • For example, raise a cost per click from 0.20 USD to 0.25 USD on a product that needs more impressions. Older advice (2020).
  • To change bids per product, split the catalog into product groups (sets of products) so each product or brand can get its own cost per click. Older advice (2021).
  • Raise the bid only on products that get impressions but too few clicks, or that have low visibility. Older advice (2021).
  • A product with no impressions should be checked for search demand, relevance and competition before its bid is raised.

Product Groups and Item ID Subdivision

1 min

Subdivision Options

  • When you add a subdivision (splitting a group into smaller parts) to a Shopping ad group, the options are product type, category, brand and Item ID.
  • Choose Item ID (your product's own ID number) to break the ad group down by individual product.
  • Each product then gets its own reporting row and its own bid.
  • In Google Shopping campaigns, each product group corresponds to one product. Older advice (2020).
  • In standard Shopping campaigns, place each product in its own product group so impressions, clicks and sales are visible per product. Older advice (2022).
  • Performance Max does not show product-level data. Older advice (2022).
  • Split product groups by individual product so spend and return are visible per product in Google Ads. Older advice (2020).

The Everything Else Node

  • When a Shopping product group is subdivided, Google Ads adds an Everything else node (an extra catch-all box). Older advice (2021-22).
  • It is set to Excluded and carries no bid. Older advice (2021-22).
  • Products that match none of the named subdivisions fall into this node. Older advice (2021-22).
  • They do not show. Older advice (2021-22).
  • So place every product in a labelled group. Older advice (2021-22).
  • Check the product count in the Everything Else group inside All Products. Older advice (2024).
  • If all products are split out at product level, that group should be empty. Older advice (2024).
  • Check whether an All products product group sits next to individually listed products in the same ad group. Older advice (2020).
  • If it does, pause the All products group, so products are not counted twice. Older advice (2020).

Launch

1 min
  • Launch campaigns with the full product catalog (your complete product list) in one Alpha and one Beta campaign.
  • Then remove products based on data as clicks and conversions come in.
  • Do not pre-select products before there is data.
  • Start Google Shopping campaigns with the full product catalog as soon as the products are uploaded and the feed is set up. Older advice (2020).
  • Driving traffic to the homepage with broad keywords is expensive and brings the least qualified buyers. Older advice (2020).
  • Keep ad structure simple at the start, with all products in the Alpha and Beta campaigns. Older advice (2020).
  • Negative keywords and ad group bid adjustments can be added after the store has data. Older advice (2020).
  • Launch three core campaigns on Google Ads first, before any social traffic: product listing ads (PLAs), search text ads (text ads on Google search results) on profitable terms, and search audience campaigns with retargeting. Older advice (2019 onward, undated source).
  • Once they work on Google, copy them over to Bing Ads (Microsoft's ad platform). Older advice (2019 onward, undated source).

Google Shopping Funnel and Process Overview

2 min
  1. Connect your store to Google Ads (Google's paid ad tool). Shopping ads (product ads with a photo and price) are then made automatically. They use your product name, price and store name. No copywriting (writing ad text) is needed.
  2. Submit the feed (your product data file that Google reads). Google reads product titles, descriptions and attributes (product details such as color or size). It uses them to decide which searches your products match. Searches are matched by keywords (the words people type). It uses the Merchant Center product pages (Google's product data tool) for matching keywords.
  3. Launch the full catalog (all your products) in one alpha and one beta campaign. Alpha and beta are two campaigns, each a set of ads with its own rules. Set the shared budget (one money pool both campaigns use) and the priority (which campaign is chosen first when two match the same search) correctly.
  4. Build the PLA (product listing ads, Shopping ads with a product photo and price) alpha/beta pattern. Run two campaigns per PLA setup. The alpha campaign uses negative keywords (search words that block your ads) to block lower-converting search terms (searches that rarely lead to a sale). The beta campaign receives those terms. Every PLA campaign is typically duplicated this way.
  5. Compare channels (the places where you advertise). In the owner's Google campaigns, Shopping converted much better than Search (text ads on Google's results page). "Converted" means the visitor bought. Conversion rate is the share of visitors who buy. Shopping had a strong conversion rate. For a store running both, Google Shopping is the channel to scale first, which means grow its budget first.
  6. Optimize with data (your real results). Remove products that do not perform. Split product groups (sets of products grouped together) by Item ID (the unique number of each product). Adjust bids (the price you pay per click) on products that need impressions (how often your ad is shown). Add negative keywords for unwanted searches.
  7. Copy proven campaigns to Bing Ads (Microsoft's ad tool) after they work on Google.

Each PLA setup also follows the long-tail principle (aiming at longer, more specific search phrases). Product listing ads mostly target long-tail keywords, and the alpha and beta structure applies.

Where Sources Disagree

2 min
  • Full catalog at launch or product-level split (one group per product) from the start:
    • Launch with the full catalog in alpha and beta. Then split products by individual product once data arrives.
    • Older 2020 advice gives a per-product bid split from the outset.
    • Apply the full-catalog launch first.
    • Apply the per-product split when there is data to justify each product's bid.
  • Brand split or product split:
    • Source B's older default splits Shopping campaigns by brand within three priority levels (high, medium and low). Split by price, margin (profit per sale) or product type only when there is a specific reason for that product group.
    • Source A's guidance splits standard Shopping by individual product so that performance is visible per product.
    • A brand split is also named as a valid option for bid control.
    • Use the brand split where the three-priority-level structure is in use.
    • Use the per-product split for standard Shopping.
  • Number of campaign tiers (levels):
    • The default is two tiers, alpha and beta. A three-tier structure also works.
    • The standard Shopping setup is named as Generic, Brand and SKU (a stock code for each single product).
    • The three priority levels named by Source B are also an option.
    • The basic starting setup of four campaigns adds remarketing campaigns (ads shown again to people who already visited) on Google and Meta (the company behind Facebook and Instagram).
    • Start with two tiers. Add the others as the account needs them.
  • Negative keywords:
    • Negative keywords are added to both campaigns from the start.
    • Adding many negative keywords can block searches that would have converted, so they should be used with care.
    • Negative keywords and bid adjustments can wait until there is data.
  • Smart Shopping or smart campaigns:
    • Smart Shopping (an older automatic campaign type) is phased out and should not be used.
    • Smart campaigns, offered on the goal screen (the first setup page where you choose a goal), are described as suitable for beginners.
    • Advanced advertisers should use custom campaigns (campaigns where you set every option yourself).
  • Starting bid examples:
    • The 2026 example uses $1.00 for alpha and $0.50 for beta. Its starting range is $0.50 to $1.00 CPC (cost per click, what you pay each time someone clicks your ad).
    • The 2020 example raises one product's cost per click from 0.20 USD to 0.25 USD.
    • These are examples for different situations. Neither is a fixed rule.
