Unit economics before spending: break-even, target ROAS and budget

Section 10.2 · 2 of 23 in this chapter · 25 min read

In short

  • Know your break-even (the sales level where you earn back exactly what you spend) and your target cost per sale before you spend on ads.
  • Keep ROAS (sales money back for each dollar of ads) at 10x or higher for Google Shopping, or you lose money.
  • Products under $200 are usually too cheap to run Google Shopping ads on profitably.
  • Start small, at $5 to $30 per day, and give Google 2 to 4 weeks to learn.
  • Only advertise products whose margin (money you keep per sale) covers the ads, and whose brand enforces MAP (the lowest price sellers may advertise).

Why the Numbers Come Before the Spend

2 min
  • Starting ads without knowing your target cost per sale usually leads to overspending. It also means you take home less profit than planned. Calculate the break-even and target CPA before the campaign spends money. Break-even is the point where sales pay back exactly what you spend, with no profit and no loss. Target CPA (cost per acquisition, here the cost of one sale) is the most you can pay in ads for one sale.
  • The data you need before scaling paid traffic is the break-even and target cost per sale, per product or product group. Scaling means spending more on what works. Paid traffic means visitors you pay for with ads. Without these answers, you cannot tell when a channel can take more budget or when it must be reduced.
  • ** Compare the actual CPA, ROAS and CPC of running campaigns with the break-even and target figures. CPC is the cost of one click. The gap shows whether to scale up a paid channel, keep it, or pull it back.
  • ** Bid and budget changes must be checked against the target cost per sale and target ROAS calculated beforehand. A bid is the price you pay for one click. Without these targets, bid changes are guesses. Guessing with ad money usually loses money.
  • ** Before raising bids or spend, check that the new bid is still profitable at the conversion rate you are actually getting. Conversion rate is the share of visitors who buy. Assuming that more traffic will convert at the same or a better rate is risky and rarely holds.
  • ** Most sales in this model come from direct-response Google Ads. These are ads that send shoppers straight to a product page. Each product's ad spend must stay profitable after shipping cost and merchant fees (payment fees charged by the payment company), or the sale should not be advertised in that region.
  • When starting Google Ads campaigns, the main goal is to buy data. Find which search terms (words people type into Google) and products reach your target 1% conversion rate before scaling budget.
  • In an ideal case, each keyword gets at least 100 clicks and each product gets at least 100 visitors before you make scaling decisions. A keyword is a search word that triggers your ad.

Break-Even ROAS and Target Cost per Sale

1 min
  • Run Google Ads at at or above your break-even ROAS to avoid losses. ROAS (return on ad spend) is the sales money you get back for each dollar spent on ads. Below break-even ROAS, you lose money on ads even when sales happen. Running above break-even creates profit to reinvest or keep.
  • Use the break-even ROAS as the reference for setting Google Ads bids. ROAS is also a column in Google Ads that helps you decide how high to bid. If you use a paid advertising agency (a company that runs ads for you), give them this number as the target for optimizing your campaigns.
  • ** The goal of bidding by product is to set the allowed ad spend per sale based on gross profit (money left after product cost). For example, spend up to 50% of gross profit to get one sale. Higher-priced products with the same margin can therefore justify higher bids.
  • ** Some competitive niches (crowded product markets) need about $150 to $250 to start a Google Product Listing Ad campaign. A Product Listing Ad is a Google ad that shows your product's picture and price. Budget for this before launching in a crowded category.

ROAS Formula and the 10x Benchmark

2 min
  • ROAS (return on ad spend) = revenue from ads / ad spend. A target of 10x means 100 dollars of ad spend should return 1,000 dollars in revenue.
  • Maximum acceptable ad cost per order = average order value (AOV, the average amount one customer spends per order) divided by target ROAS. Example: an average order value of 1,000 dollars at a 10x ROAS allows up to 100 dollars of ad cost per sale.
  • ** For a strong Google Shopping campaign, target at least 10x return on ad spend within two to five days of the click.
  • Target ROAS of 10x: every 1 dollar of ad spend should return at least 10 dollars in sales. This target applies after the first two to three weeks of the campaign learning phase (the time Google needs to learn), not on day one.
  • ** Minimum ROAS threshold: maintain at least 10x return on ad spend to achieve profitability. Scale upward gradually (increase spend slowly) while keeping ROAS above 10x.
  • ** Keep at least 10:1 ROAS, meaning 10 USD back for each dollar spent, while raising the budget. Increase spend for as long as the ratio holds.
  • For Google Shopping, the baseline target is a blended ROAS of at least 10x across all campaigns. Blended means one average for all campaigns together. Products priced too low for their cost per sale fall below this target and need optimizing (improving) first.
  • ** The ROAS target for Google Shopping is at least 10x. If an agency runs category campaigns instead of Alpha and Beta, judge them only by ROAS against this minimum. Alpha and Beta are two Google Shopping campaigns, explained below.
  • The 10x ROAS target and the ad-cost rule of thumb (ad cost per sale equals one tenth of the order value) were given specifically for Google Ads. Treat them as a Google Ads benchmark (a reference standard), not a universal target for all ad platforms.
  • (2023) The minimum target ROAS stayed at 10X.
  • ** Goal is 10x return on ad spend, which should be reachable within a few weeks with minor campaign optimization.
  • ** Maximum ad spend per sale = 10 percent of the sale price. Example: a $1,500 sofa allows up to $150 of ad cost to earn one sale.
  • ** At a cost per click (CPC) of $1, a $150 budget buys 150 clicks. If those clicks produce 2 sales, the ad cost per sale is $75. If only one sale results, the cost per sale doubles.
  • ** Conversion rates (the share of visitors who buy) and click costs vary by product. Use the 10 percent ceiling as a starting point, and adjust bids with real campaign data.

Campaign-Level ROAS Targets

1 min
  • Target ROAS for the Alpha campaign is around 15X (about $15 revenue per $1 of ad spend). For the Beta campaign it is around 8X ($8 per $1). Alpha bids are set higher because its keywords convert better.
  • The ROAS figures for the two Shopping campaigns (about 15X for the high-converting Alpha campaign and about 8X for the broader Beta campaign) are planning benchmarks. They show how much revenue each dollar of ad spend should bring back. They justify setting different bids per campaign. They are not entered as a target in a Manual CPC setup, where you set each click price yourself.
  • ** Google Shopping with about 5x ROAS should receive most of the budget. Facebook retargeting (showing ads to past visitors) below 1x ROAS should be cut or stopped, since it overspends on people who are already reachable.
  • (2020 example) For the 15x example in the scaling procedure, see Scaling Rules.
  • Source conflict: Source C (an e-commerce mentoring program) gives a 3x to 4x target ROAS for a Google Shopping start with a $500 to $1,500 starting budget. The Source A sources above set 10x as the minimum for Google Shopping. Both values are given here because they come from different sources and setups. Use 10x as the profitability floor (the lowest level that still makes profit). Check which setup you are running before applying 3x to 4x.

Ad-Spend Ceilings: The 10% Rules

1 min
  • Set maximum PPC ad spend (pay-per-click, where you pay for each click) at no more than 10 percent of total monthly revenue. Example: $25,000 monthly revenue allows a maximum of $2,500 PPC spend. $2,500 monthly revenue allows a maximum of $250 PPC spend.
  • ** Cap ad spend at 10% of MAP per sale. MAP (minimum advertised price) is the lowest price a brand lets sellers advertise. For a $1,000 MAP product, the ad budget per sale should not exceed about $100.
  • (2023) A sales budget of 300 dollars per day for about 1,100 sessions (visits to your site) was given as a high but acceptable amount of clicks, if they convert.
  • With an average order value of $1,000, you could spend up to about $100 in ads to get one sale. Even so, start with a $5 to $30 daily budget and let Google learn, rather than spending $100 per day.
  • ** Use a 90/10 budget split: 90% of the ad budget goes to Google Shopping (alpha beta) and 10% to remarketing (ads to past visitors). Google Shopping typically performs better on new traffic than other channels.

Price Thresholds ($200 Minimum)

1 min
  • Products priced under $200 are generally not worth running Google Shopping ads for, because reaching a 10x ROAS on them is very difficult. Use $200 as the minimum price for products that receive ad spend. Products near $197 are borderline.
  • Lower-priced products are hard to make profitable under a 10x ROAS target, because ad cost per sale must stay low. Shops that want paid traffic should avoid sales prices far below a few hundred dollars.
  • Before setting up Shopping ads, list every product in the store priced above $200. Check which ones are not yet selling. Products already above the threshold are the first candidates for ad spend.
  • Excluding unprofitable products from Shopping campaigns also reduces negative keyword work. A negative keyword is a search word that blocks your ad. An excluded product cannot be triggered by a search term and cannot spend money. Fixing the product set first removes a source of wasted spend before search terms need review.
  • Starting a new Google Shopping campaign at a low daily budget, such as $10 per day, is acceptable. Low monthly spend alone should not be read as failure, because the cause may be bids or product prices rather than the budget.

Margin Screening

1 min
  • Products without a 2X wholesale margin get organic traffic only. Organic traffic means free visitors, not paid ads. Wholesale is the price you pay your supplier. A 2X wholesale margin means the sale price is at least double the wholesale price. Ads should focus on products where wholesale is at most half the sale price. **
  • Run ads only for products where the margin can cover ad spend. Do not advertise low-margin products. Negotiate better prices or drop those products. **
  • Advertise every product where your margin supports the cost of ads. Remove products from suppliers that do not enforce MAP (make sellers keep the minimum price). They will not perform. **
  • Products with a small margin should be excluded from paid campaigns when ad spend would be unprofitable. High-margin products keep their paid campaigns.
  • Do not spend ad budget on products where competitors sell at unprofitable prices, below your 2x wholesale minimum. Example: with a $500 wholesale cost, your minimum price is $1,000. If competitors sell at $800 (20% below your $1,000 price), your ads would likely not convert against those cheaper offers. Convert means turning a visitor into a buyer.
  • Check Google Shopping listings to see what competitors charge for the same product. Compare those prices with your 2x wholesale minimum before setting your sale price or deciding whether to run paid ads.

What to Do with Products You Cannot Sell Profitably Against Competitors

1 min
  1. List the product on your store at your full minimum price of 2x wholesale.
  2. Let it be found through free traffic sources rather than paid ads.
  3. Do not run paid ads for it while it appears next to competitors selling at unprofitable prices.

MAP Screening

1 min
  • Run Google Ads only on products from suppliers that actively enforce MAP policies. MAP (minimum advertised price) is the lowest price a brand allows sellers to advertise. Enforcing means the brand checks and stops sellers who go below it. Do not allocate ad spend to products from suppliers without MAP enforcement. Competitors will price below your cost and remove your profit. **
  • Products whose sellers price below the brand's MAP, which removes your margin, should be excluded from Google Shopping campaigns. Suppliers do not all enforce MAP, so check enforcement before advertising a product.
  • For products with MAP restrictions, use asterisks and exclusions on the /promotions page to list brands that cannot be discounted. An exclusion means leaving an item out of the sale. This satisfies supplier agreements while still running promotions to your full warm audience (people who already know your shop).
  • If a MAP-compliant supplier also sells on marketplaces such as eBay, Amazon, or Walmart at MAP, you may list them on your site. Do not pay for ads to them. Rely only on organic traffic for those sales. **

Clicks Needed per Sale

1 min
  • At 2.5% conversion, two and a half sales come from every 100 targeted visitors.
  • A quality-traffic goal of 2.5% from Google Shopping means at 300 clicks you should expect at least 6 to 7 sales, possibly up to 10.
  • ** About 100 unique clicks (clicks from different people) should produce around 2 sales within about 3 days at the 2.5% goal.
  • Increase the Google Ads budget until the store gets about 100 targeted clicks per week, which should lead to roughly 2 to 3 sales. Count clicks across all campaigns combined. **
  • ** Traffic check: if this produces about 75 unique clicks per day at $1 CPC, with the 2.5% target conversion rate, the store can generate consistent revenue.
  • ** Traffic consistency check: for a store to run consistently, roughly 75 qualified unique clicks per day at about 2.5% conversion is enough traffic in the beginning. Combine this with remarketing to win back visitors who did not buy.

Learning-Speed Math

1 min
  • A lower daily budget takes proportionally longer to gather enough click and sales data for optimization decisions. A $5 per day budget needs six times as long as $30 per day to produce the same data (30 divided by 5 is 6).
  • A budget of 15 dollars per day roughly doubles the time needed to collect enough data for decisions, compared with $30 per day.

Conversion-Rate Benchmarks

1 min

  • Target conversion rate from Google Shopping ads is 2.5%, meaning two and a half sales per 100 targeted visitors. **
  • Google Ads conversion goal for the store is a 2.5% conversion rate. About 100 unique clicks should produce around 2 sales within about 3 days. **
  • A typical e-commerce site (online shop) running Google Ads should expect a conversion rate of roughly 1% to 2%. A store with a lower rate, such as 0.5%, can still be profitable if its lifetime value (total money one customer spends over time) or cart value is higher. The acceptable rate depends on the business economics.
  • As a rough benchmark, the buy-ready segment at the bottom of the funnel (people ready to buy) converts at about 1% to 3%. Check this figure against your own account data before planning budgets.
  • Conversion-rate planning figures in the sources differ: 1% (target for a $1,000 monthly start), 2% (example store), and 2.5% (Google Shopping goal). Use the figure that matches your own account data. Treat 2.5% as the goal and 1% to 2% as the typical range.

Scaling Rules

2 min
  • Start Google Ads with small budgets and optimize every two weeks. Starting with large budgets and letting campaigns run without tweaks is what causes thousands of dollars to be spent with zero conversions. (2024)
  • Start Google Shopping campaigns with small initial budgets. Scale spending upward only as performance data proves profitability.
  • Buy data first: the starting budget is for collecting data on which products get clicks and sales, not for scaling.
  • ** Starting with a small budget is for collecting data: impressions, clicks and purchases show which products people find and buy. Use that data to add negatives and adjust cost per click per product, then increase budget once return on ad spend improves a few days after optimizing.
  • Scale the budget gradually with ROAS above 10x, and only as profitable campaigns prove the ratio.
  • Products with a strong MAP-to-wholesale spread and good conversion are the only ones that should receive increased spend.
  • The scaling procedure for Google Ads budgets is:
    1. Set a target ROAS for the current budget, for example 15x on $2,000 spend.
    2. Improve efficiency until that target holds.
    3. Only then scale the budget, expecting ROAS to fall somewhat as spend grows.
  • Increase the budget as long as ROAS holds; the ratio is the signal to raise spend.
  • Add money in steps: after the first 2 to 4 weeks of learning, judge the campaign on the data and do not scale on day one.
  • The learning phase for the ROAS target is the first two to three weeks of a campaign.
  • Use the 2019 approach: scale up the campaigns that are profitable while cutting the ones that are not.
  • Keep the test budget unchanged until it is profitable, apply the Google Ads optimizations, and only then scale the budget by reinvesting profits from existing sales. (2025)
  • Scale spending for a product only after it proves profitable and reinvest store profits into ads once the free credits are used.
  • The budget for the second half of the first month is raised only after conversions appear, not before.
  • Budget can increase with a profitable ROAS, with no upward limit set in the 2023 source.

Bonus Offers and Conversion Cost

1 min
  • A bonus gift can lower advertising cost, not only raise sales. When the bonus removes the reason to compare prices, buyers purchase on their first visit instead of leaving, searching again and clicking a paid ad a second time, so the cost per sale falls.
  • You pay for every click in Google Ads, including clicks from visitors who never buy. Irrelevant or poorly matched traffic therefore costs money with no sales in return.
  • Irrelevant and wasted spend is reduced by excluding unprofitable products and by adding negatives after data is collected.
