How Google Ads works and where it fits

Section 10.1 · 1 of 23 in this chapter · 47 min read

In short

  • Google Ads shows paid ads to people who are already searching for what you sell.
  • You pay per click. A better Quality Score (Google's rating of how relevant and clickable your ad is) lowers what each click costs.
  • For dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships to your customer), Shopping ads are the main format.
  • If few people click your ad, fix the ad before you raise your bids.
  • Use Google Ads as one traffic source, not the only one.
More key points
  • Google Shopping puts your product next to the same product from other sellers. Price, title, shop name, reviews and delivery decide who gets the click.
  • Google works best for products people already search for. It fits expensive products best, roughly 1,000 USD and up.
  • Start with Google Ads only if the product already sells somewhere else or people already search for it. Test small first.
  • Do not follow Google's advice without checking it. Its suggestions usually ask you to spend more.
  • Check your competitors and their prices in the country you sell to before you launch.
  • Google Ads is usually the steadier paid channel than Facebook or TikTok, but it takes time to learn.
  • Your ad's position and cost depend on your bid and your Quality Score (Google's rating of how relevant your ad is). Raising your CTR (click-through rate, the share of viewers who click) is the first fix.
  • Start with Shopping ads and retargeting (showing ads again to people who already visited). Plain search ads come later, once your data supports them.
  • Judge an ad by the visitors it brings and whether they buy, not by the ad alone.
  • Do not pay an outside agency until your own ads are profitable.

Definition and Scope

1 min
  • Google Ads is a customizable, self-serve advertising platform. Self-serve means you set it up and run it yourself.
  • It includes search ads and the Google Display Network, a large group of partner websites, apps and videos where ads can appear.
  • The Display Network reaches many internet users beyond search results.
  • It is a pay-per-placement platform. Advertisers bid to show text, banners, videos, product listings or app install promotions.
  • A store owner pays to be seen by people who are already searching for or browsing related products.
  • Pay-per-click (PPC) means the advertiser pays each time a user clicks an ad.
  • PPC is the paid format used for search and shopping campaigns. A campaign is a group of ads with its own goal and budget.
  • Google Ads reaches people who are actively searching for solutions.
  • So it taps existing demand (people who already want something) rather than creating awareness.
  • It places you where people are already looking for what you sell.
  • The core of Google Ads is showing the right ad to the right person at the right time.
  • Getting these three elements right puts an advertiser well ahead of competitors.
  • Google Ads gives complete cost transparency, meaning you can see clearly what you spend.
  • You can see how much revenue each campaign generates.
  • This gives you full control of spending and profitability. Profitability means whether you make money after all costs.
  • Google Ads fits this model because spend per sale is small compared to the product price.
  • It can work for any product. It works best when the product's margin (the money left after the product cost) covers the ad budget.

Channels, Campaign Types and Targeting

1 min
  • Google Ads serves on three main channels.
  • Channel 1: text search results on Google search.
  • Channel 2: Product Listing Ads (PLA, ads that show a product photo and price) through your Merchant Center (Google's account that holds your product data), using your Google Shopping data.
  • Channel 3: the Google Display Network, including YouTube, Gmail and many other sites.
  • Campaign types include Shopping Campaigns, Search Campaigns, Video Campaigns and campaigns on the Google Display Network.
  • Eight targeting options (ways to choose who sees your ad) are available.
  • The eight are: keywords for search (the words a user types); website and placement targeting for display; demographics; audience interests and life events; topics; YouTube videos and channels; customer match; and remarketing (showing ads again to people who already visited).
  • The four Google traffic methods (ways visitors reach your store) work as one integrated system.
  • Organic search provides free, high-intent traffic. High intent means the visitor is close to buying.
  • Product listings add free visibility.
  • Shopping ads deliver scalable paid traffic with maximum control.
  • Remarketing recaptures interested visitors who did not buy.
  • Google Organic Search is free traffic from visitors who click non-paid text search results.
  • Most clicks concentrate on the first page of Google results.
  • Google Ads is one traffic source among several, never the only one.
  • Once the account is running, the next step is to add other marketing channels.
  • Google Ads should not be the only channel.
  • Use it as the starting point. Get campaigns running and producing results.
  • Then add other traffic channels that complement the Google Ads account.

The Auction

1 min
  • Google Ads runs an auction (a contest between advertisers) for every search query (the words a user types).
  • Advertisers place bids (the price they offer per click) to have their ads shown for specific queries.
  • Competing advertisers bid on the same queries at the same time.
  • The auction decides the order of ads on the results page: which ad appears first, second, third and so on.
  • Ads are ranked for each query individually. The same advertiser can hold different positions for different queries.
  • Google decides which search terms your ads are eligible for, based on the product data you supply.
  • Google's auction is a second-price auction, not a typical auction where the highest bidder pays their own bid.

Second-Price Logic

1 min
  • The winning advertiser pays just above the next competitor's ranking.
  • It does not pay the full amount of its own maximum bid.
  • The standard cost-per-click (CPC, what you pay for one click) formula for the second-price auction uses Ad Rank (your position score, which is your bid times your Quality Score).
actual cost per click = (Ad Rank of the advertiser directly below you ÷ your Quality Score) + $0.01
Ad Rank = maximum bid × Quality Score
  • A higher Quality Score lowers the price you pay for the same position.
  • Approximate cost per click: take the Ad Rank of the bidder directly below you, divide by your own Quality Score, then add $0.01.
  • You never pay more than your maximum bid. The actual price depends on the competitors around you.
  • Worked example: the bidder with Ad Rank 32 sits below a bidder with Quality Score 10, giving 32 / 10 + $0.01 = $3.21 per click.
  • Do not assume you pay your full bid per click, as you would at a house or product auction.
  • Your actual cost depends on the competitor below you and your Quality Score.
  • So a high bid with a low Quality Score can still cost more per click than necessary.

Ad Rank and Position

1 min
  • Ad Rank = maximum bid × Quality Score.
  • The highest Ad Rank takes the top position. A bidder with a strong Quality Score can outrank a higher bidder.
  • Google earns money when people click ads, not when they buy on the advertiser's site.
  • For this reason, it rewards ads that people want to click with a higher Quality Score.
  • A higher ad position brings more traffic than a lower one, regardless of competitors.
  • A bidder with a well-optimized Quality Score can therefore get more clicks while bidding less than the competition.
  • Google and Facebook prefer ads that get clicked.
  • So they reward advertisers whose ads perform well with cheaper clicks. Their quality metrics exist to enforce this.
  • Google Ads uses Quality Score, Facebook uses Relevance Score, and Amazon also uses a quality score.
  • All three measure how well an ad matches what people want and how often they click it.
  • A bidder with a very low Quality Score can have the highest bid and still rank low.
  • A bidder who bids too little with a strong Quality Score can miss the top results entirely.
  • Ad rank historically combined the bid with a bundle of factors known as Quality Score.
  • A competitor with a higher Quality Score can rank above you with a lower bid.
  • A lower Quality Score forces you to bid much higher to move up.
  • Ad rank and Quality Score cannot be measured directly in the Google Ads interface.
  • Any change to them has to be tested and judged from indirect results, because no one knows the exact outcome.

Cost Per Click and Click Quality

1 min
  • A high Quality Score gives discounted clicks.
  • A low Quality Score triggers a penalty that raises the cost per click.
  • So Quality Score changes what you pay, not only where your ad shows.
  • Quality Score affects ad rank, not only the price paid.
  • In the example, a bidder with the highest bid but a low Quality Score was not shown.
  • A bidder with a lower bid and the strongest Quality Score took the top position.
  • A high Quality Score lets an advertiser win placement with a smaller bid.
  • Moving from a Quality Score of 5 to 6 gave about a 16% CPC discount.
  • Moving from 5 to 7 gave about a 30% discount.
  • When Quality Score rises, CPC falls.
  • So an advertiser with a higher score can bid low and still appear first and receive the most clicks.
  • Bidding high is not required to win top placement.
  • Shopping ads charge per click.
  • Cost-per-click is lowered when your ad gets a higher click-through rate (CTR, the share of people who see your ad and click it) and visitors stay longer on your site than on competitors' sites. (2023)
  • Google evaluates post-click behavior on the store: bounce rate (visitors who leave right away), time on site, add-to-cart (putting an item in the basket) and purchase. (2024)
  • Strong engagement raises Quality Score and lets ads show more for less. (2024)
  • So the conversion tag (a small code that reports sales back to Google) must be set up for tracking. (2024)
  • Shopping ads show the price in the ad, so shoppers who object to the price do not click.
  • This filters out unqualified clicks before they cost money.

Quality Score

1 min
  • Quality Score is Google's rating of how relevant your ads and landing pages are to a search query. A landing page is the page a visitor arrives on after clicking.
  • It is based on expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing page experience.
  • It affects both the price you pay per click and how competitive your ad is against others for the same query.
  • Quality Score in Google Ads is based on how relevant the ad is to the searched term.
  • It also depends on how often people click the ad.
  • It also depends on whether visitors stay on the landing page instead of leaving immediately.
  • A high score lets an advertiser rank well without outbidding everyone.
  • Quality Score depends largely on click-through rate, along with landing page quality and site experience.
  • Advertisers with a higher Quality Score compete better in the auction.
  • At equal bids they usually pay less per click within a certain range.
  • A slow website can lower Quality Score through the on-page experience factor.
  • Search text ads once showed per-keyword quality breakdowns.
  • Shopping never showed these breakdowns, though the same factors still apply.
  • Google does not publish the exact Ad Rank or Quality Score factors or their weights.
  • The bid is the main lever for search impression share (how often your ad shows out of all the times it could show).
  • Quality Score factors help make your bid more competitive.
  • Google Shopping uses its own algorithm (set of calculation rules), separate from search.
  • Google Ads shows Quality Score per keyword, with change history.
  • It does not show a Quality Score column for Shopping campaigns.
  • Google has no separate advertiser-level Quality Score visible to the advertiser. Only the keyword level is displayed.
  • Improving an existing campaign is generally better than creating a new one.
  • Campaign history and steady spend help the account.

CTR as the Main Lever

1 min
  • Google measures whether people like an ad mainly through Click-Through Rate (CTR).
  • A high CTR is the main signal of a clickable ad.
  • CTR = Clicks ÷ Impressions (impressions are the number of times your ad was shown).
  • A higher CTR increases Quality Score and lowers CPC.
  • So a higher CTR gives cheaper clicks.
  • CTR has the largest influence on Quality Score.
  • If your CTR is below the benchmark, you probably have a Quality Score problem.
  • Google measures relevance through click-through rate.
  • A low CTR, where people see the ad but do not click, lowers ad rank.
  • Google reads it as weak relevance compared with competitors.
  • Most paid-traffic problems can be traced back to CTR.
  • So start diagnosis there.
  • For Shopping, the two ad types worth optimizing most are ad engagement and Shopping campaign CTR.
  • These are the two levers to address first.
  • When an account has high bids but almost no traffic, the usual cause is that the ads are not clickable.
  • It is not that the bids are too low.
  • So fix ad quality before raising bids.
  • Raising CTR, which drives Quality Score, is therefore the main lever for cheaper clicks.

Benchmark CTR for Shopping Campaigns

1 min
  • Google does not show a Quality Score column for Shopping campaigns.
  • Use the Benchmark CTR, which reflects the average CTR of competing advertisers, to estimate the Shopping Quality Score.
  • If your CTR is below the Benchmark CTR, a Quality Score problem is likely.
  • Bidding more will not fix it.
  • In the example, the advertiser had a 0.77% CTR against a 0.94% benchmark.
  • So competitors were paying less per click for the same visibility.
  • Google shows an ad in more than one position for a query when the ad's CTR is higher than the average for that position.
  • Each position has an average CTR.
  • An ad that beats its position average gets moved up.
  • Moving up then makes its CTR even higher.

Funnel Position

3 min

Funnel Model

  • The sales funnel is an inverse pyramid (wide at the top, narrow at the bottom).
  • It has TOFU (top of funnel, awareness stage), MOFU (middle of funnel, consideration stage) and BoFU (bottom of funnel, buying stage).
  • Going down the funnel, audience size declines but purchase intent increases.
  • Apply this framework when structuring Google Ads campaigns.
  • The funnel runs Awareness, Interest, Learn, Shop, Buy.
  • Google Ads works well because its campaigns target people at the bottom of the funnel who are ready to buy.
  • The goal is to reach only those at the Buy stage.
  • Do not target people who are only researching or aware of a product.
  • Paid traffic works when the right ad reaches the right customer at the right stage of the funnel.
  • Most people at the top are only researching.
  • A larger group is comparing options.
  • Only a small share at the bottom is ready to buy now.
  • Funnel-aligned spending is more effective and profitable.
  • BoFU audiences have much higher conversion rates (the share of visitors who buy).
  • They require less budget to generate sales than spending on TOFU awareness audiences.
  • Intent maps onto the sales funnel. Low-value terms sit at the top, medium at the middle and high at the bottom.
  • Google Shopping and search text ads perform best in the middle and bottom of the funnel.
  • They perform poorly for high-ticket products at the top. High-ticket means expensive products.

Matching Message to Stage

  • Match each funnel stage with a different ad message.
  • At the top, reach people who are unaware of the product or the problem with informational content.
  • In the middle, serve people comparing options with webinars, buying guides, comparison guides and detailed video reviews.
  • At the bottom, show direct product ads to people who are ready to buy.
  • No tool currently matches the right ad to each funnel stage automatically.
  • So audience and ad segmentation (splitting people into groups) by funnel stage has to be planned and set up manually.

Where Google Sits in the Funnel

  • It is weak for top funnel because it cannot reach cold audiences (people unaware of the product).
  • Google is mostly a bottom-of-funnel platform. Its search and shopping ads are triggered by product searches.
  • Top-of-funnel testing should be done on Facebook and TikTok first.

  • It performs poorly for high-ticket businesses at the top of the funnel.
  • Spending heavily on top-of-funnel traffic makes the campaign unprofitable.
  • Google Ads are strong in the middle and bottom of the funnel but weak at the top.
  • Social media is strong at the top, and YouTube works across the whole funnel.
  • Using them together gives full coverage of the customer journey.
  • Google is strong for retargeting and for buyers who search for the product.
  • Cold traffic from Google is less scalable than Facebook, because customers must already be searching for the product.
  • Use Google to reach people with search intent rather than to find new audiences.
  • Target Google Ads at search queries with a high chance of leading to a sale rather than broad interest.
  • Expensive products should not be pushed to cold audiences on social media in the first phase.
  • Cold traffic from Google includes Google Shopping, Google display ads, Google search text ads and YouTube ads.
  • Google and Bing are the main channels for front-end demand on expensive products, since they reach people already searching.
  • Facebook and Instagram are better suited to remarketing.

  • This is because buyers of expensive items use search engines.
  • Social media is not a main channel in this model. It is used only for retargeting.
  • Google Shopping reaches people who are actively searching for a specific product.
  • Social media reaches people who are mostly not ready to buy.
  • For expensive products, this makes search-based traffic more likely to convert.

Ad Types and Placements

7 min

Overview of Types

  • Four main Google Ads types matter for a dropshipping store: Search Text Ads, Display Ads, YouTube Ads and Shopping Ads.
  • Other ad types exist but are rarely used in this approach.
  • Google Ads shows ads in several placements (places where ads appear).
  • These are search results (SERP, the page of results after a search), Shopping (including on mobile), the Display Network on individual websites, Gmail and YouTube.
  • For e-commerce, search and Shopping placements usually deserve the most attention first.
  • Google Ads and Bing Ads are paid search channels. They show ads to people actively searching for products.
  • Use them as main paid traffic sources next to Facebook Ads.

Search Text Ads

  • Search Text Ads appear at the top of Google search results for a keyword (the word or phrase you bid on), marked as paid.
  • They are won through a keyword auction where the highest bidder ranks first.
  • Bid amount alone does not decide position, because Quality Score also counts.
  • Google search text ads are the sponsored ads that appear above the organic results. Organic results are the free, unpaid listings.
  • Search text ads can outperform Shopping in some cases, especially outside the US.
  • So test both formats before deciding.
  • On a results page, Shopping ads come first.
  • Then comes the first block of search text ads, then organic results, then a second block of text ads at the bottom.
  • Shopping results are shown above all other ads when Google has relevant products.
  • When relevant Shopping ads exist for a search, they are the first results a shopper sees.
  • They appear above search text ads and organic results.
  • On desktop they sit at the top of the page, above the fold (the part visible without scrolling).
  • Google shows no further Shopping ads lower on the page, only search text ads.

Shopping Ads

  • Shopping Ads show a product image, product name, price, store URL and a short description for matching searches.

  • For high-ticket dropshipping, Google Shopping produces most of the sales that come from Google Ads traffic.
  • This holds for about 90% of businesses, usually at the best profitability.

Product Listing Ads (PLA)

  • Product listing ads (PLAs) are image-based product ads shown in Google search results.
  • They display a product image, title, price, store name and star ratings.

  • They are one of the main traffic sources in this model.

  • Facebook ads, by contrast, interrupt users who are browsing their feed without buying intent.

Display Network

YouTube

  • Viewers can skip after 5 seconds.
  • They need extra production work to set up.
  • They usually give a weaker return on ad spend than other Google ad types.
  • So they are not recommended for beginners.
  • YouTube works across the whole funnel, so it is useful at every stage when combined with other channels.

Performance Max and Other Types

  • Google Ads campaign types include Shopping Campaigns, Search Campaigns, Video Campaigns and Display Network campaigns.

How Google Shopping Works

1 min

Comparison Engine

1 min
  • Google Shopping works as a comparison shopping engine. It is a search site that lists the same product from many sellers so buyers can compare them.
  • Shoppers compare the same or similar products by price, title, shop name, reviews and delivery.
  • They do this without opening each site.
  • Your product page is judged directly against the competitors shown beside it.
  • Customers compare the same product across several retailers.
  • They look at price, brand, delivery, special offers, reviews and seller.
  • As a reseller (a shop that sells products it did not make), you are always compared directly with other sellers of the same product.
  • Google Shopping is a comparison engine by design.
  • You cannot avoid comparison with other sellers of the same product inside it.
  • If you cannot accept competing with identical listings, this channel is not a good fit.
  • Shoppers decide mainly on price, product title, retailer name and reviews.
  • When five or more shops sell the same product, the listing also shows delivery and special offers.
  • Shoppers decide on price, product title, retailer name and reviews.
  • They may also see delivery and special offers when several shops are listed.
  • Shopping ads place your product next to other sellers' near-identical listings.
  • This makes price competition a race to the bottom: prices keep falling until profit is gone.
  • Differentiate with better offers, better content and better images, not with price.
  • Do not compete against the AliExpress listing itself.
  • Google Shopping only shows "compare prices at X stores" when multiple sellers list the same product.

Shared Product Pages

1 min
  • A shared product page is one Google page where several sellers' offers for the same item are grouped.
  • When several retailers sell the same product, Google groups their listings together.
  • The price range can be wide.
  • A listing marked as refurbished (used or repaired, but sold as like-new) can show the lowest price.
  • Check the condition label when you benchmark competitors (compare your prices and offers with theirs).
  • Google groups listings with matching product data onto one shared product page.
  • Matching data includes the same GTIN (a product's barcode number) and brand.
  • Each seller shows its own price, delivery and offers on that page.
  • Resellers of the same item therefore compete on that page.
  • Price, delivery and offer details decide which seller gets the click.
  • A seller can list a refurbished unit under the same identifiers (product codes such as GTIN).
  • That seller can appear with the lowest price on the shared product page.
  • The condition is only visible in the details.
  • A retailer that sells only new products must expect such lower prices.
  • It must compete on other factors: delivery, offers and page clarity.

Query Matching and Auction Entry

2 min
  • A query is what a customer types into search.
  • For each search, Google checks whether the seller's product feed contains a matching product.

The Auction: Price and Position

1 min
  • Each time someone searches, Google runs an auction (a ranking of advertisers for that search).
  • Your bid is the highest price you will pay for one click.
  • Your cost per click (CPC: what you actually pay for one click) usually lands slightly below your own bid.
  • It usually lands above the bid of the advertiser directly below you.
  • Some advertisers in neighboring positions pay less than their own bid, so the result can vary.
  • Ads are shown in positions one to eight on the results page.
  • Ad Rank is the score that sets your position. In the example below, it is your bid times your Quality Score.
  • Bidders whose Ad Rank is too low to reach that range do not appear on the page at all.
  • Ads shown off the page receive no clicks.
  • So a low Ad Rank means lost traffic, even when the budget is available.

Worked auction example:

  1. Bidder A has a bid of $4.00 and Quality Score 8, giving Ad Rank 32.
  2. The bidder directly below has a bid of $3.45 and Quality Score 7, giving Ad Rank 24.15. This bidder sets the price.
  3. Divide 24.15 by 8 and add one cent: about $3.03 per click, close to the stated $3.02.

Low-bid example:

  • A bidder at $0.50 with a very high Quality Score, in a low slot, paid $0.19 per click.
  • The reason: the bidder just below was bidding $0.20.
  • A high Quality Score can keep costs low even at a small bid.

Quality Score and CTR

  • It only needs to be relevant enough to pass.

  • The ad must also match the other keywords in the same ad group (a set of keywords that share ads).
  • All keywords and the ad should relate closely to each other.
  • In an account, Quality Scores below 6 are a major problem that should be fixed first.
  • Such scores usually mean the ads pay penalties on every click.
  • Higher CTR lowers your cost per click, because Google treats more-clicked ads as more relevant.
  • That raises return on ad spend (ROAS: money earned for each dollar spent on ads).

  • When screening a PPC agency for a store, ask whether they have managed PLA campaigns specifically.
  • Do not accept general AdWords accounts (the old name for Google Ads) as proof.
  • Ad agencies that cold-email newly created Shopify stores (Shopify is an online store platform) usually sign up many clients without improving results.
  • Do not pay an outside agency until the owner has made ads profitable.
  • Set up Google Ads yourself first, and get them to at least profitable before considering an outside service.
  • Outsourcing (hiring outside help) comes after the owner knows what works.
  • A Google Ads ROAS of 3.26 was reported at one point.
  • The account was outsourced to an agency after the speaker had set it up.

Further Details

1 min
  • Product-focused Google ads that send buyers straight to product pages should be used first.
  • The reason: those people already want to buy.
  • Lead generation ads (ads that collect contact details from interested people) need more money, content and time to pay off.
  • So they are a later addition.
  1. Learn the theory of pay-per-click advertising and the buyer psychology (why buyers act) behind search campaigns.
  2. Set up and launch the Google Ads and Google Shopping campaigns so they start generating traffic.
  3. Move into a management phase, where ongoing monitoring and adjustment of campaigns is the main source of learning.
