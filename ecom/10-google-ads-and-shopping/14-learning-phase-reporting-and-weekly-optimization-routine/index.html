Playbook › 10. Google Ads and Shopping

Learning phase, reporting and weekly optimization routine

Section 10.14 · 14 of 23 in this chapter · 47 min read

In short

  • Google needs time to learn after you change something. Judge a campaign only after 2 weeks or more.
  • Every big change starts the learning again. Avoid many changes in a row.
  • Judge results by volume, not by a few days. Aim for about 100 clicks before big decisions.
  • Check which ad gets credit for a sale before you judge it.
  • Check Google Ads every two weeks once it is running. In a new store's first weeks, check weekly.
More key points
  • Judge a new Shopping campaign only after a few hundred clicks, not a few.
  • A click-through rate (CTR, the share of people who see your ad and click it) of 2% or lower on relevant keywords is a warning sign.
  • Add negative keywords (search words that block unwanted clicks) to stop wasting money.
  • Judge campaigns by profit, not by clicks alone.
  • Ads and bids that work in peak weeks can waste money in quiet weeks, so plan around the buying season.
  • A new Google campaign needs 2 to 4 weeks to learn. Judge it only after that.
  • Do not edit a campaign while it is still learning, unless it has no sales after 2 to 3 weeks.
  • Check new products and wasted search terms at least weekly.
  • Judge the holiday season only after about two weeks into January.

Time from Launch to First Data

1 min
  • New Google Ads campaigns can take up to one week to get approved.
  • So give the account a few days before expecting data.
  • After launch, ads may need up to five business days before they start getting impressions.
  • Clicks and sales come later.
  • Products approved in Google Merchant Center (the tool that sends your product list to Google) take 1 to 3 days to enter the auction (Google's instant contest for which ad shows) and start showing impressions. **
  • Do not adjust campaigns immediately. **
  • Give 1 to 3 days for data to appear. **
  • New Shopping campaigns usually need about 2 to 3 days after approval before they start getting impressions and clicks.
  • Once campaigns are approved, impressions usually start right away, and clicks follow. **
  • For a new store, expect roughly one to three days from approval for traffic to start showing. **
  • Once Google Shopping ads are approved, they show impressions and clicks within a few days. **
  • This data shows which product pages to optimise first. **
  • Use this data before optimising the whole catalogue (your full product list). **
  • Give a new Google Shopping campaign a few days to level out (settle) before changing bids or budgets. **
  • Alpha and beta campaigns (early test campaigns) that are structured correctly and have sizeable traffic should produce the first sales within about 2 to 5 days. **
  • Launch with alpha and beta campaigns.
  • Let them collect data.
  • Then adjust keywords, bids and product pages based on that data.
  • Do not judge profitability (whether you make money) from day one.

Conversion Timing

1 min
  • The conversion window (the time after a click in which a sale still counts) is typically 1 to 5 days.
  • Users see the original ads and remarketing ads (ads shown again to people who visited before) during this period before purchasing.
  • The typical buying window for Google Shopping traffic is 1 to 3 days after the click. **
  • So a campaign with about 100 targeted visitors a day should produce at least two sales a day. **
  • Once Shopping campaigns are optimised, conversions typically appear within about three days after the click. **
  • Conversions may take up to about 24 to 48 hours to appear in Google Ads.
  • Check the attribution model (the rule that decides which ad gets credit for a sale) before judging a campaign's conversions.

When Not to Change

1 min

Inside the Learning Window

2 min
  • During the learning phase, change negative keywords (search words that stop your ad from showing) rather than budgets or bids. **
  • This way, Google does not restart learning. **
  • Negative keywords can be added from day one. **
  • Do not start scaling Google Shopping ads (making them bigger) immediately after launching campaigns.
  • Allow 2 to 4 weeks for the learning phase and enough data collection before making major changes to bids or budgets.
  • Do not change the campaign structure before at least 2 weeks have passed since the ads started getting impressions and clicks.
  • Avoid drastic changes (very large ones) to bids, budgets or structure while a new Shopping campaign is still collecting data.
  • About 71 clicks over roughly two and a half weeks is too little to judge conversion.
  • New Shopping campaigns need time to learn.
  • Quality score (Google's rating of how useful your ad and page are) improves when visitors stay and browse several pages.
  • Avoid drastic bid or budget changes early on.
  • Raise CPCs only on the specific products that need more traffic.
  • Test Shopping campaign changes about once a week.
  • Give each change a full week of data.
  • Allow a two- to three-day learning curve after each change.
  • So a change made on Monday and changed again on Tuesday resets the learning.
  • Friday is an example change day for a store with low traffic.
  • For a brand-new account, do not make structural changes during the first 2 to 4 weeks of the learning period.
  • If the account spends money with no sales, lower the budget while you investigate.
  • Do not make major changes before the brand-new account's learning period of about 2 to 4 weeks has passed.
  • The first seven days of a new Google Ads account and store are not representative (they do not show the normal picture). **
  • Campaigns and product pages are not yet optimised at that point. **
  • Judge conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) and spend only after the store is optimised and enough visitors have arrived. **
  • Do not judge a new account over a small sample (a small set of data). **
  • Four weeks with 124 impressions and one click is too small to judge conversion. **
  • Judge over hundreds to thousands of visits per week. **
  • Do not draw conclusions from data right after the holiday shopping season. **
  • Buying slows in the weeks that follow. **
  • Wait about two weeks into January before making decisions. **

Sample Size and Decisions

1 min
  • Small click counts are not enough to judge a campaign. **
  • For example, 161 clicks or even 300 clicks is a small sample. **
  • The next batch can change results completely. **
  • So avoid drastic changes until more volume arrives. **
  • Do not make big decisions on a product until it has about 100 clicks.
  • Before 100 clicks, only make small adjustments such as CPC changes.
  • Wait until a campaign has at least a few hundred clicks or visitors, typically over about a week, before drawing conclusions. **
  • Judge a Smart Shopping change (Smart Shopping is a Google campaign type that sets bids automatically) by comparing a recent period with an earlier period of the same length.
  • Do not judge by individual days.
  • Daily results can swing widely while the overall trend still improves.
  • Changes take a day or two to show in the data. **
  • Check after changes every few days. **
  • Then check weekly or every other week once the campaign is stable. **
  • Google Ads data is only trustworthy after many more visits. **
  • With a small sample, wins or losses can be random. **
  • For a new store with few sales, some sales may come from random causes that do not repeat. **
  • Look for a repeating ad source or keyword across more data before drawing conclusions. **

Product Exclusion

1 min
  • Do not exclude a new Shopping product just because it has no sales after a couple of weeks.
  • Decide based on click volume (how many clicks it got).
  • About 100 clicks is the lower end.
  • 300 or more clicks gives a more reliable data basis.
  • Conversion rates can still level out between 200 and 300 clicks.
  • Conversion rates for a single product are highly variable in small samples.
  • Early zero-sale results are often randomness, not a broken product.
  • A store with about 60 active products that has run Google Ads for around a month usually has enough data for significant optimisations.
  • So waiting for more data is rarely necessary.
  • Start with the troubleshooting steps (steps to find the problem) for clicks without sales.

Budget, Bids and Live Changes

1 min
  • For high-ticket stores (stores selling expensive products), bids do not need daily changes. **
  • There is little benefit in daily changes. **
  • With steady performance, bids can stay unchanged for months. **
  • If a campaign spends an account's budget without sales, do not change bids on impulse. **
  • First confirm the campaign is set to spend the budget. **
  • A $20 daily budget that produces only one click in the first days is too little data to optimise from. **
  • Changes can be made directly in a live Google Ads campaign.
  • There is no need to pause the campaign first.
  • Pausing only makes sense if you intend to stop spending.
  • Changes should be made based on the data that exists, not on a fixed schedule. **
  • This may mean every 2 to 3 days or every two weeks. **
  • Changing dozens of campaigns from a daily export (a data file pulled each day) is far too extreme. **
  • Negative keywords are the exception. They can be added at any time. **
  • Google Ads optimisation score (a number Google shows for setup quality) is not a reliable decision tool. **
  • Stores with low scores can be very profitable. **
  • So do not change the campaign because of that number alone. **
  • Do not run new ad platforms during the low-competition window (a time when few other advertisers are bidding). **
  • Keep what works. **
  • Raise budgets there instead. **
  • Do not let a profitable campaign sit untouched, even if it already makes money.
  • A campaign that looks stable slowly loses performance over weeks and months.
  • It needs someone who keeps actively improving it.
  • Do not leave a Shopping campaign unchanged for weeks. **
  • Review the data early and optimise sooner rather than later. **

Columns, Dashboards and Reports

1 min

Columns are the data fields you see in a Google Ads table.

Core Metrics to Monitor

1 min
  • Monitor these key metrics to judge Google Ads performance.
  • Impressions: how many times your ad was shown.
  • Clicks: how many times people clicked your ad.
  • Cost: the money you spent.
  • Cost per all conversions: the money spent for each sale or other conversion.
  • Average CPC: the average price you paid per click.
  • Benchmark maximum CPC: the typical highest click price that other advertisers in your area pay.
  • Click share: the share of possible clicks you actually got.
  • Search impression share: the share of possible search ad impressions you got.
  • Search absolute top impression share: how often your ad shows at the very top of results.
  • Search lost impression share: the share of possible impressions you missed.
  • Benchmark CTR: the typical click-through rate (CTR, clicks divided by impressions) for your area.
  • Add these columns to Google Ads campaign reports: Cost, Conversion Value, Conversion Value ÷ Cost (ROAS), and Search Impression Share.
  • These metrics guide all bid and budget decisions.
  • Review Google Ads reports every week or every other week. **
  • Track whether impressions, clicks and conversions are trending up, down or flat (staying the same). **

Building the Custom Report

1 min
  • When building a custom Google Analytics report (Google Analytics is a free tool that tracks visitors and sales on your site; a custom report is one you design yourself) for Google Ads, use the metric "Transactions" instead of "Conversions". **
  • A metric is a number you measure, such as clicks or sales. **
  • Conversions may not report correctly in custom reports. **
  • The Google Analytics dimension (a category used to split the data) for the level below ad group is Keyword for search text campaigns. **
  • An ad group is a set of ads that share the same keywords. **
  • For Google Shopping campaigns, the equivalent is Product group. **
  • Shopping ads target products rather than keywords. **
  • The report template dates from when search text ads dominated. **

Procedure: create a custom Google Analytics report to track Google Ads performance at the search-term level. **

  1. Go to Customisation > Custom Reports and click Create Custom Report.
  2. Enter Title: Google Ads Performance Report.
  3. Set Report Type to Explorer (a flexible report type).
  4. Add Metrics by searching for and selecting each: Advertising Clicks, Advertising Impressions, Advertising CTR, Advertising CPC, Advertising Cost, Transactions, Advertising Cost per Transaction, E-commerce Conversion Rate, E-commerce Revenue, Advertising ROAS.
  5. Add Dimension Drilldowns (levels you dig down through) in order: Campaign, Google Ads Ad Group, Keyword, Search Query.
  6. Add Filters: (Traffic Type = paid, meaning paid visitors only) AND (Source/Medium = google / cpc, meaning visitors from Google ads that were clicked).
  7. Click Save to populate the report.
  8. Drill down from campaign through ad group and keyword to individual search terms to compare clicks, cost, transactions, conversion rate, revenue and ROAS.
  • The custom Google Analytics report for Google Ads shows revenue and ROAS credited to individual campaigns, ad groups, keywords and search terms. **
  • This is visibility that the Google Ads interface itself does not provide. **
  • It enables detailed analysis and optimisation at the search-term level. **

Attribution Models

2 min
  • Last-click attribution is the standard tracking model.
  • The most recent traffic source click within the same session gets 100 percent of the conversion credit.
  • A session is one visit to the site (approximately five minutes of on-site activity).
  • Example: if a customer clicks an ad on Monday, watches YouTube on Monday, but converts via email on Wednesday, email receives full credit.
  • When 100 percent of traffic comes from AdWords (the old name for Google Ads), the tracking system shows only about 60 percent of conversions as AdWords.
  • The other 40 percent shows as Direct/Shopify (visits with no known source).
  • Attribution becomes increasingly fragmented (split into pieces) as you diversify traffic sources (use more kinds of visitor sources).
  • Assisted conversions (sales where a click helped earlier, but was not the last click) show how many multi-touch conversions a campaign influenced.
  • Multi-touch means several touches (contacts) happen before a sale.
  • This gives a more accurate view than last-click attribution alone.
  • It shows a campaign's true contribution in multi-click customer journeys, not just the final click.
  • Use Assisted Revenue in Google Ads to see the full attribution picture beyond last-click conversions.
  • It reveals how multiple touchpoints (each place a customer meets your brand) across sessions contribute to each sale.
  • Google Ads and Google Analytics attribution models: First click gives the conversion to the first interaction (a click or visit).
  • Last click gives it to the last interaction.
  • Linear splits one conversion across all clicks.
  • This produces fractional conversions, such as 0.5 for a Shopping click and 0.5 for a text ad click.
  • Check the attribution model before judging a campaign's conversions.
  • Different models assign the same sale to different ads.
  • Keyword attribution: to see which keyword produced a sale in Google Ads, open the keyword breakdown inside the campaign that brought the sale. **
  • In Google Analytics, conversion tracking shows which campaign sold the product. **
  • Organic sales (sales from free search results, not ads) often appear as non-set or not-set keywords. **

Tracking Discrepancies

1 min
  • Shopping ads and Facebook ads are not the only traffic sources to track. **
  • Google Ads, Shopping and Facebook all over-claim conversions (take credit for more sales than they really made). **
  • So compare them against store analytics. **
  • Compare Google Ads conversions with real orders.
  • Sales can be attributed elsewhere if customers visit from an ad, bookmark the page and buy later.
  • Sales can also come from an organic search or email link.
  • The search term report in Google Ads is not updated in real time. **
  • Its dates use the account's time zone (GMT-05:00, Eastern Time). **
  • Compare date ranges with that in mind. **
  • Do not judge a search term from the last day or two of data. **
  • Google Shopping does not show every search term that triggers your ads. **
  • A campaign with 1,000 impressions may list only a few search terms. **
  • So the search term data is incomplete. **
  • It cannot fully explain performance. **
  • When checking whether an excluded Shopping product group still gets clicks, use a date range that starts after the exclusion.
  • A longer range such as 30 days still shows older data.
  • Check whether a cost-per-click increase has been applied to all product groups. **
  • Some products can be excluded from bid changes and stop getting traffic. **
  • Audit the account regularly, since errors can appear without warning. **

Weekly Routine for a New Store

1 min

Procedure: weekly Google Ads optimisation routine for a new store.

  1. Set a fixed weekday and time for the account review, for example with a recurring alarm.
  2. Audit both the alpha and beta campaigns (the two Shopping campaigns used in this routine) and note what is working and what is not.
  3. Add new negative keywords to both alpha and beta.
  4. Adjust bids for products that get too few impressions or clicks.
  5. Once the account has 50 or more sales, switch the campaigns to Enhanced CPC (a setting where Google adjusts your bids on its own).
  6. When one brand clearly wins, move it into its own alpha campaign.
  7. Exclude products or brands that lose money or disappoint customers from all campaigns.
  8. Products that get clicks but no sales go to conversion optimisation of the store (improving the store so more visitors buy).

Daily Audit in the First Week

1 min

Procedure: daily Google Ads audit in the first week. **

  1. Day one: check where your ads appear and which search terms (the exact words a shopper typed) triggered them.
  2. Then add irrelevant terms as negative keywords.
  3. From day two: check click-through rate by comparing clicks with impressions.
  4. An impression is one view of your ad.
  5. If click-through rate is low, compare your prices with competitors' Google Shopping listings (product ads on Google).
  6. Also check whether they honour MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price a brand allows sellers to advertise).
  7. Compare your product images with competitors' images and improve yours.
  8. Repeat the audit every day, aiming to have the main optimisations done by about day four or five.
  9. Around halfway, expect visitors to add items to the cart and to use live chat or send inquiries.
  10. By the end, aim for the first sale.
  • Do not wait for perfect data before optimising (improving), especially if the store must be profitable soon. **
  • Even a small budget like $20 per day gives enough information to start.

Early Shopping Campaign Optimisation

1 min

Procedure: early Google Shopping campaign optimisation in the first days and week. **

  1. Check which keywords produce impressions and which produce clicks in the Google Ads account.
  2. Identify search terms and products you do not want to appear for.
  3. Add negative keywords for unwanted searches in the Shopping campaigns.
  4. Adjust cost per click (CPC, the price you pay each time someone clicks) by product.
  5. Lower it for products that get impressions but that you do not need to sell.
  6. Let the data build up before making more changes.

  • Once the account is running, a new Shopping campaign should be judged with a few hundred clicks, not a few.

  • The early phase is about launching, collecting data and optimising from it, not about judging profitability.

  • Shopping campaigns should be checked for impressions and clicks early.

  • This way, drastic changes are avoided and small adjustments can be made.

Spend Audit

1 min

Procedure: to audit ad spend. **

  1. Look at where money goes and identify spend that is not producing sales.
  2. For products or keywords that get consistent spend, first optimise the product pages so more visitors buy.
  3. If the pages are already optimised and visitors still do not buy, cut the ad spend sooner rather than later.
  4. Move the freed budget into the products that sell.
  • Regularly review search terms, product-level performance (how each single product does) and bids. **
  • Pause or reduce spend on products and queries (search requests) that do not produce profitable sales.

Keyword Review in Alpha and Beta Campaigns

1 min

Procedure: to analyse Google Ads keyword data in alpha and beta campaigns. **

Ongoing Optimisation Routine

1 min

Procedure: optimisation routine for Google Ads data.

  1. Review the performance data.
  2. Ask how deeply it can be broken down. Segments (groups you split data into) can be keyword, placement (where the ad shows), audience, time, device or others.
  3. Stop or cut what is not working, such as keywords, placements or ads that spend without producing results.
  4. Improve and expand what is working, such as scaling budgets or adding similar keywords and audiences.
  5. Repeat this review continuously.

High-Ticket Management

1 min

High-ticket means expensive products. Dropshipping means a supplier ships the product to the buyer for you.

  • For high-ticket dropshipping Shopping campaigns, only two recurring management tasks are needed. **

  • Task one: search term control through negative keywords.

  • Task two: bid and budget adjustment.

  • Shopping campaigns start producing sales once switched on.

  • Ongoing management is where profitability (how much profit you make) improves. **

  • Each optimisation round tends to lower ad spend per sale and raise profit.

  • Optimising a store with about 60 active products after a month is a sound basis for making decisions.

  • Shopping campaign benchmark (a reference number to compare against) for performance checks: the advertiser's threshold for investigating a problem is a return on ad spend below 10x. **

  • Check click-through rate, cost per click, clicks and conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) in Google Ads first.

  • Only then look at Google Shopping for MAP violations (sellers pricing below the minimum advertised price).

  • Do this roughly every two weeks with smaller budgets, or several times a week with larger budgets.

Benchmark Principles

1 min

  • There is no reliable average click-through rate or click share (your part of all possible clicks) benchmark for Google Ads. **

  • Results depend on the spend and on how the campaigns are segmented (split into groups).

  • Judge performance by profit per campaign.

  • No universal impression or click benchmark exists.

  • Results depend on the product, the brand and the niche (the small market you sell into). **

  • Judge performance against your own products and goals.

  • Judge a new Google Ads account over a sample (a group big enough to read) of hundreds to thousands of visits per week.

  • Do not judge it over a few clicks.

Conversion Rate

1 min

  • Google Shopping and Product Listing Ads had an average conversion rate of about 2.3% (the share of visitors who buy).

  • About 97.7% of visitors did not buy on their first visit.

  • These figures come from the source and should be checked against your own account data.

  • Use Google Shopping and Remarketing (showing ads again to people who already visited your store) as the primary qualified traffic sources (visitors likely to buy) for conversion benchmarks. **

  • Treat their conversion rate target of about 2.5% as the reference.

  • Google campaigns should aim for an add-to-cart ratio around 7% (the share of visitors who add an item to the cart).

  • This is higher than Meta or TikTok, because search is intent-based (people search when they want to buy).

  • The landing page (the page a click opens) should match the keywords the buyer searched.

Impression Share

1 min

  • Use the search impression share metric (the share of possible views your ads got) as a baseline. **

  • Compare what the budget captures with the full market share needed to be seen by most eligible shoppers (shoppers who could see your ad).

  • Impression share shows what portion of possible impressions your ads actually received. **

  • A 30 percent share means 70 percent of potential impressions are missed.

  • A share of 2% means 98% of eligible searches are missed. **

  • Example: in a campaign generating about $69,000 monthly revenue (money from sales each month), Impression Share sat at 32% instead of 66%.

  • The open question was whether better campaign work alone could double revenue.

  • A Search Impression Share near 100% means an ad group (a set of ads that share the same search words) already shows for nearly every relevant query its negative keyword list allows.

  • So that targeting has no room to grow.

  • More volume would then require relaxing the negative keyword list, which usually brings back irrelevant searches. **

  • A reasonable early sample for Google Shopping is around 200 impressions. **

  • If impressions remain far below this with a normal bid and budget, check the campaign setup.

  • Impression share in Google Shopping depends on product quality and on which dashboard view (report screen) is checked.

  • Adding more products does not automatically increase impression share.

  • Better-performing products improve it.

  • To increase a low search impression share, add the keywords your products already show for.

  • Keep adding negative keywords.

  • Better click-through rate then raises Quality Score, which brings more impressions at a lower cost.

  • Raising the bid alone may not get more impressions. **

  • Check the search impression share column once the account is set up. **

  • A share of 2% means 98% of eligible searches are missed.

  • If impression share is very low, see the inch-up method below.

Diagnosing High Impressions and Low Clicks

3 min

  • When a Google Ads campaign shows high impressions but very few clicks, the most likely cause is that it appears for irrelevant or overly broad search terms.

  • Review the search terms report (the list of words shoppers typed) and add the off-target terms as negative keywords.

  • Shoppers who see the ad as less relevant than competing ads, or a search term that is not relevant, will not click. **

  • Protecting click-through rate protects Quality Score and lowers cost per click.

Procedure: handling high impressions but low clicks. **

  1. Check the search terms triggering the product ad.
  2. Add a negative keyword for every search term that is unrelated to the product.
  3. If the keywords are relevant but clicks are still low, search the product on Google Shopping.
  4. Compare the competing listings and identify what they do differently that makes shoppers click.

Procedure: diagnosing high impressions with uneven distribution (impressions bunched in one place). **

  1. In your Google Ads account, look at which products and search terms generate impressions.
  2. Look for uneven distribution, for example 600 of 1,000 daily impressions coming from one keyword or product.
  3. Compare the click-through rate of the concentrated impressions with the rest.
  4. If the concentrated part is irrelevant and has a low CTR, that is the problem.
  5. Add the irrelevant search terms as negative keywords.
  6. CTR rises because Google then shows the product only for better matches.
  7. Search for your own products in an incognito Chrome window (a private browser window that does not save your history) in Google Shopping.
  8. This shows your position (where your ad ranks in results) against competitors.
  9. If competitors above you have the same price, images and shipping, raise your bids and make your offer different from theirs to win higher positions.
  10. Check the search impression share column. A share of 2% means 98% of eligible searches are missed.
  11. If impression share is very low, raise bids across the board using the inch-up method (raising bids in small steps).
  12. Increase bids gradually, not budget, until daily spend reaches the ideal level, then keep optimising.
  • Google Shopping does not show every search term that triggers your ads.
  • So search term data cannot fully explain performance.
  • Use it to find mismatches (wrong matches), not as a complete picture. **

Case: established high-ticket Shopping funnel (the path a shopper takes from ad to sale). **

  • Starting point: a shared budget (one daily budget used by several campaigns) of $300 per day across three campaigns.

  • High priority at a $0.30 bid and an average CPC of $0.26.

  • Over 30 days the search terms report listed 6,971 terms, many of them generic and low-value.

  • One campaign showed the Limited by budget status (the ads stopped early because the budget ran out).

  • Google's budget explorer recommended $370, a rise of about $70 that was judged acceptable for a profitable account.

  • The alternatives were lowering the high priority bid from $0.30 to $0.20 to bring CPC to $0.20 or below, or adding negatives.

  • Each review then covered only the date range since the previous check.

  • Negatives were added to the master list (one shared list used by all campaigns), so all three campaigns received them.

  • Daily check for high impressions and clicks: look at the search terms.

  • Then add negatives for irrelevant ones, and check competitor listings. **

  • Break down the Google Shopping campaign daily by keywords with impressions and keywords that spent money.

  • Add irrelevant keywords to the negative list every day.

  • Raise the budget only once the account is profitable. **

Procedure: daily improvement of the Google Shopping campaign. **

  1. Open the keywords inside the Shopping campaign.
  2. Review which keywords received impressions.
  3. Review which keywords spent money.
  4. Add irrelevant keywords to the negative keyword list every day.
  5. Raise the budget only once the account is profitable.

Diagnosing Low CTR

2 min

  • A low CTR affects the performance of the whole campaign, not the whole account. **

  • A few low-CTR products among many have little effect.

  • Low CTR across most products in a campaign has a significant effect, though the exact size is unknown.

  • A Google Shopping click-through rate in the 0.3 to 2.7 percent range usually has three causes. **

    • Very broad keywords that attract non-buyers.
    • Competitors or suppliers pricing below your MAP.
    • A bid so low that your listing sits at the bottom of results.

  • Remove non-buying keywords and check pricing before raising bids.

  • A rate of 0.32 to 2.71 percent indicates a problem with keywords, pricing or bids. **

  • Aim for at least 3 percent.

Procedure: troubleshooting a low Google Shopping click-through rate. **

  1. In Google Ads, open the keyword view of the campaign.
  2. Find keywords with many impressions but few or no clicks.
  3. Add non-buyer, broad keywords as negative keywords.
  4. Search those keywords on Google yourself and check the shopping results.
  5. If competitors sell the same product for less, check for pricing discrepancies (price differences) or MAP violations.
  6. If you rank near the bottom, raise your bid by about 20 percent.
  7. Wait a few days, then check your position and click-through rate.
  8. If the position is still low, raise the bid again.

Procedure: diagnosing a low CTR on Shopping ads. **

  1. Compare your ad with competitors' ads for the same product, looking at image, title, price, review stars and any promotion.
  2. Check whether competitors are selling at a lower price across the board, which can explain why people click on them instead.
  3. Check your position in the auction (the bidding contest for ad spots).
  4. With 10 or more advertisers, ads at the bottom get fewer clicks.
  5. A small bid increase may move the ad into the middle of the pack and raise CTR.
  6. Check the search terms your ad appears for to find mismatches.

Procedure: diagnosing low click-through rate in a Shopping campaign. **

  1. Check whether prices are competitive.
  2. Check whether the campaign shows for irrelevant keywords.
  3. If it does, add negative keywords after doing negative keyword research (finding search words to block).
  4. Run the target searches yourself and judge whether your product images are engaging enough.

Procedure: diagnosing low CTR on relevant keywords. **

  1. Log into your Google Ads account.
  2. Navigate to the keywords section.
  3. For keywords showing many impressions but few clicks (low CTR on relevant terms), check three things.
    • Whether competitors are violating MAP pricing.
    • Whether your bid is too low.
    • Whether your product image is inferior to competitors' images.
  4. Make targeted improvements to high-quality keywords before optimising or removing weak keywords.
  • CTR of 2% or lower on relevant keywords signals one or more problems. **
  • Possible problems:
    • Keywords may match irrelevant search queries.
    • Your product price may be higher than competitors' (violating MAP pricing).
    • Your bid may be too low to place you high in search results.
    • Your product image and description may be weaker than competitors'.

Seasonality (Sales That Rise and Fall With the Year) and Q4 (October to December) and Black Friday Patterns

3 min

Planning Around the Buying Window

  • Plan Google Ads (Google's paid ad system) for seasonal niches around the buying window, the weeks when shoppers buy. **
  • Spend and bids (the price you offer for each ad click) that work in peak weeks can waste budget in off-season weeks.
  • A seasonality adjustment is a setting for a planned peak, such as Black Friday or Cyber Monday. Black Friday and Cyber Monday are the big US shopping days after Thanksgiving.
  • Set an expected conversion rate increase (the share of visitors who buy), for example 25% higher, for that period.
  • The strategy then bids as if the conversion rate will rise during that time.
  • Use it only for promotions with a defined start and end.

Q4 Pattern

  • A sharp drop in click-through rate (CTR, the share of people who see an ad and click it) in Q4 is often caused by higher ad prices.
  • Big advertisers spend their budgets in Q4, which pushes your ad position (where your ad shows on the page) down.
  • Check first which products and keywords (the words people type to search) lost clicks. **
  • Then compare competitors' (rival stores') prices, main images and shipping badges (small labels in ads, such as "free shipping").
  • Ad costs typically rise during Black Friday and Cyber Monday.
  • From mid-December to the end of December, ad costs often drop a lot, as many advertisers pause. This quiet window is called Q5.
  • Stores launched by Black Friday can use this period to buy traffic (visitors) more cheaply. **
  • For non-seasonal products (items that sell all year, not just at peaks), expect a sales spike around Black Friday and Cyber Monday.
  • Expect a weaker stretch in December.
  • Expect a recovery in early January.
  • Ad costs have historically dropped in that period, which makes it a good time to win extra sales. **
  • Ad costs often drop in the last two weeks of December and through January.
  • Large advertisers cut their budgets after Cyber Monday.
  • This can be a cheaper window to reach buyers. **

Year-on-Year Comparison

  • In Google Ads, use the date range selector (the calendar tool for picking dates) to compare the same week across two years.
  • That week runs from Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday.
  • Check clicks, CPC (cost per click, what you pay for each click) and which products sold in each year. **
  • Google can shift budget to different products over time.
  • A year-on-year review (this year against the same period last year) should check whether last year's best sellers still get clicks and page visits. **
  • Refine bids and CPC to send budget to products that convert (turn visitors into buyers).

Competitor Supplementary Campaigns

  • Holiday sales drops are often caused by competitors running extra short campaigns (groups of ads run together under one budget).
  • These extra campaigns are added on top of their main campaigns.
  • These extra campaigns raise auction prices (the price per click, set by bidding) for everyone.
  • So the fix is not to change main bids alone.
  • Plan promotional campaigns of your own, such as email to abandoned checkouts (emails to shoppers who left before paying). **

Holiday Timing

  • Do not draw conclusions from the holiday shopping season until about two weeks into January. **
  • Schedule holiday campaigns weeks in advance.
  • Leave a buffer (spare time) of at least a couple of days for edits. **

  • The learning phase averages 2 to 4 weeks.
  • During this time, Google optimizes (improves) the campaign.
  • Results from this period are not representative of final performance.

Checking New Products

  • After uploading new products, Google Ads goes through a learning phase. **
  • Check new product performance at least weekly, sometimes every few days.
  • Adjust budgets and negative keywords (words that stop your ad from showing for them) accordingly.

When Not to Change

  • Do not edit campaigns or account settings during the Google learning phase.
  • Each change restarts or disrupts optimization.
  • Let the campaigns run as configured until the learning phase is over.
  • The exception is a campaign with no sales and no checkouts after two to three weeks. That calls for a setup change.

Restarting After a Pause

  • Ads that were turned off and turned back on enter the learning phase again.
  • Results can drop only if the campaigns had very strong historical performance before being paused.

Reporting: Columns, Dimensions and Metrics

1 min

Columns are the fields in your spreadsheet. Dimensions are ways to split your data, such as by product. Metrics are the numbers you measure.

Columns to Add for Google

  • The same funnel logic (the step-by-step path from ad to sale) can be applied to Google Ads.
  • Add these checks: unit economics (profit on each sale), cost per click, cost per purchase against target, and ROAS (return on ad spend, money made per dollar spent) against break-even (the point where you make neither profit nor loss).
  • The tracker (your results spreadsheet) is built around Meta metrics (numbers from Meta's Facebook and Instagram ads).
  • So Google metrics need their own columns.

Ad-Level Check

  • At ad level (each single ad), check spend, rank, clicks, click-through rate and quality score (Google's rating of how well an ad matches its searches) together.
  • A weak ad can be spotted before it drains budget.
  • In the example, a quality score of 4 against a target of 7 was a reason to pause that ad and introduce a new one.

Dimensions to Split In a Dashboard

  • A dashboard is a screen that shows your numbers at a glance.
  • Additional dimensions worth splitting in a dashboard include desktop bid modifiers (extra bid changes for desktop computers), gender, product IDs, search terms (to find converting queries, meaning searches that lead to sales) and headlines.
  • Comparing headlines by click-through rate reveals which messages attract clicks.

Combined Sample Size

  • When judging a campaign, add the clicks from all ad platforms you run.
  • Examples are Google Ads and Microsoft Ads (Microsoft's ad system, also used on Bing).
  • Add them to reach the total sample size (the total number of clicks you judge from).
  • Optimize based on that combined sample, not on each small platform total alone.

Analytics Custom Reports and Attribution

1 min

Analytics means Google Analytics, a free tool that tracks visitors on your site. Attribution means which ad gets credit for a sale.

Search Terms in Two Places

  • Review paid traffic (visitors who came from ads) for a product by checking search terms (the exact words people typed).
  • Check them in the search term report of each campaign and ad group (a set of ads inside a campaign) in Google Ads.
  • That report shows terms that produced impressions and clicks.
  • Also check a custom Google Analytics report (one you build yourself).
  • This custom report breaks search terms down to individual product level.
  • Use both where possible, or at least one. **

Decision Data for ROAS

  • Decide which products, ads or campaigns to pause, scale (spend more on) or highlight.
  • Base that decision on Google Ads data, not on Analytics or accounting totals.
  • Use Google Ads metrics when the goal is to improve ROAS.

Audit Routines and Cadence

2 min

An audit is a check-up. Cadence means how often you repeat a routine.

Spend and Search-Term Audit

  1. Look at how much ad spend goes to each product.
  2. Break spend down by dimensions such as search terms, age ranges, devices and products.
  3. Identify search terms that produced zero revenue.
  4. Remove wasted areas: age ranges that do not fit the target customer, devices that do not convert, and products that should not appear for a given search.
  5. Block the wasteful search terms as negative keywords, in advance.

Campaign Hierarchy Audit

  • Review the campaign hierarchy (how campaigns are organized, from top to bottom).
  • Confirm all relevant targeting types (who your ads aim at) are active.
  • These are audiences, YouTube ads, display ads (image ads on websites), Bing ads, Facebook ads and Gmail ads.
  • Missing targeting types are unused reach (people you could reach but do not).
  • Checking them is part of a campaign audit.

Impression Share Check

  • Check impression share (the part of possible views your ads actually get).
  • If it is low, the account is missing potential impressions.
  • Frequent ad testing can raise click-through rate.
  • Higher click-through rate improves quality score and lowers cost per click.
  • The same budget then reaches more buyers.

Ongoing Optimization

  • Ongoing Google Ads optimization (continuous improvement) includes these tasks:
    • Keyword expansion (adding more relevant search words).
    • Bid monitoring (watching your bid prices).
    • Bid strategy adjustment with a ROAS target (changing how bids are set, aiming for a target return).
    • Adding negative keywords to block unprofitable spend (money that makes no profit).
    • Improving CTR.
    • Lowering cost per click through quality score.
    • Reviewing costly keywords.
  • Cutting non-productive spend is part of the same routine.

Weekly Product Audit

  1. Look at cost trends per product: note which costs are rising or falling.
  2. Look at conversions per product (sales or other goals reached): note which are rising or falling.
  3. Work out what caused the change, whether good or bad.
  4. Adjust bids, budgets or product groups accordingly.
  5. Cut spend on products that have spend but no sales.
  6. Add negative keywords for terms that trigger ads without sales.
  7. Repeat weekly, or at least every two weeks for small budgets. **

Diagnosing Impressions Without Clicks and Low CTR

1 min

Starting Point

  • A sub-1% CTR can be normal for a broad campaign.
  • Judge CTR against how targeted the campaign is before acting. **

Five-Step Procedure to Raise CTR

**

  1. Segment your campaigns into alpha and beta (two campaign types named in the source), then add brand campaigns (ads that use your store name) and best-seller campaigns (ads for your top-selling products).
  2. Add negative keywords at campaign level (blocking words for a whole campaign). Base them on search terms that show your ads but do not match your ideal buyer, such as free, guide or in-store purchases. The data is in your Google Ads account.
  3. Check your bid: search your product in an incognito Google window (a private browser window with no saved history) and see where your listing appears. If you sit at a low position, such as 12th, raise your bid.
  4. Check the keywords that give you impressions without sales. Then search them in an incognito window to see whether competitors are breaking MAP (minimum advertised price, the lowest price a seller may show in ads).
  5. Compare competitor ads for promo text such as free shipping, and for product images that look better than yours. Then improve yours to match or beat them.

Ad-Level and Headline Tests

  • At ad level, check spend, rank, clicks, click-through rate and quality score together.
  • A weak ad can be spotted before it drains budget.
  • A quality score of 4 against a target of 7 was a reason to pause that ad and introduce a new one.
  • Comparing headlines by click-through rate reveals which messages attract clicks.
  • Frequent ad testing can raise click-through rate.
  • Higher click-through rate improves quality score and lowers cost per click.

Low Impression Volume

  • For high-ticket products (expensive items) with no impressions, raise the bid in their ad group.
  • For example, raise it from a default CPC of $0.40 to $0.45. (Older advice, 2025 source; new-store context.)

More rules worth knowing

1 min
  • Analyzing inconsistent monthly Google Ads spend (same budget, different sales):
    1. Review data from the best-performing month.
    2. Check which products received impressions and clicks in that month.
    3. Identify which products have higher conversion rates.
    4. Adjust bids to direct budget to those high-performing products.
    5. Remove unrelated keywords that attracted clicks with no conversions.
  • With 270 products and a $50 daily budget, expect 6 to 12 months for full campaign optimization.
  • Google's 2-week learning phase is just the beginning.
  • Ongoing optimization through bid adjustment, negative keywords, and product exclusion (hiding products from ads) takes months.
  • After a Google Ads account has been paused for a long period, such as 18 months, create new campaigns instead of reactivating old ones.
  • The old ones would re-enter the learning phase.
