Bidding, budgets and bid optimization

Section 10.12 · 12 of 23 in this chapter · 69 min read

In short

  • Start every new campaign on Manual CPC, where you set the price you pay per click yourself.
  • Only let Google set bids automatically after about 30 sales per month.
  • Set the starting bid low, then raise it in small steps, waiting days or weeks between changes.
  • Set each product's bid on its own, using: revenue per click ÷ your goal return per dollar.
  • Do not make big bid or pause decisions until a product has at least 100 clicks.
More key points
  • Check your profit before you raise any bid.
  • Raise budgets slowly: wait 1 to 2 weeks after each step.
  • Use Target ROAS only after you have 30 or more sales per month.
  • Use one shared budget for two campaigns that work as a pair, so the money is not wasted.
  • Start with a small bid, such as $0.20, and raise it slowly. Raise it by about 20 percent at a time, never doubled.
  • Judge each product by ROAS (sales money earned for each dollar of ad cost), not by how many sales it has yet.
  • Work out a target cost per sale (CPA) for each product. Change bids in small steps, and never by more than 50% at once.
  • A product with no sales gets more time first, then a lower bid or a pause. A product with no clicks gets a small bid raise.
  • Google charges you each time someone clicks your ad. If a product gets clicks but no sales, lower the price you pay per click for that product only.
  • Raise the budget only when the campaign uses its full daily spending limit every day and the visitors are still good buyers.
  • Start with manual bidding, where you set each price yourself. Let Google automate bids only after your sales numbers are steady.
  • Grow slowly. Make small changes, then wait one to two weeks before you judge them.

Choosing the Bid Strategy

1 min
  • Campaigns with fewer than 30 conversions per month (a conversion is one tracked sale) need manual bid adjustments, based on how each product performs. Campaigns with 30 or more conversions can use automated Google Ads bid strategies, where Google sets the bids for you.
  • Start every new campaign on Manual CPC, where you set the price you pay for each click yourself (CPC means cost per click). This keeps full control over spending and buying customers.
  • In a new account, uncheck the Enhanced CPC checkbox, which is checked by default. Enhanced CPC lets Google raise your bids on products it has not yet proven sell.
  • Use Manual CPC instead of Google's automated conversion-focused bidding, unless the account already tracks many conversions, with several per day.
  • Do not use Maximize Clicks for campaigns that must make a profit. Use it only if the goal is more visitors rather than profit.
  • Target ROAS (Google aims for a set return for each dollar spent) and Maximize Clicks do not work for the Source B shopping funnel (a funnel is the set of steps a customer takes, from ad to sale). **

Core Bid Formulas

1 min
  • Target ROAS = total conversion value (revenue) ÷ total cost spent on the campaign. ROAS means return on ad spend: how many dollars come back for each dollar spent.
  • Target CPC (maximum bid, the most you pay per click) = revenue per click (RPC, the average money one click brings in) ÷ target ROAS.
  • Worked example: 100 clicks generating $1,000 gives an RPC of $10. For a 5× ROAS (5× means five times the money back), $10 ÷ 5 = $2 maximum CPC. At $2 per click with $10 expected revenue, you will consistently hit 5×.
  • Verification: multiply the target CPC by the target ROAS. The result should match the RPC, allowing minor rounding. Example: $1.96 × 6 = $11.76, matching an RPC of $11.78. If it does not match, recalculate RPC ÷ target ROAS.
  • Choosing targets: the alpha campaign example uses a target ROAS of 3.3×, calculated as revenue ÷ desired ad spend. Adjust it to your actual margins (the share of each sale you keep after costs) and your profit needs.
  • The beta campaign uses a lower target, such as 2.5×, because beta captures less qualified research and comparison searches, meaning shoppers who are still comparing and less ready to buy.
  • Keywords (search words that trigger your ad) use the same formula: keyword RPC ÷ target ROAS.

Daily Budget and CPC Limit

1 min
  • Start with the same daily budget in alpha and beta. The course team launches new campaigns at about $20 per day each. A 27-product merchandise store (a store selling 27 items) used $15 per day each. A lower budget means slower data collection.
  • A $25 daily budget is an example starting point for testing. Budget is independent of the alpha and beta split. Each campaign can share a budget or have its own.
  • Start Google Shopping with a daily budget between $15 and $35.
  • A common starting setup is a daily budget of about $20 with a cost-per-click limit (CPC limit, the most Google may charge per click) of $1. The maximum daily budget to start with is about $33.
  • Set both a daily budget and a CPC limit. Spend stops when the daily budget is reached. Ads usually begin running 2 to 3 days after setup.
  • Do not set the CPC limit too low at launch. A very low cap restricts impressions (how often your ad is shown) and slows data collection. A limit near $1 is a common starting point. Adjust it once the data shows which products are overbid or underbid, meaning paying too much or too little.
  • Use "standard" delivery, not "accelerated." Accelerated delivery spends the budget as fast as possible and may use it all up before the day ends.
  • Test with a small budget first. Increase spending only after the tests show results.
  • To control spend without losing campaign history, lower the daily budget instead of pausing, for example from $50 to $10 per day. Pausing causes a messy restart.

New Campaign Launch Procedure **

1 min
  1. Upload all available products.
  2. Confirm every product is approved and receiving impressions.
  3. Set a very small default cost per click.
  4. Wait a few days for impressions and clicks.
  5. Once clicks arrive, raise the cost per click by about 0.05 at product level (for each product) every couple of days.
  • Raising budgets and bids right after launch is a common error. Products that are not yet approved do not enter the auction (the instant contest where Google picks which ads show), so the increase has no effect.

When Data Is Enough to Change a Bid

1 min
  • Wait for at least 100 impressions before changing a product's CPC. Use 200 impressions to be safer. Then raise or lower the CPC, depending on whether clicks cost too much or too little.
  • Start with low bids. If ads are approved and get some impressions but no clicks, or too few impressions in total, raise bids gradually.
  • About 100 impressions per week is far too few for a product. Raise the bid in small steps, for example from 25 cents to 30 or 35 cents. Never jump to one dollar.
  • Each optimisation session takes about 15 minutes once you are familiar with it. Adjust cost-per-click bids every 1 to 2 weeks.

Step Patterns

1 min

  • Zero-impression ladder (a set of price steps for products that get no ad views) **: default 50 cents. Products with no impressions go to 75 cents. If the budget still is not fully spent after a week, raise to $1.00. Continue step by step until the daily budget runs out.

  • Zero-impression ladder (2023): start at $0.50. If you see no impressions, raise to $0.60, wait a few days, then $0.70, $0.80, and $1.00. This method reveals the competitive bid landscape (what other advertisers bid) without needing Google's benchmark data (Google's typical price figures).

  • Underspent campaign (the budget is not fully used): raise CPC gradually, for example from $0.35 to $0.40, rather than making large jumps such as $0.50. Small steps show whether more traffic is available at the current budget.

  • Formula for underspent products: new CPC = current CPC + $0.05 (for example, $0.35 becomes $0.40). Apply this only to underspent products. Do not raise every CPC at once.

  • Beta ladder: start Beta at $0.20. After one week with no traffic, raise to $0.25. Wait another week. If traffic is still very low, raise to $0.30 and continue the same way. If traffic is good, leave the bid unchanged.

  • Inch-up method **:

    1. Start from the current cost per click, for example 10 cents.
    2. Raise the bid in the evening, for example to 15 cents.
    3. The next day, check impressions, clicks and sales.
    4. If results rise but not enough, raise again (for example to 20 cents) and repeat daily.
    5. Stop when ads get impressions and clicks and sales are profitable. This is the sweet spot, the best balance point.

  • There is no universal average bid. Cost per click varies by niche (the market your product sits in), brand and product group. Judge a bid by the results it produces, not by a fixed number. **

Raising Bids Carefully

1 min
  • Do not double all bids at once when spend is low. Raise them in measured steps. **
  • Low spend with low impressions means other advertisers are bidding higher. A bid of a fraction of a cent would spend almost nothing, even with a million-dollar budget. **
  • Raise bids, not budgets, when a daily budget is not being spent and impressions are low, because low bids lose auctions to competitors. **
  • Raise bids on products that you want to sell but that get no impressions, so they start appearing. **
  • Before raising CPC in a new account:
    1. Review the past month of data.
    2. Make the optimisations recommended in the optimisation section, using that data.
    3. Then raise CPC so the full daily budget is spent on the products with the most potential.

Product-Level Bids

1 min

Why Each Product Needs Its Own Bid

1 min
  • Conversion rates (the share of visitors who buy) differ strongly between products, even between size or colour variants of one product. A single bid overpays for weak products and underbids strong ones. Set bids per product.
  • In one real store, product conversion rates ranged from 0.2 to 3.2%, with most between 0.6 and 0.9%. The best product converted 16 times better than the weakest (3.2 ÷ 0.2).
  • Leaving dozens of products under one shared bid hides large differences in conversion rate and cost per sale. Strong products get underfunded and weak products keep spending.
  • A 30-day campaign example: $671 spend, $5,278 revenue, 1.45% conversion rate, 7.8× ROAS. Products within it ranged from 0.5% to 8.3% conversion rate and from 0.04 to 90 ROAS, despite nearly identical bids.
  • Do not bid the same amount on products with very different conversion rates and ROAS. Each product needs a bid tailored to your profitability target.

Applying the Formula to Products

1 min
  • Strong performer: Product 1 shows an RPC of $11.78 with a 6× target ROAS. $11.78 ÷ 6 = $1.96 target CPC. Raise the bid from $1.00 to $1.96, because this product converts above target and can bid more aggressively for more traffic.
  • The principle: when RPC is above the target, the strong conversion rate supports the higher CPC. Higher bids increase volume while keeping you profitable.
  • A strong product with low search impression share (the share of matching searches where your ad appears, 29% in the example) gains both traffic and impression share when its bid is raised. This captures demand you are missing.
  • Weak performer: Product 2 shows an RPC of $5.62 with a current bid of $1.00. $5.62 ÷ 6 = $0.93 target CPC. Reduce the bid to $0.93 to move it toward target profitability.
  • For products with very low RPC compared to target (for example, $0.56 RPC with a 6× target gives $0.09 target CPC), either pause the product to free budget and inventory for better performers, or reduce the bid to the calculated amount and continue at minimal scale.
  • A product's maximum CPC can be lowered when it is overpriced, so Google spends the budget on products that convert. **
  • Remove products that do not reach the target ROAS. Move the remaining budget to the most profitable products. **
  • Lowering CPC is preferred to removal for low performers. They can still bring free organic traffic (visits from search results without ads) or profitable sales at lower ad costs. **
  • Lower CPC on unprofitable products after months of data. After six to seven months, reduce CPC possibly to zero, or moderately, for example from $0.50 to $0.25. Add negative keywords to remove irrelevant search terms first. **

Products That Are Far Above Competitors or Not Converting

1 min
  • For a product whose CPC is far above competitors and above what its selling items pay, lower the bid to just under its current average CPC first, not to a calculated target. A bigger cut is only justified if the item is not selling at all.
  • Example: in one product group (a set of similar items), the only selling item paid about $1.40 per click, while the others paid $3.00 and $5.44. Lowering the overpaying items was expected to bring the group to a ROAS of 6 or better within about 30 days.
  • Example: a product with 190 clicks and $300 spend and no sale should have sold by now. Pausing it redirected the budget to the top product. In the example, this lifted campaign ROAS from 4.2 to about 6.0 without losing revenue.
  • Alternative to pausing: reduce the bid drastically (example: to $0.50) and keep some low-cost traffic. Do this only if you are confident the conversion rate can be improved through website work.
  • Adjust bids before pausing. Poor ROAS is often a symptom of wrong bids.
  • In a two-keyword example, the weak keyword (ROAS 1.8, bid $304, cost $4,100) was not paused. Its bid was lowered to $211. Its ROAS rose to 3.9 over time, its cost per click fell, and its conversion rate stayed the same.
  • Where the actual CPC is far below the calculated target because competition is low, keep the bid above the formula value.
  • A product that gets impressions but not enough clicks may need a higher CPC. Test increases on the top 20% of valuable products first.

Prioritising Products

1 min
  • Raise CPC first on the 20% of highest-price products that are not getting impressions. Monitor impressions and clicks, then slowly repeat for the remaining products. This prioritises valuable inventory. **
  • Aim for high impression share only on top-performing products. Segment out low performers, meaning split them into their own group.

Segment Bids

Why Segments Multiply the Work

1 min
  • Google Ads lets you set separate bids by gender, age range, household income, location, day of week, hour of day, and device. Each of these dimensions (a way to split your shoppers into groups) multiplies the number of bids for one keyword. Manual management quickly becomes impossible. This is the main reason to use automated bidding.
  • Dimensions interact. A group that looks valuable overall may only be valuable within certain age ranges. Analysing combinations can reverse conclusions drawn from a single dimension. Segment bids by combinations, not one factor alone.
  • Choose bid dimensions based on price point. For high-ticket products (expensive items), household income matters, because many buyers in lower income brackets cannot afford the item.
  • Apply the same bid optimisation formula to all dimensions, not just products.

Demographics

1 min

  • Procedure for a target CPC per segment (a segment is one group of shoppers, for example by gender):

    1. Pull the number of clicks for each segment, for example men and women.
    2. Pull the revenue for each segment.
    3. Calculate RPC for each segment by dividing revenue by clicks.
    4. Divide RPC by the target ROAS to get the target CPC for each segment.
    5. Repeat for each added dimension, such as age ranges and income levels.

    In one example, women converted 56% more valuably than men.

  • Adjust bids on age ranges, geographic locations, and time periods by their individual RPC versus target ROAS.

Devices

1 min

  • Keep desktop and mobile traffic in one campaign rather than separate ad groups, because separating them showed no clear gains. If mobile converts much worse, reduce mobile bid adjustments by roughly −20% to −30%. **

  • Procedure for mobile and desktop bids:

    1. Run the campaign on both mobile and desktop at the start.
    2. Track conversion rates by device over time.
    3. If mobile converts clearly lower, open the campaign settings and decrease mobile bids by a percentage.
    4. Example: a desktop bid of $0.50 per click with a 10% decrease for mobile.

  • Device bid adjustments must be based on maths, not guesswork. Increase bids on mobile if mobile converts better than your target ROAS. Decrease bids on tablets and other devices using the same RPC formula.

  • Device adjustment formula: device conversion value per cost (ROAS) ÷ target ROAS − 1. Example: mobile conversion value over cost 3.55 with target 6 gives 3.55 ÷ 6 = 0.59, minus 1 = −41%, so mobile bids are reduced by 41%.

  • Do not adjust a device with too few clicks. Example: a tablet with 29 clicks lacks confidence, meaning there is not enough data to trust.

  • Leave device bid modifiers (percentage changes to a bid) unset and bid equally on all devices. Around 80% of internet traffic is mobile, and most Google Shopping conversions happen on mobile. **

Locations

1 min

  • Google Ads offers 55,423 geographic biddable locations (places where you can set their own bid). Location modifiers can be set far below country or state level. A state can carry a modifier such as 10% more value, but rural and urban areas inside a large state can convert very differently. Review at a finer level before settling on modifiers.

  • Geographic bid adjustment workflow:

    1. Open the geographic report.
    2. Set the time range (example: all time). Remove the columns for location type, most specific location and city, leaving the region or state level.
    3. Only make decisions on regions with at least 100 clicks. With fewer clicks the data is too noisy, unless the conversion rate is very high (around 3.3% or more).
    4. Sort from high to low and compare conversion rate and ROAS per state.
    5. Save the data and export it to Excel for analysis.
    6. Calculate the bid adjustment: divide the state's current ROAS by the target ROAS, subtract 1, and apply the result as a percentage.
    7. Enter the state in the campaign's location targeting and enter the adjustment percentage.

Time of Day and Day of Week

1 min
  • Control the schedule of ads by time of day. Switch off unprofitable hours (the example given is 22:00 to 04:00) and raise bids for periods of higher demand, such as Valentine's Day for gift buyers.
  • Time periods use the same RPC versus target ROAS formula as other dimensions.

Pause Thresholds

1 min
  • Pause threshold formula: divide 1 by the average conversion rate (the share of clicks that become sales). This gives the minimum expected clicks per sale.
  • Then multiply by 1.5 to add a 50% safety buffer (extra room for bad luck).
  • At a 1.2% conversion rate: (1 ÷ 0.012) × 1.5 = 125. This is usually rounded to 120 clicks without a sale as the pause trigger.
  • Standard thresholds by conversion rate:
Conversion rate Threshold Source
1.2% Pause at 120 clicks without conversion (minimum 83 clicks plus 50% buffer)
1.0% Pause at 100 clicks
2.0% Evaluate every 50 clicks

  • The text does not say which one applies. Check both before pausing.
  • Example: a geographic area (a region, here South Carolina) has 122 clicks and no sale at a 1.2% average conversion rate. It has passed the 120-click threshold.
  • It is getting close to the point where lowering bids or pausing that location makes financial sense.
  • Do not make major bid or pause decisions on products with fewer than 100 clicks.
  • Instead, extend the data window (the span of time you look at), for example from 30 to 60 days, to collect enough clicks.

1 min
  • A data window is the number of past days you look at.
  • A 60-day window gives more reliable data than a 30-day window with fewer clicks per product.
  • Example: $1,438 spend, $8,200 conversion value (the total sales value), and 5.8× ROAS (return on ad spend, the money earned back for each dollar spent) against a 6× target.
  • Use a 60-day window when possible to collect more data.
  • Only apply the bid formula to items that have passed the minimum click threshold.

  • Add the revenue-per-click column (RPC, the sales money each click brings in) for product-level optimisation.

  • Before raising CPC (cost per click, what you pay each time someone clicks) in a new account, use the past month of data (see Raising Bids Carefully).

Enhanced CPC

1 min
  • Enhanced CPC uses your manual bid (the bid you set yourself) as a starting point.
  • It lets Google raise bids on searches likely to lead to a sale, and keeps your normal bid for other searches.
  • It sits between full manual control and full automation.
  • Google raises a manual bid when a search looks more likely to convert (lead to a sale). It lowers the bid when a sale looks unlikely.
  • It compares your sales history with customer behaviour. Without sales data, it only guesses. **
  • Enhanced CPC works well only after enough sales data exists.
  • For Search and Display, new campaigns no longer offer Enhanced CPC, because Google retired it (deprecated).
  • New campaigns should use Target ROAS. (2024)
  • Existing campaigns keep Enhanced CPC until it is switched off in March.
  • Do not change them in the meantime. (2024)
  • Enhanced CPC can be switched on later in the campaign settings once sales are tracked. **

Target ROAS

1 min
  • Target ROAS is a setting where you tell Google the return you want on ad spend.
  • Do not use Target ROAS in a new account. Without sales data, Google has nothing to optimise against (improve for). **
  • Do not set a target far above current performance.
  • If current ROAS is 3×, start at 5×. Move to 8× only after 5× is reached.
  • Larger jumps create volatility (unstable results) and slow the strategy down.

Setup procedure:

  1. In Google Ads, use the search bar at the top of the account.
  2. Type "bid" and select portfolio bid strategies. A portfolio bid strategy is one set of bid rules shared across several campaigns.
  3. Create a new strategy and choose Target ROAS bidding.
  4. Give the strategy a name.
  5. Enter the target ROAS as a percentage, where 5× is entered as 500%.
  6. When asked for a minimum or maximum bid, leave the fields empty, because setting them is not advised.
  • Portfolio setup: choose the target return on ad spend option and enter the target as a percentage (example: 600).
  • Minimum and maximum bid fields under advanced options are usually left empty.
  • Set Target ROAS as the bidding strategy once you have 30 or more conversions (sales) per month.
  • For alpha (the name of one of your campaigns), enter the calculated target, for example 3.3×.
  • Once active, the portfolio strategy takes over individual product and bid adjustments.

Maximize Clicks

1 min
  • Do not use Maximize Clicks unless the goal is traffic volume over profit.
  • Maximize Clicks gets as many clicks as the budget allows.
  • Use Manual CPC instead of Maximize Clicks in a new account. Manual CPC means you set each bid yourself.

Setting Up a Shared Budget

Limited by Budget

  • "Limited by budget" means the daily budget runs out before the day ends. The campaign is capped.

Starting bids and the inch-up method

Starting bids and the first weeks:

  1. Start with a bid of $0.20.
  2. Check the clicks the next day.
  3. If the campaign is on track for fewer than 100 clicks per week, raise the bid by $0.10.
  4. Check again whether the campaign is on track for 100 clicks per week.
  5. If not, raise the bid a little further and check again.
  6. Repeat every week until the Shopping campaign gets about 100 clicks per week.

A conversion is a completed sale. The conversion rate is the share of clicks that become sales. At a 1% conversion rate, 100 clicks per week should bring about one conversion per week. Whether this is acceptable depends on the site's economics (the money the store keeps after costs). It may work at 0.5% or fail at 2%.

Inch-up method for raising Shopping bids (about 20% per step). "Inch up" means raising slowly, in small steps:

  1. Raise the CPC (cost per click, the price you pay per click) by about 20 percent (for example 10 cents to 12 cents, 25 cents to 30 cents).
  2. Let it run for a few days.
  3. If performance holds or improves, raise again by about 20 percent.
  4. If the same or fewer sales come in for more money, step back one increment (one small step) and keep that bid.
  5. Do not double bids, because Google will spend the extra budget on whatever traffic exists and the bid will likely be overpaid.

Bid formulas and CPA targets

3 min

Worked example for the generic term 'men's sunglasses' (a broad search phrase, not a brand) with a 0.5% conversion rate, a 200 USD cart value (the average order) and 1,000 clicks: a 0.25 USD bid costs 250 USD and returns 1,000 USD (about 4x, meaning 4 times the money back), while a 1.50 USD bid costs 1,500 USD for the same 1,000 USD and loses money.

  • Before setting bids you need a target cost per acquisition (CPA, the ad cost to make one sale) for each product and its current CPA. The gap between current and target tells you how much to raise or lower the bid.
  • Setting bids once and never adjusting them wastes budget, because low-converting products keep drawing expensive clicks. Review bids against a target CPA using real data.
  • Bid modifier (the percentage change to a bid) = (target CPA / current CPA) - 1. A negative result means a bid reduction of that percentage. Examples: current CPA 50 with target 25 gives -50%; current CPA 4 with target 2 gives -50%; current CPA 135 with target 50 gives about -63%.
  • Apply the modifier to the current max CPC: new max CPC = current max CPC x (1 + modifier). A modifier of -50% halves the bid and +50% raises it by half. Calculate the modifier first, then enter the resulting bid in the campaign.
  • Mobile and time-of-day bid adjustments use the same calculation: take the CPA for that segment (a group, such as mobile users, or a specific day), divide your target CPA by it and subtract one. The result is the bid modifier for that segment.
  • Limit any single bid change to about 50%, even if the calculation suggests more, such as a 101% increase. Make the rest of the change after more data has come in, so the decision rests on a statistically meaningful sample (enough data to trust the result).
  • Keep the target CPA for every product in one spreadsheet in a fixed order, so the bid calculation runs consistently each time. Products with different margins (the profit left per sale) will have different targets.
  • Daily budget for a target number of clicks equals the average CPC over about seven days multiplied by the target clicks. For example, an average CPC of $1.00 with a target of 100 clicks needs a daily budget of about $100.

Optimise product bids in a spreadsheet:

  1. In the Shopping campaign, open the product groups tab (sets of products that share a bid) and click the download tab to export the data.
  2. Open the file in Excel or Google Sheets (spreadsheet programs) and check the columns: campaign, ad group (a set of ads inside a campaign), product group, partition type (how products are split into groups), max CPC and cost per conversion.
  3. Add a column with your target CPA for each product.
  4. Enter the formula target CPA / current CPA - 1 in the first row, then drag it down to all products.
  5. Apply the resulting bid changes to the campaign.
  • AdWords Editor (Google's free desktop app for editing many ads at once) imports hundreds of bid changes at once, which is faster than editing products one by one in the interface. Use it after you have calculated the bid changes in the spreadsheet.
  • AdWords rules and scripts (automatic tools that change ads) generally cannot be applied to product listing ad campaigns (Shopping ad campaigns), although they work for text ads. For bulk changes to Shopping campaigns, the Optimizer tool (a bulk-change helper) or the spreadsheet method (free) were the options given.

Decisions on products without sales

2 min
  • Products without sales yet can stay in the feed (the product list sent to Google) at a very low bid, for example one cent, until they reach about 100 unique clicks (each person counted once). Only remove products that you do not want to sell.
  • For products with zero conversions, first look at a longer date range (a longer time period), because some items sell only every few months. If they still have no sales, pause them (stop showing them) or set a very low bid, such as 0.10 USD, and collect more data.
  • Lower CPC on products that have spent at least 50 without a conversion, even down to one cent, so the budget flows toward products that have already converted.
  • When a product has at least 100 clicks from Google Shopping and no sales, review its product page and the top two or three competitor results before reducing its cost per click or removing it. Cut waste by adding negative keywords (search words that should block your ad).
  • A product that receives 200 unique clicks in Google Ads without conversions, even after a competitor comparison and page optimisation, should have its cost per click reduced to limit its spend.
  • Products that cost money but do not convert: lower the default CPC instead of removing them (example: 0.50 to 0.30). Test lower bids for a few days and see whether the product becomes profitable.
  • If a product gets many clicks and a high impression share but no conversions, first check the product page conversion elements in the store (the parts of the page that make people buy), then lower its max CPC so it does not take budget.
  • Lower the CPC on products that spend money but do not sell, and keep it higher on high-profit products or products that already convert. This directs more of the daily budget to the products that earn a return.
  • Lowering the bid on an unprofitable product does not stop its traffic entirely. It moves budget toward proven products and can make the remaining sales profitable.
  • ** Lowering Google Shopping bids without optimising the product catalog (your full list of products) and negative keywords can lead to traffic that does not convert. Lower bids should go together with catalog and campaign optimisation.
  • A high cost per click is not automatically a problem, because some clicks may convert profitably. Analyse spend by keyword and product before cutting bids or budgets.

Low-visibility and zero-click products

3 min
  • A product with fewer than 100 impressions after a few weeks in campaign should have its CPC raised to get Google to show it. Products with over 100 impressions are judged by click-through rate and conversions before changing bids.
  • Example: a store with 100 products where 40 products get under 50 impressions after a month should increase CPC for those 40 products. The aim is to give them enough exposure to get data.
  • For products over 100 impressions, raise the bid when click-through rate and conversions are good, and lower the bid when clicks cost money without sales. Make the decision separately for alpha and beta campaigns.
  • Products with very low impressions can get more visibility by raising their target cost per click.
  • Older (2018)? Not used. [-]

Inching up bids for products with no impressions:

  1. Do not raise all campaign bids at once.
  2. Open the product groups in the Alpha campaign and select only the products with no impressions.
  3. Raise each one from its current CPC by a small step, for example from $1.70 to $1.95 or $2.00.
  4. Let it run for one week.
  5. If impressions are still missing, raise it in small steps again.
  • Raising all bids from $1.70 to $3.00 at once can overspend on products that already convert and does not fix the real cause of missing impressions. Missing impressions can come from low search demand (few people searching for it), weak relevance (your ad does not match the search) or too much competition, so check these first.
  • A Shopping product with a high bid spends at the level the traffic supports, so a high bid on a product with a lot of search demand will spend heavily. Bids should therefore be inched up rather than raised sharply.
  • ** Products with very low impressions and clicks can be pushed with small bid increases.

Raising bids for products with no clicks:

  1. Keep high-performing products (over 50 clicks, profitable) at current bids or raise them.
  2. Raise CPC on zero-click products in small steps (for example 0.50 to 0.60, then 0.65) and wait a few days each time.
  3. Do the same with products under 10 clicks, moving up more slowly (for example 0.55, then 0.60).
  4. Then decide budget changes based on results.
  • Raising the total daily budget on a campaign usually sends most of the extra money to products that already get clicks, so zero-click products stay at zero. To give them traffic, raise their cost per click in small steps and wait a few days after each step. Only then decide on budget changes.

Pushing sales on products that get few clicks:

  1. Keep the CPC on the top-clicking product at its current level, such as 50 cents.
  2. Raise the CPC on the other products, such as to 60 cents.
  3. Wait a few days and check the results.
  4. Keep raising CPC until those products start spending and the daily budget is used up.
  5. Only then increase the daily budget.
  • Raise a converting product's traffic: find the keyword and product behind the first sale in Google Ads, check whether impressions and clicks are low for it, and if so, raise the default cost per click for that product in Google Shopping, since it has already converted.
  • ** Keep the same one to two weekly sales while lowering spend. See the cutting section below.

Bid adjustments on converting products

2 min
  • Bid adjustments tell Google to bid less for products that do not convert and more for products that earn the most. This concentrates the budget on items that make money.
  • Example: a product with a return on ad spend of 20 but a search impression share of 30% is profitable and under-shown (shown too rarely). Raise its max CPC from $0.40 to $0.50, then check again one week later.
  • Adjust max CPC per product in Google Shopping:
    1. Go to Campaigns, open the alpha campaign, click Ad groups, and open the alpha ad group to see the product groups.
    2. Product groups are subdivided per product. Each product starts with the same max CPC of $0.40.
    3. Review each product's clicks, cost, conversion value, return on ad spend and search impression share, ideally every one to two weeks.
    4. If the return is above 10X and the search impression share is below 70%, raise the max CPC for that product (for example from $0.40 to $0.50), then re-check after about one week.
    5. Keep raising the max CPC in weekly steps while the return stays above 10X and the impression share stays below 70%, until the spend limit is reached or the return drops.
    6. If a product gets many clicks and a high impression share but no conversions, first check the product page conversion elements in the store, then lower its max CPC so it does not take budget.
    7. If a product has a low impression share (for example 20%) and no clicks, raise its max CPC to make it visible and test whether it sells.
    8. Repeat the same steps in the beta ad group.
  • The Benchmarks max CPC column (the column showing the bid Google suggests) shows a bid that is always higher than what a product needs. Do not use it to set your own max CPC. It can be removed from the product group columns.
  • Google Shopping placement depends on both bid and relevance, so a highly relevant campaign can get cheaper clicks even at lower bids. Relevance is improved through negative keywords and a well-optimised product catalog.
  • ** Optimising Google Shopping before lowering bids: add negative keywords to block irrelevant search terms; optimise the product catalog so each product is described clearly; lower bids if high bids did not convert, and keep monitoring. Review campaigns at least every other week, and probably weekly when you are first starting.

Cutting cost on an underperforming supplier (a supplier doing worse than the others):

  1. Open the product groups view inside Google Ads that shows each product's cost per click.
  2. Lower the CPC for only the underperforming supplier's products, leaving the default CPC for alpha and beta unchanged.
  3. Set the reduction based on profitability. If a supplier costs $1 to make $5 in sales, halve its CPC to reach $0.50 per $5.
  4. Optionally, split the supplier into its own campaign (e.g. alpha beta two) and keep other suppliers in the original campaign.
  • Google Ads spend should go toward clicks that are likely to convert, especially at the start. Optimise around keywords that bring quality clicks and products that sell at specific price points.

Reducing spend and scaling winners

2 min

Case: after raising bids (the price you offer per click), daily traffic rose from 2 to 3 visitors. It got so high that it used up the daily budget (the most you let Google spend each day). One product had over 800 views and no sales, and it used much of the budget. Actions: add negative keywords (search words that stop your ad from showing) for window-shopper terms (searches from people who only look and do not buy). Use exact or phrase match (ways to tell Google how closely a search must match your keyword) for niche keywords. Lower the CPC (cost per click, what you pay each time someone clicks) for only that product, for example from 50 cents to 30 cents. Other products keep the default CPC, so spend shifts to them.

  • To control spend per product, open the Shopping campaign (Google ads that show product photos and prices). Break down the results by product ID (the code number of each product). Lower the CPC for that one product, not the whole campaign.
  • Lowering the CPC on a product that takes many impressions (how often your ad is shown) but matters little to you moves budget to your other products.
  • For example, if 800 of 1,000 impressions go to one product you do not care about, reduce its bid so Google shifts budget elsewhere.

Cutting a Google Shopping budget without losing sales:

  1. Review keywords for impressions, clicks, CPC and sales.
  2. Add keywords that bring clicks but no sales and are unrelated as negatives (blocked search words).
  3. Review product groups (sets of products you group together) and find the top spenders, about 30% of budget.
  4. Lower CPC on high-spend, low-conversion product groups (products that cost a lot but rarely sell) without removing them from the feed (the product list you send to Google).
  5. Raise CPC on product groups that are selling if you want more volume.

Case: a new store spent $45 per day on Google ads and made one to two sales per week. It wanted to cut spend to $30 per day. The fix was to review the keyword list, add unrelated no-sale keywords as negatives to remove waste, and lower CPC on high-spend product groups that did not sell. The goal was to keep the same one to two weekly sales while dropping about $15 of daily waste, and then to scale the winners (products that sell well).

Reducing the cost of a large product group while protecting proven sellers

1 min
  1. Identify the products that generated most of the revenue (money from sales), for example 11,000 USD from 4 out of 200 products.
  2. Lower the CPC for the remaining products to a low level, for example from 1 USD to 0.20 USD, to get cheap traffic.
  3. Lower the total daily budget, for example from 50 to 30 USD per day.
  4. Keep most of the budget on the proven products, a roughly 80/20 split.
  5. Raise CPC on low-cost products only once they start converting (turning visitors into buyers).
  • Allocate about 80% of the ad budget to proven winning products and about 20% to new products being tested.

Reducing wasted ad spend when a product brings traffic but does not pay for itself

1 min
  1. Check how much was spent on impressions and clicks for each product that generated a sale.
  2. If a sale costs a large share of its value, for example 50 to 100 USD in ads for a 215 USD sale, lower that product group's CPC, for example from 50 cents to 25 cents.
  3. Move the remaining budget toward products where the profit (money left after costs) is higher.

Reducing spend on a store with many products and little traffic-to-sales conversion

1 min
  1. Review historical (past) Google Ads spend by keyword and by product.
  2. Remove products that consume budget without sales, or lower their CPC.
  3. Move the freed budget (money no longer used) to products that convert.
  4. Compare any product page with 100 or more unique visitors (different people, each counted once) and no sales against the top two competitors.

Lowering bids on unprofitable products

2 min
  1. Open the alpha campaign (one of your two Shopping campaigns, named alpha) and then the beta campaign, one at a time.
  2. Open the Product groups view, where each product appears with its Max CPC (the highest price per click you allow).
  3. Leave the winning products at their current bid.
  4. For unprofitable products, open the bid setting (the gear icon, a small wheel symbol) and lower Max CPC, for example from $0.50 to $0.30 or $0.25.
  5. Do this in both campaigns and check the account the next day. Lower bids further if spend is still going to the wrong products.

  • The main lever for controlling Google Shopping spend across alpha and beta campaigns is the cost per click set per product in each campaign.

  • Negative keywords should be correct before you adjust CPC.

  • For low-margin (small profit per sale) or low-priced products, set a very low default CPC (the base price per click for products without their own price), for example 2 to 3 cents. They are rarely shown but cost little.

  • Products with real margin should take most of the budget.

  • When traffic is spread across both Alpha and Beta campaigns, apply CPC increases to both, based on the traffic data.

  • For non-MAP products (products not covered by a MAP, the minimum advertised price a brand lets sellers show) that do not leave enough profit at competitor prices:

    1. Open both the Alpha and the Beta Shopping campaigns.
    2. Set the CPC of every non-MAP product to 0.01 dollars (one cent).
    3. Leave the existing bids on MAP products unchanged.

    This tells Google not to spend much on uncompetitive products (products where others sell cheaper), so the budget goes to products that earn money.

  • For all products priced under $200, set the Google Ads CPC to $0.01 so that they do not absorb budget.

  • Direct spend toward products that earn more when they sell.

  • Add-on products (extras sold with a main product) under $200 that sell well can receive the same $0.01 CPC, or be excluded entirely.

Budget or bid: which one to change

1 min
  • If a campaign does not spend its full daily budget, the audience may be too small or the bid too low for impressions. Increase the bid, not the budget.
  • If the budget is spent every day, keep the bid where it is and increase the budget.
  • Google Ads budget logic: if a campaign does not spend its full daily budget, raise the bid. If it spends the full daily budget every day, raise the budget.
  • Do not raise the ad budget while the account is not profitable, even if Google says the campaign is budget limited.
  • Google benefits from larger budgets, and a higher budget spreads spend across less qualified traffic (visitors less likely to buy).
  • Raise the budget only when the campaign keeps spending its full daily budget and the traffic quality is still good.

Procedure: when the daily budget is not being spent:

  1. Compare the daily budget with the daily ad spend in Google Ads.
  2. If the full daily budget is not being spent, raise the default CPC by 10 cents, for example from 20 cents to 30 cents.
  3. Monitor traffic for one to two weeks.
  4. If the daily budget is still not spent, raise the default CPC by another 10 cents.
  • After raising the default CPC by 10 cents, check traffic again about one week later.
  • If traffic has not increased and the daily budget is still not fully spent, raise the default CPC by another 10 cents.
  • ** Procedure to raise the default CPC: wait a few days, step up 5 to 10 cents at a time, and decide budget changes only after bids have had time to work. (see the bid steps above)
  • ** If only about 10% of a daily budget is being spent, your bids are far too low. (see the bid steps above)

Scaling budgets gradually

1 min
  • Larger daily budgets reach wider audiences, so return on ad spend (ROAS, money earned per dollar spent on ads) tends to fall as budget grows.
  • The smallest budgets capture the highest-intent searches (people searching with a clear plan to buy) and usually give the best return.
  • Scaling is still possible, but expect the return to drop as reach widens.
  • The "limited by budget" status (Google's label saying your budget is holding back ads) alone does not explain poor performance. See the section below.
  • ** Google often recommends large budget jumps that are not needed. Increase budgets gradually, for example from 20 to 30 dollars for about a week, then raise them again in steps.
  • ** Use the inch-up approach (small steps, an inch at a time) for budgets: raise the daily budget slightly, about 10%, while watching whether spend increases and the quality of the traffic holds.

Scale Google Shopping from the start:

  1. Set the daily budget and default CPC across all products.
  2. If the budget is not spent, look at which products get impressions and clicks.
  3. Raise CPC on products with too few impressions and clicks.
  4. Add negative keywords and lower CPC on products that get clicks but no sales.
  5. When campaigns reach a ROAS of 10x or higher (ten dollars earned per dollar spent) and the daily budget is maxed every day, raise the daily budget.
  • Doubling the daily budget has worked once everything is dialled in (set up and tuned). Google budgets can be scaled faster than Facebook budgets.
  • Expect three to five days of bad results after a jump.
  • Let it run about a week, and scale back if it does not level out.
  • Scaling is the path, but the goal is the highest spend that still reaches target ROAS (the return you aim for), not the highest traffic.

Limited by budget

1 min
  • Google Ads shows "limited by budget" on almost all campaigns. This is normal, because Google wants you to spend more.
  • The status alone does not explain poor performance.
  • A Google Shopping "limited by budget" message is not a warning and does not require raising the budget.
  • Hovering over it (moving your mouse over it) shows estimated conversions at higher budgets.
  • Do not raise the ad budget while the account is not profitable, even if Google says the campaign is budget limited.

Segment bids: device, demographics, geography and time

2 min

Segments are groups of people or places. Device means phone, tablet or computer. Demographics means age, income and similar traits. Geography means location.

Device bids:

  • Start with no adjustments for mobile (phones) or desktop (computers).
  • Once sales come in, review the conversion split in Google Ads. If mobile produces no sales, reduce the mobile bid, for example to 50% of desktop.
  • Adjust device bids in Google Ads by going to Campaigns, opening the device settings, and increasing or decreasing the bid for mobile, tablet or desktop.
  • For example, reduce mobile by 30 percent if desktop converts better.
  • Mobile bids can be raised or lowered when setting up campaigns. This is useful when most traffic comes from phones (example: 80% mobile).
  • Mobile bid adjustment in a Google Shopping campaign: open the campaign's bid adjustment for mobile devices, choose decrease, and set it to 100% to exclude mobile traffic.
  • Google Ads > bid adjustments for devices > mobile set to about -50% relative to desktop.
  • Test this setting, and raise mobile bids again if return on ad spend drops.
  • Example: with a -95% mobile bid adjustment on a $100 ad set (a group of ads sharing one budget and settings), Google put about $95 toward desktop and only about $5 toward mobile.
  • Sales and return on ad spend then dropped across the board.
  • Cutting mobile bids to nearly zero can also lower desktop conversions.
  • Mobile visitors who return later on desktop to buy stop being reached.
  • Adjust search bids by device (mobile vs. desktop) based on where sales are strong.
  • Google Ads also allows bid differences by gender (added in the 12 months before this guidance). Raise bids for segments more likely to convert, such as men buying gifts.

Demographic and geographic bids:

  • Google Ads lets you exclude the lower 50 percent of household income from seeing search ads, shown in the geographic settings of the demonstration.

  • This suits premium or luxury products.

  • Age range, financial status and other demographic options can also be used to build better-targeted search campaigns.

  • Do not bid by location, time of day, or device at the budget levels most stores use.

  • The added control usually hurts performance.

  • Time-of-day bids follow the segment formula in the section "Bid formulas and CPA targets" (CPA = cost per acquisition, what one sale costs you).

Where sources differ:

  • The 2023 guidance starts with no device adjustment and reduces mobile only after it proves weak.
  • The 2018 and 2019 examples applied large cuts (-100% and -95%) that cut desktop conversions too.
  • The device adjustment should be made gradually, and a large cut should be reversed if return falls.

Keyword estimates, benchmarks and competitive position

2 min
  • Pre-launch average cost-per-click figures from keyword research tools do not predict what an alpha campaign will pay.
  • This is because you do not bid on generic keywords (broad, common search words).
  • Use the cost data from your own campaign dashboard after the ads start running.
  • ** Do not use the niche research tool's data to estimate CPC.
  • Its data is based on search text ads that are not run for niche keywords.
  • ** Legal and malpractice-type search terms (claims of professional negligence) can carry estimated cost-per-clicks around $80.
  • These figures do not apply to product listing ads (Google Shopping product ads) in the same niche.
  • Keywords for products sold by one or two small craftspeople usually cost less on Google Shopping than keywords for the top brands.
  • This is because there is less competition for the same searches.
  • ** In Google Shopping, most shoppers click one of the top three listings, so an identical offer ranked fourth gets few clicks.
  • Moving up requires higher bids or an offer that stands out from competitors, not a higher listed price.
  • ** Search the product as a customer on Google, compare competitor prices, look for MAP violations (sellers breaking the minimum price rule), and compare offers such as free shipping.
  • Also check where your listing appears among the competing stores.
  • If pricing and offers are competitive, make a small bid adjustment to move your listing higher.

Diagnosis from the Benchmark column (Google's typical figures for your category):

  • If the average CPC is well below the Benchmark max CPC, but click share (the part of all possible clicks you get) and search impression share (the part of matching searches where your ad shows) are low, and search impression share lost due to rank (shows missed because your ad ranked too low) is high, the bids are too low and should be raised.
  • Example: a CPC of about $0.30 against a $0.91 benchmark with 16% click share, 34% search impression share and 65% of impressions lost to rank points to raising bids.
  • If the cost per click already looks too high to raise bids, the likely problem is the conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy).
  • In that case, remove bad keywords and search terms, and optimise the website.

Where sources differ:

  • The Benchmark column is not used to set bids.
  • It is used only as a diagnostic signal (a clue for finding problems), to compare your CPC and share of impressions with what competitors pay.
