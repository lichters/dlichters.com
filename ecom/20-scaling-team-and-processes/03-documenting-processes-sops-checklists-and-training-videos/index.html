Documenting processes: SOPs, checklists and training videos

Section 20.3 · 3 of 26 in this chapter · 22 min read

In short

  • Write down each task as fixed steps before you hire anyone, so someone else can do it.
  • Only write down tasks that already make money.
  • Keep each guide short and simple, with pictures.
  • Keep all guides in one shared place (Google Drive) and share the link, never copies.
  • Test each guide with someone who does not know your business.
More key points
  • Write it down first. Make a step-by-step guide (an SOP, or standard operating procedure) for each task before you hire anyone or hand it off.
  • Answer customers yourself first. Save every repeated question and its approved answer, then turn them into a playbook (a written guide of answers) for your helper.
  • Film a hard task once. A recorded video shows the whole task. Add a written SOP for quick checks.
  • Build systems, not habits. A repeatable system gives the same result every time, even when someone else runs it.

Why document before you delegate

1 min
  • Write down your business systems and processes before you need to hire. This lets you hire without disrupting the business.
  • Writing down your processes gives you a repeatable plan for training new people.
  • A documented system gives three benefits:
    • It makes handing over work smooth.
    • It means less stress for you, the owner.
    • It raises the business value (what a buyer would pay for the business).
  • Record every process from the very start, even before you hire anyone. Someone else will need the knowledge one day.
  • A business where all knowledge sits only in the owner's head ends when the owner is away, sick or unavailable.
  • If all processes live only in your head, you cannot hand over any work. Write down processes and build a system before you hire anyone.
  • Do not wait for a big life event to force you to build systems. Start while you still run the work yourself, so a new hire can take over when revenue allows (when there is money to pay them).
  • When an ecommerce brand (an online store that sells products) grows fast, three problems tend to appear:
    • Operations become unstable.
    • Data is spread across systems that do not connect.
    • Priorities lose clarity.
  • Build standard systems and processes during growth, so that scale (getting bigger) stays sustainable.
  • Outsourcing (paying outside people to do tasks) needs SOPs (standard operating procedures, which are written step-by-step guides) and training.
  • At first, outsourcing feels like a step backwards. It frees your time later. Expect several months of stress before the benefit appears. **
  • New staff need written processes more than experienced staff do. Without them, they miss steps that are routine for the owner. **

What to document first

2 min
  • Document only processes that already make money. Scaling an unprofitable task adds waste and makes a small business bloated (too big and slow).
  • Check that a process is profitable before you hire anyone for it. Hiring for an unproven process means paying someone to learn something you have not yet proven works.
  • Start with the repetitive, time-consuming tasks that eat your hours.
  • Do customer service and daily operations yourself at the start, even if it means very long weeks. This lets you write down how the store works.
  • Outsource only after you have documented processes to hand over. **
  • Learn every task yourself before you delegate it. This includes customer reply templates (ready-made answers), comment moderation (checking and handling comments) and assigning a page moderator (a person who manages a page's comments).
  • Then record your screen with a tool like Loom (a free tool that records your screen, camera and voice). Give the recordings to new hires for training.
  • Answer customer questions yourself for the first period of the business.
  • Systemize repeatable tasks (turn them into fixed routines) so they can be done efficiently and consistently.
  • Systemizing uses four methods: reverse engineering (taking a finished result apart to see how it was made), outsourcing, automating (letting software do it) and writing fixed steps.
  • Systemizing is the basis for scaling the business to the next level.
  • Give each supplier (the company that makes or ships your products) its own project and documentation.
  • A team member working on one supplier's uploads will not know another supplier's workflow (the steps of the work).
  • Pair every launch step with a downloadable checklist and a ready-to-use template (a ready-made form to fill in).
  • A defined output format lets the operator follow the list instead of working out the method each time. This reduces delays.
  • Build an SOP for repeatable tasks that are not specific to your business, such as SEO (making pages easy to find in search engines), Instagram or Facebook work.
  • Share and store these SOPs so the team can reuse them. **
  • Write SOPs for every recurring task you hand over, so team members know exactly how to do the work.
  • Templates and SOPs can be reused, because most businesses repeat the same tasks.

Recording tools

1 min
  • Loom is a free Google Chrome extension (a small add-on for the Chrome browser). It records screen, microphone audio and webcam (camera) at the same time.
  • To make an SOP recording with Loom, record your product upload process (adding products to your store). Aim for at least 20 to 30 seconds. Then send it to a freelancer (a self-employed helper hired for one task). **
  • Loom records screen and camera quickly. Use it to document processes and train team members or contractors without long meetings.
  • The free version of Loom is enough. It records screen, webcam and microphone at the same time and gives a shareable link (a web address you can send to others).
  • Loom creates shareable links. Use it to send walkthroughs (step-by-step demonstrations) and explanations to team members or clients, instead of long written instructions.
  • Loom recordings are quick to make. You can record five to ten follow-up recordings in a row and send them in one session.
  • Batching (doing many similar tasks at once) keeps the time cost per lead (per potential customer) low.
  • A screen recording of you doing a booking yourself can be quicker to make and easier to follow than a written instruction.
  • SweetProcess is a paid tool for modular (built from separate parts), visual SOPs.
  • In SweetProcess, each step is numbered automatically. Steps can carry directions, images and recordings. The worker ticks off steps as a checklist.
  • A Google Doc (a free online document) can serve as a free alternative to SweetProcess.

SOP definition and principles

1 min
  • A standard operating procedure (SOP) is a written workflow (steps in order) or manual. It tells a person how to handle a specific situation.
  • The purpose of an SOP is to make sure tasks always run the same way and as efficiently as possible.
  • An SOP is a written step-by-step list for a recurring task.
  • Create SOPs for everyday tasks as the business grows. Then work can be handed to a team member without one-on-one training (teaching one person in person).
  • Written SOPs let any new person do the same job the same way.
  • This includes a long-term employee, a newly hired stranger or a first-time VA (virtual assistant, a remote helper).
  • A new person can read the SOP and handle situations without prior training from the owner.
  • Write SOPs simpler than seems necessary. What is obvious to you after many repetitions can confuse a new person.
  • Visual steps (pictures, screenshots of the screen) help people who read the language less fluently.
  • Break long processes into short chunks. A team member can finish one part in one sitting (one work session) and know exactly where to resume.
  • A process that must be finished in one go is harder to delegate.
  • Keep SOPs light on words and heavy on screenshots or recordings.
  • A five-minute task should not need a ten-page document. **
  • Writing an SOP for a task you have never done yourself leads to wrong time estimates and missing steps.
  • Someone else then fails at the task or takes far longer than expected.

Writing an SOP: the procedures

1 min

Steps to build an SOP:

  1. Confirm that the task makes money before you document it.
  2. Do the task yourself at least a couple of times, and time it. This shows how long it really takes and that the steps work.
  3. Start with the repetitive, time-consuming tasks that eat your hours.
  4. Write the steps at an entry-level, almost basic level, as if a fast-food employee had to follow them.
  5. Split complex tasks into small chunks. When a sub-task is complicated, link it to its own separate process.
  6. Add screenshots or short recordings to each step, especially for team members who are not native speakers.
  7. Test the SOP with a peer (someone at your level) or another entrepreneur who does not know your business. Fix every step they cannot follow.

Systemizing a repeatable task:

  1. Look for inefficiency (wasted time or effort), especially in tasks that repeat often.
  2. List what currently works for each task, and what wastes time or effort.
  3. Combine the best parts into one written procedure for the task. It can then be handed to others or automated.

Writing a simple SOP with Loom **:

  1. Perform the task once while recording it with Loom.
  2. Write a short bullet list of the steps, using few words.
  3. Place the recording next to each step, so it shows exactly how that step is done.
  4. Keep all SOPs in a shared Google Drive, so the team can access and edit them in real time (at the same moment).

Documenting a repeatable process before handing it over:

  1. Write down how the task is done and the rules that apply.
  2. Prefer a screen recording of you completing the task. For example, record from order collection in Shopify (an online store platform) through the supplier order and fulfilment (packing and shipping).
  3. Store the documentation so new hires can use it. It will also ease a future business handover to a buyer.

SOP format: text, recording and checklist (older advice, 2021-22, unless stated)

3 min
  • Recorded walkthroughs of the task on screen, with spoken narration (a voice explaining), are often the easiest SOP format.
  • The person hears and sees each step, so a recording is easy to follow.
  • Not every task suits a recording. Many operators provide both a recording and a text document for the same SOP, because people learn in different ways.
  • Text SOPs are easier to follow when screenshots of the relevant screen are added.
  • Screenshots let the person confirm they are in the right place.
  • Record the SOP as a screen share (showing your screen to others), with voice and screen. You do not need your face on camera.
  • Add screenshots or recordings to each step.
  • Structure every SOP in this order:
    1. Use text or recording as required.
    2. Always start by explaining the purpose of the task.
    3. Break the task into a step-by-step sequence.
    4. Finish the SOP with a section on what NOT to do (no-nos or reminders).
    5. Create a single-page checklist for the SOP.
  • The purpose section explains what the task helps the business achieve and how it supports the wider business.
  • Example: uploading products to a Shopify store widens the product range. That engages more customers and increases revenue (money coming in) and profit (money kept).
  • Knowing their role fosters a sense of inclusion, which improves engagement and the quality of work.
  • Break the task into as many specific numbered steps as possible (Step 1, Step 2 and so on).
  • In a recording, state each step and show it. In a text document, outline each step with its details.
  • In a text document, add screenshots where a particular page or screen needs to be recognised.
  • Example for a Shopify product upload SOP: Step 1 is to copy the product title into the title field. Step 2 is to paste the product description into the description field. Further steps cover every other product data field.
  • Each step is one concrete action, so the person can follow it without guessing.
  • A no-nos section lists what the person must not do, such as copying content from other sites.
  • Specifying prohibited actions is as important as the steps. It removes doubt about expectations.
  • It also clarifies whether a contractor should be kept if they repeat a forbidden action.
  • If someone repeats an action after being told not to, that is grounds for immediate dismissal (firing right away).
  • Example: a contractor writes unique product descriptions with research. Copied content from another site is a clear no-no.
  • Product descriptions must contain no duplicated content.
  • Every SOP must end with a checklist that fits on a single page.
  • The checklist is a quick reference the person ticks off after completing the task. The full SOP is used for learning, not for memory checks.
  • Checklists reduce errors in industries that use them. Aviation is the classic example: accidents fell after pilots began running through pre-flight checklists (lists of checks before a flight).
  • Checklists also reduce time spent fixing and checking errors.
  • Checklists also lower the need for later performance management (formal reviews of staff work).
  • The book The Checklist Manifesto (a short book of a few hundred pages, sold by major bookstores and Amazon) covers why checklists support business processes, safety and quality.
  • Keep every SOP simple by starting from one minimal template that already contains all required parts.
  • Those parts are: purpose, numbered steps, a no-nos section and a single-page checklist.
  • Adapt the template for each new task instead of designing a new layout each time.
  • Record screen recordings of recurring tasks without using the employee's name. The same recording can then train every future hire, including replacements.

Templates

2 min
  • SOP templates are Word documents (files for Microsoft Word, a word processor). They can be opened in Microsoft Word, Apple Pages (Apple's word processor) or Google Drive.
  • Templates are meant to be edited so they fit your own business.
  • SOP templates are a starting point, not a final document.
  • Adapt each template to your own products, suppliers and policies before your team uses it.
  • The set contains seven SOP templates:
    1. Customer Service SOP
    2. Order Management SOP
    3. Virtual Assistant Interview Template
    4. Hiring Questions
    5. Supplier Procedures SOP
    6. Website Style Guide SOP
    7. Shipping and Return Policy SOP
  • Procedure for adopting the SOP templates:
    1. Download all SOP templates.
    2. Open them on your computer and read through them.
    3. Look at the processes described and identify what you actually use.
    4. Modify each template so it fits your business better.
    5. Save all finished SOPs in your own Google Drive account.
    6. When you hire someone, share a Google Drive link with them instead of sending a file.
  • A user guide (a written instruction manual for the business) is the core document for outsourcing.
  • A user guide should describe each recurring process in enough detail that a new person can follow it without the owner present.
  • Pre-built templates for the user guide let you plug in your own business information.
  • Using them speeds up documenting processes before delegation. You do not write each document from scratch.
  • Write quotes and emails from fixed parts. Keep the same opening and closing sentences in every template.
  • Edit only the middle part for each customer. This keeps all messages consistent, even when different people send them **.
  • Create a quote template (a fixed price offer form) in Excel (Microsoft's spreadsheet program) or Google Sheets (Google's spreadsheet program).
  • Include your logo, company colours, the date, the company email, the customer name and contact details, and fields for item number and price.
  • Store the file in the PDF (a fixed-layout document file) or templates section, so every team member uses the same version **.
  • Staff then only replace the item and price fields instead of building the quote from scratch.
  • Build a pre-built template for each new product page (the page that sells one item) and reuse it.
  • Once one product page works, reuse the same template and checklist for the next product.

Storage and sharing

2 min
  • Store all SOPs in Google Drive, using separate folders per work area.
  • Example folders: Shopify product uploads with attachments, website design, content creation and posting.
  • Share a folder with a freelancer or VA to give them access. Updates reach them automatically, without resending files.
  • Dropbox (another online file storage service) works equally well.
  • Keep originals also on a hard drive or backup storage **.
  • Store your SOPs in your own Google Drive and share the link with each new team member.
  • Do not send downloaded files. The team then works from one central, consistent version.
  • A common mistake is sending SOP files directly to new team members. This creates several copies that drift apart over time.
  • Sharing one Google Drive link keeps every team member on the same current version.
  • Creating and storing SOPs:
    1. Write each SOP down or record it as a screen share.
    2. Store the SOP in Google Drive or on YouTube.
    3. Add a link to each SOP in the shared business spreadsheet, so staff can find it by clicking.
  • Storage location: Google Drive is the default. YouTube is the other option named for stored SOP recordings.
  • Dropbox is an equal alternative to Google Drive **.
  • Create a shared resource board (a central place for files) for guidelines, logos, photography and key information. Use SharePoint (Microsoft's file-sharing system) or Google Drive.
  • Set it up as a self-service library, so team members find materials without asking the creative team.
  • Share all strategy documents (brand, marketing, content, influencer and so on) in a central, accessible place.
  • Then any team member can check the company direction at any time.
  • Keep one master folder per product, with subfolders for thumbnails (small preview images), product videos, review screenshots and brand assets (logos, colours and similar files).
  • Use a consistent naming pattern. This makes it fast for a team member to rebuild a product page or ad without searching the whole computer.
  • Store important information in more than one place. For example, the price list appears in both the company information and the quotes section, because people search in different ways.
  • Attach documents like price lists directly to the relevant card in Trello (a board tool where tasks sit on cards).
  • Update every copy whenever a manufacturer sends a new price list. **

Data standards

1 min
  • Standardize controlled lists (fixed lists of approved options) in SOPs. Example: approved product tags.
  • Create an official set of tags that all team members must use, with no made-up variations.
  • The manager approves new additions only.
  • This keeps data consistent. It makes collections, filters and reports work across all products.
  • Create a live SOP file that team members can add comments and suggestions to.
  • If someone finds a faster method, they add it to the SOP and the manager approves.
  • SOPs then improve over time, and everyone invests in them.

Process repository and team questions

1 min

Answering recurring team questions through a process repository (a shared library of how-to guides):

  1. Whenever a team member asks how to do something, record the answer as a short screen recording (or write an article) instead of explaining it again.
  2. Save it in the shared repository and send the link to the team member.
  3. When a team member asks about a process that is not in the repository, check first whether it exists.
  4. If it does not exist, the team member records the process, adds it to the repository, and reports that the new entry is there.

Staff who handle customer service need the same knowledge base (shared store of answers). Written standard procedures for returns, support, uploads and ads reduce errors as the team grows. **

Customer service playbook

1 min

Building a playbook (a written guide of answers) from customer questions:

  1. Answer customer questions yourself for the first period of the business.
  2. Record every recurring question and its approved answer in one document.
  3. Turn that document into a playbook that a future hire or assistant follows when responding to customers.
  4. Update the playbook as new question types appear.

Company information board and communication scripts (older advice, 2021-22, unless stated)

1 min

Build a general company information board (one shared page of basic facts) for new team members, in this order:

  1. Company history.
  2. Company values.
  3. Shipping and return policies.
  4. How the business works with vendors (the suppliers who make or send your products).
  5. How customers should be addressed and what image the company projects.
  6. Examples of difficult customer situations with model answers (sample good replies).
  7. Working hours and schedule.
  8. Clear expectations for the role.

Create a standard operating procedure (SOP: a written step-by-step guide for one task) in a tab (one section of a shared file) that holds all repeatable communication:

  1. A phone greeting script (the exact words to say when answering a call).
  2. A chat opening and closing script.
  3. Email templates (ready-made email text) for common cases, such as requesting quotes from manufacturers.
  4. A list of questions to ask customers before quoting (giving a price).
  5. Follow-up templates for quotes (messages sent after a price is given).

Onboarding and training

1 min

  • Onboard new team members through a video library (a set of saved videos) that covers company goals, the vision and all core processes. Newcomers then reach working speed without the owner.

  • The library also makes it easier to sell the business later, since buyers expect documented processes.

  • Create a training video for a complex recurring task, such as product uploading:

    1. Record a narrated screencast (a screen recording with spoken explanation) of about 30 minutes. It should show exactly how to upload the product, including the tags (keywords attached to a product), description format, removal of files and linking (adding the links).
    2. Have the virtual assistant (VA: a remote helper who does tasks for you) watch the video at half speed.
    3. Have the assistant repeat the task for each product.

    This approach saves hundreds of hours over time.

  • Before a new hire starts, record a sample upload of about 5 to 10 products and send it to them, so they can see the correct process.

  • Give a VA or freelancer both a written SOP and a video walkthrough (a recorded run-through) of each task.

  • Record the video after you run through the task once.

  • The written SOP is the quick reference for later use, because it is faster to search than a long video. **

  • A virtual assistant can book shipping by following written step-by-step instructions for the booking process.

  • Assign the product page SEO checklist (SEO, or search engine optimization, means making pages easier for Google to find) to whoever creates or uploads product pages. Have them run through it during the product data upload.

  • Team members can also work through the checklist as a learning tool when onboarding someone new. **

Hiring and outsourcing

2 min

Hire a Business Process Outsourcing (BPO: handing a whole process to an outside specialist) freelancer to build documentation when the owner is overloaded. **:

  1. Post a job on Upwork (a website where freelancers are hired) for a BPO specialist, not a day-to-day virtual assistant.
  2. Choose a candidate whose Upwork profile shows experience writing SOPs.
  3. Have them do the tasks and document each process as they go, so the owner spends only a few hours a week with them.
  4. Once the documentation exists, bring on a customer service VA and hand over the documents.

A BPO freelancer costs more than a customer service VA. But the Philippines offers strong workers at good prices. **

  • Write SOPs before hiring a virtual assistant, so they know how to run the business.
  • Hire someone who has held jobs at other companies for long periods. Short tenures (how long someone stayed in each job) often signal instability. **
  • Write the SOP before posting a job advertisement, not after hiring someone.
  • Documenting the task first clarifies what to ask candidates in the application and interview stages. It also avoids pressure during onboarding. **
  • Create an SOP for every task you delegate, regardless of how small or simple it seems.
  • For large tasks, several SOPs covering individual components can be useful. But every outsourced task needs at least one. **
  • Adapt every SOP to the specific business before it is handed to a VA. Add the business's own preferred methods, storage locations for files and any other specifics. **
  • Update the SOP whenever the business changes.
  • Link building (getting other websites to link to yours) outsourcing SOPs should be written first for the Skyscraper method. This is a method of making a better version of popular content and asking sites to link to it. **
  • The Skyscraper method is the most time-consuming and most complicated link building approach. Its tasks are the priority for documentation.
  • Learn as you go and document each step. Then whoever runs the operation later can understand how every supplier works, without asking the owner. **
  • Build the business so that it can be handed over.
  • Create a video of every process from the start, and keep the team's SOPs current.

Systemizing and continuous improvement

1 min
  • Build repeatable systems for every recurring task. Systems maximize efficiency and produce consistent results.
  • A good system can be handed to an employee or virtual assistant with predictable output.
  • Use the same proven selection criteria (the rules you use to pick a product) every time you choose a product.
  • Use the same repeatable process every time you build traffic (visitors to your store), instead of starting from scratch each time.
  • This makes results predictable across stores.
  • Study competitors who already succeed in your market. Reverse-engineer their systems (take them apart to see how they work) to see what works.
  • Then replicate it and improve on it rather than guessing.
  • A system should work like a recipe: anyone who follows it exactly should reach the same result.
  • This lets you outsource or automate tasks without wondering whether they are being done correctly.
  • If you cannot describe a task as a clear step-by-step process, you do not understand it well enough to delegate or repeat it.
  • Document every routine as you go, including tasks like uploading a product or researching suppliers.
  • Look for inefficiencies (wasted steps) in repetitive tasks and improve them. A system should be the most direct route to the result.
  • Keep what works and build it into the process for your future employees.
  • Build systems as an ongoing habit, not a one-time to-do list. Keep refining them as the business changes.
  • Treating process improvement as part of your normal way of working makes it continuous.
  • Build an SOP for repeatable tasks that are not business specific. Then share and store them so the team can reuse them. **

Mistakes to avoid

1 min
  • Running a business with all processes stored in the owner's head makes it impossible to hand over work.
  • Write down processes and build a system before hiring anyone.
  • Do not wait for a major life event to force you to build systems.
  • Start documenting and building the operation while you still run it yourself.
  • Writing an SOP for a task you have never performed yourself leads to wrong time estimates and missing steps.
  • Sending SOP files directly to new team members creates copies that drift apart. Instead, use one shared Google Drive link (Google Drive is an online storage space for shared files).
  • Hiring for an unproven process means paying someone to learn something you have not yet proven works.
