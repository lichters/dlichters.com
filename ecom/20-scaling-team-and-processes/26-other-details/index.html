Section 20.26 · 26 of 26 in this chapter · 13 min read

In short

  • Keep checking your store. Helpers can quit, and rival shops change.
  • Test a product before you spend big. Look for real buying signs.
  • Keep one clear topic, and add new products often.
  • Profit takes time. It does not show in the first six months.

Owner role and team growth

2 min
  • ** Growing the team multiplies your positive impact. It improves team members' lives through meaningful work. It improves customer service. It creates wider community benefit.
  • ** Lasting growth comes from finding and cutting weaknesses again and again. One fix is not enough.
  • ** Assign one person on the team to watch the online spaces your customers use. These spaces are a steady source of pain points (problems customers complain about), desires and market research for your avatars (made-up profiles of your ideal buyers).
  • Running a store passively (with a helper doing the work) is not permanent.
    • The virtual assistant (a helper you hire to do tasks for you) may quit or stop answering.
    • Rival stores (competitors) may add bundles (several products sold together for one price) or redesign their sites.
    • So the store must keep being monitored and improved.
  • Case: a dropshipping store (a shop where a supplier ships each order straight to the buyer, so you hold no stock) started in 2014. It made its first sale within a few weeks, by Christmas.
    • It ran as a side business (a second income next to the main job) while the owner worked as a bookkeeper. It helped pay off about 70,000 in debt. That debt was built up with a husband from buying a house. It took just under two years.
    • After the owner quit the bookkeeping job, the store tripled its revenue (total sales money) in the first full-time year.
    • The owner still worked about 50 hours a week on customer service, orders and fires (urgent problems). Only in 2018, about four years after starting, did the owner step away from daily business operations.
    • The turning point was a pregnancy in 2017. The owner built the business system (the written steps and tools that run the business). The owner found a virtual assistant within about three months.
    • Training lasted two weeks. After that, customer service and order management (handling each order from start to finish) were handed over. The owner later could travel for months while the business ran. The owner worked on the business, not in it.
    • The owner later hired a second assistant and raised pay after one year.

Delegation and done-for-you services

1 min
  • Done-for-you services (tasks the team does for you) are listed at the bottom of the members area (your private member page). You can book them at any time.
    • Most carry an extra fee and are optional.
    • They suit people who want faster progress. They also suit people who dislike tasks such as website design, product uploads (adding products to the store) or market verification (checking that people want the product).
  • Done-for-you store building usually takes two to five business days, depending on the team's workload. Ask for the team's current turnaround (how long jobs take now) before you commit.

Working setup and tools

1 min
  • Use a screen split (two windows side by side) or an external monitor for supplier and product research. Suppliers are the companies that sell you the products. You will stop switching windows constantly, which saves time.
  • Do not open a normal chat or create a new Cowork task (a new work job in the Cowork app) without first selecting the project (the folder where your files and rules live). Work started outside the project does not get the project's files, instructions or memory.
  • Downloaded course files belong in one dedicated folder inside the project location. You can then drag them into a chat to install skills (extra instructions an AI can use) or to reference them.

Business identity and accounts

1 min
  • Use the exact same email address for all Google products. This includes:
    • Google Merchant Center (Google's tool that lists your products for shopping results)
    • Google Analytics (Google's tool that counts your visitors)
    • Google Ads (Google's paid ad system)
    • and the other Google products. This lets Google link them and confirm domain ownership (proof that you own your web address) automatically.
  • Set the same time zone (the clock region your data uses) across all Google products, so data shows consistently everywhere.
  • ** Use an existing store's customer support email, at the store's own domain (the web address after the @ sign), for business email. Avoid email from another business domain.

Store build and technical checks

1 min
  • ** When doing image optimization (making pictures small and fast to load), start with the highest-priority pages and products you want to rank most (appear high in search results). Then work through lower-priority pages. This staged approach makes the task manageable without needing to optimize everything at once.
  • ** Clicking every link by hand is too slow on pages with dozens of outgoing links (links that lead to other sites). Use a link checker extension (a browser add-on that tests links for you) or outsource the scan (pay someone else to do it).
  • Build single-product stores (shops that sell just one product). Add relatable products at the bottom of the page. Use upsells (an extra offer to buy more) in the cart (your shopping basket) to grow average order value (the average amount each buyer spends per order).

Offers and average order value

1 min
  • Quantity discounts (buy one, buy two, buy three) can do better than a complex bundle for a first test.
    • They are simple to set up.
    • They lift average order value without needing to pick related products.
  • ** Consistency drives upsell results. Keep offering the upsells across all three channels (the ways you reach buyers) every time. Do not try once and stop.
  • ** Upsell action items:
    1. Decide which on-site upsell positions to use: product page (the page for one product), cart page, or post-purchase (the offer after the buyer has paid).
    2. If no post-purchase upsell exists yet, add one. Keep existing product-page upsells. Add later upsells in the sequence (the planned order of steps).
    3. Add an upsell email sequence (a planned series of emails with an extra offer) for each core product from the planner list into your email system (the tool that sends your emails).
    4. Customize the email templates (ready-made email texts) with your specific products.
    5. Pass the script (the words to say) and instructions to the customer service team. Discuss the planner list with them, so they offer the related upsell when a customer calls about a core product.

Product research and validation

1 min
  • Find someone already getting the results you want. Then model their process (copy a method that already works) instead of reinventing it. A proven model can squeeze years of trial and error (trying things and learning from mistakes) into a short period.
    • Test and improve the model only after it makes money.
  • Product review workflow used by the team:
    1. Watch the product's TikTok clip and judge whether it is good.
    2. Read the comments for buying intent (signs a buyer is ready to buy).
    3. Check the seller's account for upload dates and posting consistency (posting on a steady schedule).
    4. Open the store on mobile and desktop (phone and computer). Review its structure, product page and payment options.
    5. Estimate cost and selling price.
    6. Score the product and decide whether to test it.
  • Spending more does not beat a competitor's higher budget if your targeting is sharper (your ads reach exactly the right buyers).
    • ** A focused store with a few thousand dollars a month can outperform a competitor spending tens of thousands by cutting weak products.
  • Roughly 80% of sales often come from about 20% of products. Focus effort on finding and adding the few suppliers whose products drive most of the revenue.
  • Validation (testing if a product can sell) and profit timing:
    • A store that passes all validation tests can usually become profitable (make more money than it spends).
    • How large it grows (hundreds of thousands, millions or more in annual sales) is learned over the first year or two.
    • Profit does not appear in the first six months.

Competitor signals

1 min
  • When a competitor (a rival store) switches off many active ads (ads still running), this can signal a stock problem (not enough products to sell) around the holiday. It may not mean the product failed.
    • Example: 22 to 30 ads switched off after about three weeks of running.
    • If the product meets your criteria, the wait for stock may be worth it.

Product range and rebranding

1 min
  • Launch a new product regularly to give existing customers a reason to buy again.
    • The target was one new product per month. One per quarter (every three months) may be enough in some niches (narrow topic markets).
  • Keep a stable store with the same products over time. A single winner (a product that sells well) can keep a business running. The goal is to keep the store alive by upgrading the best product, not chasing one-off hits.
  • Rebrand (change the name and look of) a store with a mixed product line (many kinds of products):
    1. Remove the products that do not fit the target niche.
    2. Change the domain name (web address) and logo (brand picture) in Shopify (the software the store runs on).
    3. Keep the remaining products and rename the store to match the niche (for example a carport store).
    4. Keep the removed products only for a future second store, if there are enough suppliers to justify it.

Sub-niches and multi-niche stores

1 min
  • ** Use the same workflow for multiple sub-niches (smaller topics inside one niche). Build keyword silos (groups of pages built around one set of search words) for each sub-niche in one store, not separate stores.
    • One store is easier to rank with one keyword silo.

Launch sequence and growth

1 min
  • ** Start paid traffic (visitors you pay for with ads) and suppliers right away, instead of waiting to build content first. Make money from paid traffic. Then reinvest that money into content and SEO (search engine optimization, getting found on Google through pages and articles).
  • ** A store can grow to seven-figure revenue (a million or more in sales) if it stays focused on one niche. It can become a leading store there and keep scaling ad budgets (raising the money spent on ads).
    • The main limit is how far the owner pushes, not the product.
  • ** A customer multiplier funnel (the steps that bring buyers back for more) consists of about six to seven main actions plus some smaller supporting actions.
    • The main actions work best when they all run together, one after another. They move customers toward a second purchase or toward new customers.
  • ** Follow-up is keeping in touch with prospects (people who visited but did not buy) and with customers who already bought. The goal is to move them toward a second or third purchase.
    • Its value builds over time as traffic sources (places that send visitors) bring in many top- and middle-of-funnel visitors (people early on the path to buying, not yet ready).
  • ** High-ticket stores (selling expensive products) need far fewer orders than low-ticket stores (selling cheap products) for the same profit.
    • Five saunas a day can be fulfilled (shipped and completed) by one owner in a few hours.
    • Equal profit from 40 USD items needs hundreds of orders and a team.

Copy, direct mail and product arguments

1 min
  • Older advice... no year given: When one sales argument (the reason a buyer should buy) works for one buyer group, apply it to every group with the same need. For example, use it for all gas companies within several hundred miles, then for all stove makers within 500 miles.
    • Expanding a proven argument to new segments (new groups of buyers) is faster than inventing a new one.
  • Test changes to the product itself, such as the screening of coal (sifting out the small bits), the cooking time of coke (fuel made from coal), or the size of oven charges (the loads put into an oven). Do this to stay ahead of competitors.
    • Improving the product keeps the argument for it strong over time.
  • Study proven direct mail (printed letters sent by post) and letter-writing references. Apply their principles to your own mailings on a regular basis.
    • A systematic study routine (a planned, step-by-step habit) produces better results than relying on instinct (gut feeling) alone.

Store updates and course materials

1 min
  • A major update to the outsourcing and automation part (handing work to others, and letting software run tasks) was in preparation. It replaces all existing trainings with new strategies and tools.
    • Existing material still works.
    • Users who want the newest tools should wait about one week before going through that part.
  • Store updates in the training are published in one place. The member login (your sign-in for the member area) always shows the latest version.
    • Announce significant updates in the team announcement channel (the team's news chat), with a link to the changed part.

Owner income and reinvestment

1 min
  • An online business can scale far beyond a salary (fixed pay from an employer), because revenue is not tied to hours worked. It can also run from any location.
    • This makes it a stronger base for reinvesting income (putting earnings back into other assets) than a fixed-salary job.
  • A large share of business income can be reinvested. The source cites 80% of earnings invested from 2013 until COVID (the 2020 pandemic).
    • The reinvestment should be spread across diversified assets (many kinds of investments). Examples are index funds (funds that copy a whole market) and stable stocks (shares in steady companies). Only a small part should go into speculative assets (risky investments).

Income beyond the own stores

1 min
  • Monthly income for a freelancer (a person who sells skills to several clients) who serves multiple dropshipping stores = monthly fee per client × number of clients. Example: 150 dollars × 10 clients = 1,500 dollars per month.
  • ** Skills gained from running your own store, such as paid traffic management (running and tuning ads that bring visitors), can be sold as a service. Other store owners will pay for them.
    • Early client work of this kind can grow into a separate income stream (a separate source of money) alongside the own stores.
  • ** When the same questions and problems keep coming from clients, package the answers into a structured course or program (a planned teaching series). Do this instead of repeating one-on-one advice.
    • Courses can deliver the same knowledge to more people in less time. One-on-one coaching (private help, one person at a time) can continue alongside.

Personal environment and idea generation

1 min
  • Being in a different environment or culture, even within your own country, shifts how your brain works and generates new ideas. Visit other places. Meet people who do things differently. Bring back what you learn.

Case studies: store design and launch

2 min
  • Case: a jewelry store was the sixth product tested, after several earlier products without a winner. It launched when buyers wanted to show identity (who they are).
    • Ads pointed to a collection page (a page showing several related items) with about 20 designs of an African map necklace. It was priced at 30 with a buy-one-get-second-free offer, so two cost 30 instead of 60.
    • A later collection of African country necklaces let buyers choose their country free with the map necklace.
    • The store made about 60,000 in revenue within two months.
    • The store was closed after two months because the operator lacked ads experience. Money went into unprofitable ads (ads that lose money). The daily budget (the most you spend per day on an ad) was raised from 5 to 100 dollars in one step. This got the ad account blocked (shut off by the ad platform). Negative comments were left unanswered, which led to the Facebook page being blocked.
  • Case: a student's store sold a mix of unrelated products (items that do not fit together). These included hair wax, pet grooming gloves, men's jewelry, pet accessories, hats, car accessories and a bathroom apron. Its traffic (visitors) came from a Facebook ad for a baby hammock sleeping bed.
    • The homepage (the front page) showed unrelated lifestyle photos (everyday-life pictures). The product pages used default featured images (the standard main pictures). They had no reviews, no urgency (no reason to buy now), no estimated delivery dates, no live chat link (a button to chat with the store) and no social media accounts.
    • The reviewer judged the store to look like a burner store (a shop built to sell fast, then be dropped).
    • Recommended changes:
      • Choose one product type, then theme the site around it (make the whole design match it).
      • Replace the images.
      • Add a review system (a place for customer ratings).
      • Add scarcity counts (numbers showing how few are left).
      • Add urgency offers (deals that end soon).
      • Add estimated delivery dates.
      • Add a live chat link.
      • Use tabbed product pages (information split into clickable tabs).
      • Add a fuller footer (more links at the bottom of the page).
      • Activate social media accounts.
    • The source gives no sales figures or results for the store.
