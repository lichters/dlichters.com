Operating rhythm: tracking, finances and change logs

Section 20.24 · 24 of 26 in this chapter · 20 min read

In short

  • Check the store every day: orders going out, email box empty, missed calls returned.
  • Check ad spending every week: stop what loses money and add money to what earns.
  • Write down every change with its date, its goal number, and the starting number.
  • Keep the books simple at first. Move to a full accounting setup once sales reach 10,000 to 20,000 a month.
  • Put early profit back into the business instead of taking it out.
More key points
  • Pay for a new cost only after revenue (money from sales) grows enough to cover it.
  • Set a revenue level for each hire or big expense, and wait until you reach it.
  • The 35% and 30% staff figures are only comparison points. Set your own share from your own payroll (total wages paid).
  • Check your numbers every month: revenue outlook, past changes, and cash (money you have right now).

Daily and weekly checks for a live store

1 min

A live store is a store that is open and taking real orders.

Daily and weekly routines

1 min

Older advice from 2020:

Daily checks for a live store:

  1. Confirm that all orders are going out.
  2. Keep the inbox at zero, meaning no unanswered messages in your email box.
  3. Return every missed phone call.

Weekly checks for a live store:

  1. Audit every ad account to confirm that spending is profitable. An ad account is where you pay for ads. Profitable means earning more than you spend.
  2. Optimize any campaign that no longer makes money. Optimize means adjust to improve. A campaign is one ad run with its own budget and goal.
  3. Put more budget behind products that are earning well. Budget means the money set aside for ads.

Older advice from 2021: daily work after launch should include the following. Launch means the day the store goes live.

  • Contact new brands. Brands here means the companies that supply your products.
  • Follow up with brands you already contacted.
  • Upload and optimize products, not just run ads.

Weekly supplier outreach

1 min

Supplier outreach means contacting suppliers to ask them to work with you. A supplier is a company that supplies your products.

  • Schedule supplier outreach on a fixed weekday, ideally Monday to Friday, because many suppliers are closed on weekends.
  • If Monday does not suit you, pick any weekday and block it in your calendar. Block means reserve that time.
  • Set a recurring weekly calendar reminder for supplier outreach. Recurring means it repeats every week.
  • Plan for the next one to two months, then keep the routine going as long as you are still building your supplier list.
  • Allow 30 minutes to 2 hours per week for supplier outreach.
  • Do it as one dedicated block on a single day. Dedicated means set aside only for this.
  • Consistency matters more than the length of any single session.

Daily reporting on new product tests

1 min

A product test means trying a new product to see if it sells. A report is a short update sent to the support team. Creatives means the ad videos and images.

Daily reporting sequence for a new product test:

  1. After the store or website is built, send the first report within 24 hours.
  2. After the product videos (creatives) are found, send the report within 24 hours.
  3. After the campaign is launched, report at the 24-hour mark for Day 1.
  4. Report within 24 hours after Day 2.
  5. Report within 24 hours after Day 3.
  6. Continue reporting every 24 hours after that until told otherwise.
  • Missing a daily report costs money.
  • It also takes away the support team's control over the test.
  • So submit all required reports before moving on to the next step.
  • Do not proceed to a later phase while earlier reports are outstanding, meaning not yet sent.

Tracking setup

1 min

Tracking means recording your numbers.

  • Inventory tracking, which means checking stock on hand, is commonly disabled even for live stores. Checking stock across all products constantly is impractical, meaning too hard to do.
  • Enable it only if you maintain an active inventory management system.
  • An automation is a set of steps that runs by itself, with no person needed.
  • The automation writes the recovery date when a paid order comes in.
  • It captures sales made when nobody on the team could call, such as at night or during time off.
  • This keeps the sheet accurate without someone updating it manually.
  • Set up the sheet and automations early, even when one person handles everything.
  • Do this so the data is ready for month-over-month improvement, meaning comparing each month with the month before.
  • Tracking is worth doing from the start rather than after the team grows.

Planning that feeds daily work

1 min
  • Older advice from 2021-22: turn each priority (your most important goal) into the full list of tasks needed to reach it within six months.
  • Mark recurring tasks, meaning tasks that repeat, as weekly so they are entered once.
  • These task lists then feed the daily to-do list.
  • The 90-day rocks, meaning the main goals for each 90 days, are built from these task lists.
  • Older advice from 2021-22: the priority task lists in the planner feed the daily to-do lists for the owner and the team.
  • This means one plan keeps everyone on the same top priority each day.

Changelogs

1 min

A changelog is a running, dated list of changes.

Why every change is logged

1 min
  • Keep a running changelog of every change made to the store and its marketing.
  • Over months, you forget what was tweaked and why sales moved.
  • Without this record, teams repeat failed tests. They also cannot tell which change caused a rise or drop in sales.
  • Review the changelog regularly, so past experiments stay available when a similar idea comes up again.
  • Record every platform change in a log. A platform is where you run the change, such as Meta, Google or Shopify.
  • Each entry needs the date, the platform, the exact change, the expected result, and a baseline measured before the change. A baseline is the starting number you measure before the change, to compare against later.
  • Fill in the result and its date later, so each change can be judged on its own.
  • Compare results against a 90-day trailing baseline. Trailing means the average of the last 90 days.
  • Do not judge by a single day. One day of data is too noisy, meaning too random, to show whether a change worked.
  • Write the baseline into the log next to the change, so the comparison is fixed in advance.
  • A change logged without a baseline or a numeric expected result cannot be evaluated later.
  • Always define the target metric, meaning the number you aim to improve, and its current value before making the change.
  • Keep a dedicated record of every ad, supplier and store change, so you can explain what moved the key numbers.
  • Keep a file of every letter, variant and result. A variant is a different version of the same ad or page.
  • The record is what lets you repeat winners and avoid losers.
  • A seller who loses the file must rebuild its knowledge from scratch.

Setting up the changelog spreadsheet

1 min

Procedure:

  1. Open the master Google Docs changelog template from the provided link. It is read-only, meaning you can look but not edit. To get an editable copy, click File, then Make a copy. This puts the copy in your own Google Drive, which is your online file storage.
  2. Alternatively, click File and Download to save it as Excel or CSV. CSV is a plain table file. You can open it in any spreadsheet software.
  3. For each change, fill in the columns:
    • Date: the date the change was implemented.
    • Platform: where the change was made, for example Google Ads or Shopify Email.
    • Change: what was changed.
    • Expected Results: a hypothesis, meaning a testable guess, of the outcome, stated as a number where possible.
    • Additional Notes: anything you will need to remember later, such as starting budget or baseline.
  4. When the experiment ends, enter the Result Date (the end date of the test) and the Results (what actually happened). An experiment is a test.
  5. Compare the result with the expected result. Then decide whether to stop the change, or adjust it and log the adjusted version as a new experiment row. A row is one line in the sheet.

Changelog template column layout (Google Docs / spreadsheet):

Column Name Content
A Date Date the change was implemented
B Platform Platform where the change was made (e.g. Google Ads, Shopify Email)
C Change Description of the change
D Expected Results Hypothesis of the outcome, e.g. a target ROAS or open-rate increase
E Additional Notes Anything relevant to remember later, e.g. starting budget, baseline figure
F Result Date End date of the experiment (e.g. two weeks after the start date)
G Results What actually happened, entered as a number

Log structure and example entries

2 min

Some terms used in the log below:

  • ROAS means return on ad spend: money earned back for each dollar spent on ads. 10x ROAS means $10 back for every $1 spent.
  • CTR means click-through rate: the share of people who see an ad and click it.
  • Open rate means the share of emails that people open.
  • Remarketing means showing ads to people who already visited your store.
  • Advantage+ Shopping is an automated shopping campaign type on Meta (Facebook and Instagram).
  • Demand-Gen is a Google campaign type.
  • An opt-in bribe is a free gift given in return for someone's email address.
  • Shopify is the software your store runs on. Shopify Email is its email tool.

  • Additional note: starting budget of $10 per day.
  • Result Date: two weeks after the start.
  • Actual result: 5X ROAS, below the expected 10X. So the test was iterated, meaning changed and run again, instead of kept as it was.

  • The test was logged again as a new experiment, with the expected result of over 10X ROAS.
  • The note in the new row referred to the 5X result of the original test. This keeps the link between the two experiments visible.

Deciding after a test and before repeating an idea

1 min
  • When a test fails to reach its expected ROAS, you have two options.
  • Option one: turn the campaign off.
  • Option two: change its structure (how it is set up), its targeting (who sees it), or its ads. Then run it again as a new logged experiment.
  • Either way, record the decision, so the same idea is not retested without knowing the earlier outcome.

Use the changelog in team meetings before a new idea is accepted:

  1. When someone proposes a change, search the log for earlier tests of the same element or platform.
  2. If a matching test exists, read its Expected Results, Result Date and Results.
  3. Only propose the idea again if the earlier outcome is recorded as a different setup or was never run. Then log the new attempt.

CRO test boards

1 min

CRO means conversion rate optimization: changing your store so more visitors buy. A test board is a list of tests kept in one place.

  • A Trello board (an online app that uses boards of cards) can track every CRO test in one place.
  • Its columns are: future tests, current tests, completed tests, insights and notes.
  • Trello is easy to share with virtual assistants (remote helpers) and team members, who often already know it.
  • The tool does not decide success. A spreadsheet with the same columns works equally well.
  • Another project tool such as Asana or Basecamp (other online project-management apps) also works equally well.
  • Pick the tool that keeps all tracking in one place and is quick to use.

Structure of a CRO test tracking board:

Column Purpose
Future Tests Test ideas planned but not started
Current Tests Tests running now
Completed Tests Finished tests, with sub-lists Winning Tests and Failed or Inconclusive Tests
Insights Ideas for further tests drawn from results
Notes Loose ideas and reminders not related to a current test

Contents of each test card:

Field Content
Description What is being tested and the exact change
Duration How long the test will run
Notes Specific data points or other points worth recording
Attachments Exports from Google Analytics, Google Ads or Shopify with data linked to the test

Test card workflow:

  1. Create a card for each planned test in Future Tests, with its description, duration and attachments.
  2. When the test starts, move the card to Current Tests.
  3. Set a calendar reminder for the end date. Review running tests in a weekly to-do session.
  4. When the test ends, move the card to Completed Tests. Update it with results, comments and any extra attachments.
  5. Decide whether the test is winning, failed, or inconclusive (no clear answer).
  6. Copy the card into the matching winning or failed sub-list.
  7. If the test produced insights, add a card to Insights describing them. These feed future test ideas.

P&L and bookkeeping

1 min

A P&L (profit and loss report) shows money in, money out, and the profit left over. Bookkeeping means recording every money movement.

Starting the P&L with Claude Code

1 min

Claude Code is an AI tool that works on the files in a folder on your computer. A project here means that folder.

Procedure:

  1. Open the Claude Code project and type "execute this project".
  2. Answer the onboarding questions (the first setup questions) in one message. Include:
    • Brand name.
    • Accounting software: Xero, QuickBooks, MYOB or spreadsheets. Accounting software is a program that records money.
    • A monthly revenue ballpark.
    • Your primary priority, for example margin visibility (seeing your profit as a share of sales), ad ROI (return on ad spend), or cash forecasting (predicting future money in the bank).
  3. Review and approve the generated files CFO_DECISION_LOG.md, MEMORY.md and DATA_INVENTORY.md.
  4. Drag your Xero P&L export into the folder. An export is a file downloaded from the software.
  5. Ask iterative questions, meaning repeated follow-up questions, to improve formatting or analysis.
  • Claude Code is preferable to Claude Cowork (another Claude tool) for initial P&L projects.
  • Pass the Xero transaction export directly to Claude Code. A transaction export is a list of every money movement.
  • Pass the classification prompt directly to Claude Code. A classification prompt is the instruction that sorts each transaction.
  • Claude Code handles larger datasets better and keeps better file organization.

Operating expenses in the P&L

1 min

Operating expenses are the costs of running the business.

  • Enter operating expenses on a 30-day (monthly) basis.
  • Then multiply by three to create a 90-day total. This matches the 90-day revenue figures.
  • Include only fixed operating expenses. Fixed means costs that stay the same regardless of revenue level.
  • Examples: employee salaries, rent, warehouse costs, and subscriptions to Shopify (store software), Klaviyo (email software), Gorgias (customer-support software), and other software.
  • Exclude variable expenses. Variable means they change with sales volume.
  • Example monthly operating expenses: $25,000.
  • $25,000 × 3 equals $75,000 for a 90-day period.

Scaling and fixed costs

1 min

Scaling means growing your sales.

  • Scaling example: selling 50 products per month at 1,000 USD each gives 50,000 USD in revenue. Revenue means money from sales.
  • Fixed costs stay roughly the same at about 30 USD (Shopify plus domain). A domain is your web address.
  • Fixed costs therefore barely increase as volume grows.

When to upgrade bookkeeping

1 min
  • Older advice from 2021-22: start with the main goal of making sales and more sales.
  • Do not perfect your bookkeeping before the business has revenue to account for.
  • Only move to proper accounting once questions about cash flow (money moving in and out) or money in the bank become important.
  • Also move once you can afford to pay someone, or have the time to set it up yourself.
  • A practical threshold for upgrading from DIY bookkeeping (doing it yourself) to professional accounting is roughly 10,000 to 20,000 in monthly revenue.
  • The figure is in the source's currency. The German version of the course states it in euros.
  • Above that level, the business is considered real and should be run more seriously.
  • Scaling a dropshipping business (selling products that suppliers ship to customers, without holding stock) means taking on bigger problems.
  • These problems need better tools and more advanced help than the starter setup provides.
  • The starter tools are not wrong. They are simply not suited to a business that is just getting started.
  • A spreadsheet (a table file such as Excel or Google Sheets) is recommended while the store has low order volume.
  • When revenue grows, and you have a bookkeeper or accountant, and the extra cost is justified, switch to a paid accounting tool.
  • The author names Xero.com (online accounting software) as the tool used once the business had an accountant.

Decide when to upgrade from a basic bookkeeping setup to proper accounting:

  1. Upgrade once questions about where the money goes, or why the bank balance does not match the P&L, keep coming up.
  2. Upgrade when cash flow becomes important to you.
  3. Upgrade when you have the money to hire someone for bookkeeping, or the time to set it up yourself.
  4. If you are unsure, upgrade when monthly revenue reaches 10,000 to 20,000.
  5. Upgrade when the business moves into a growth phase with full financial systems.

Until one of these applies, put the focus on getting sales.

Who does the books

2 min

"The books" means your money records.

  • When a store grows, a fractional CFO (a part-time chief financial officer, the top money manager) can help.
  • A better accountant can help too. Both can provide a finance plan and budget.
  • They can also advise on financial issues that do not matter at the starter stage.
  • Hiring a cheap bookkeeper from freelance sites is risky. Examples are Upwork, Fiverr (websites where freelancers offer their services) or general online job boards.
  • Poor work is often not visible for many months.
  • A business owner without accounting skills usually cannot inspect the work.
  • A clean-up by experienced professionals later costs far more than good work would have.
  • When hiring an external bookkeeper, choose someone with proven experience with e-commerce businesses (online stores).
  • Ideally, choose someone who has worked with dropshippers, rather than an unknown freelancer.
  • Specialists in e-commerce accounting exist.
  • A business owner who has run an e-commerce store themselves may offer this service.
  • Professional e-commerce bookkeeping typically costs at least a few hundred dollars per month.
  • That is the minimum a reputable bookkeeping company charges for this type of work.

Budget-conscious alternative (the source presents this as far better than hiring an unknown cheap freelancer):

  1. Hire a virtual assistant who already has some bookkeeping experience.
  2. Give the assistant access to a DIY e-commerce bookkeeping course that the business owner has purchased or has free access to.
  3. Have the assistant learn the bookkeeping process from the course and apply it to the business.
  • If the owner dislikes the books or lacks the time for them, a done-for-you e-commerce bookkeeping service (a company that does the books for you) is a reasonable option.
  • Use this option once the business has grown.
  • Choose a provider that specializes in e-commerce and can show it works with many dropshippers.
  • Bookkeeping is needed for the owner to understand performance and solve problems.
  • The main goal of the books is clarity about what is happening in the business. It is not a formal record for its own sake.
  • Outsourcing bookkeeping to a trained virtual assistant is allowed and encouraged when the owner purchases a course.
  • This is allowed as long as the course is used in the business for the assistant's training.
  • Once manual tracking (recording by hand) is established, you can delegate order profit entry to a virtual assistant.
  • Full automation with Zapier-style automations (tools that connect apps and run steps by themselves) is possible.
  • Full automation is an advanced setup. If you are not comfortable with such tools, hire someone to build it.

Financial review cadence

1 min
  • Review the financial reports every month.
  • The monthly picture allows decisions before problems grow bigger over time.
  • Review the reports alongside the cash position (how much money is in the bank), not only the sales figures.
  • An outside bookkeeper or advisor who knows ecommerce can turn the reports into clear actions.
  • If an advisor is not affordable, a self-taught bookkeeping course can also provide the same reports.
  • Financial analysis is built in layers.
  • First, understand the profit and loss, the balance sheet (a list of what you own and owe), and cash flow.
  • Then address cash flow, supplier and profit problems in turn.
  • Each analysis answers some questions and raises new ones.

Profit allocation

1 min

Profit allocation means deciding where your profit goes.

Reinvest before taking profit

1 min
  • Early profits from a drop shipping store should be reinvested, meaning put back into the business, not taken out.
  • Reinvest so you can work through the full cycle. The cycle is: finding a winner (a product that sells well), testing, scaling, and creating unique content.
  • The cycle can end with moving toward a private label (your own brand made by a manufacturer) or bulk-buying brand (buying stock in large amounts from a brand).
  • Take profit out only once you have a second store running.
  • Also take profit out only once you have enough experience to do so without taking a personal financial hit.

90-10 rule for budget and time

1 min
  • Follow the 90-10 rule monthly.
  • Allocate 90% of time and budget to proven, repeatable methods that drive revenue.
  • Allocate 10% to experimental channels or strategies. A channel is a place where you sell or advertise.
  • If an experiment works, move it to the proven 90%.
  • Then use the freed 10% for new tests.

Paying yourself

1 min

Procedure:

  1. Calculate your personal monthly expenses.
  2. Pay yourself a fixed salary from the business account, for example 500 dollars per week, once the business is running.
  3. At the end of each month or quarter (every three months), check the net profit left in the business. Net profit is what remains after all costs.
  4. Decide a distribution, meaning money taken out of the business, based on goals and the profit available. For example, distribute 150,000 dollars at the end of a quarter when net profit passes 200,000 dollars.
  5. Keep some profit in the business. Avoid drawing the entire balance.
  • Set distributions from goals, not from what is left in the account.
  • Tie a quarterly or monthly distribution to a target net profit, so you do not overdraw the business. Overdraw means take out more than you have.

Planning sheet

1 min
  • A two-tab spreadsheet works for planning. A tab is a sheet inside the file.
  • One tab holds the Profit First allocations from revenue. Profit First is a money method that splits income into set parts.
  • The other tab holds the strategic planning. It covers the current team, staff pay, remaining budget, and max and target capacity (how much work or sales you can handle).
  • The source used a spreadsheet for this. The owner should update it with real numbers.

Hiring thresholds tied to revenue

1 min
  • A new expense can be paid for in one of two ways: raising revenue (money that comes in from sales), or raising that expense's allocation percentage (the share of revenue set aside for it). This forces a check: is the expense, such as a software tool or a hire, really needed?
  • Tie each hire or large expense to a revenue threshold (a set amount of sales money). Growth then pays for the cost before you commit to it. For example, add a new staff member only once real revenue reaches a set level that covers their cost.
  • Staff share benchmarks (comparison figures for how much of revenue goes to staff) differ by source:
    • About 35% of real revenue: observed in one established dropshipping company (a business that sells products without holding stock; the supplier ships them) that used this method.
    • 30%: the figure used in the example here.
    • Use 35% or 30% only as a reference point. The right staff percentage depends on the business's actual payroll. Set it from its own numbers.

Reviewing metrics

1 min

Metrics are the key numbers you track.

  • Every e-commerce store (an online shop) should have someone who asks each month what revenue can be reached next. Compare the latest month with the upcoming quarter (three months). This way, growth opportunities are not missed.
  • Review the changelog (the written list of past changes) regularly. This keeps past experiments available for comparison.
  • Compare each change against its logged baseline (the starting reference point you recorded before the change) and its expected result. Use the 90-day trailing figure (the total of the last 90 days, updated as time moves on).
  • Review the monthly financial reports (summaries of money in and out) alongside your cash position (how much money is available right now).
