Playbook › 20. Scaling, team and processes

Seasonal peaks, cash flow and capacity planning

Section 20.20 · 20 of 26 in this chapter · 9 min read

In short

  • Your first busy season (Q4) is for learning. Test small and build skills before you spend big.
  • Start Black Friday planning 2-3 weeks before the event.
  • Before you grow ad spending, make sure you have enough cash, stock and customer service help.
  • Raise ad budgets only when results stay steady, and order more stock in the same proportion.

Decide how hard to push in your first peak

1 min
  • Treat your first Q4 (October to December, the last three months of the year and the busiest shopping season) as a learning season.
  • Test and build experience rather than scaling aggressively. Going all in during a first peak often leads to poor returns (little money earned back from what you spent), cash shortages and disappointment.
  • Experience one Q4 before scaling into it.
  • A short-term seasonal product builds skills for a long-term brand.
  • The skills you gain include testing creatives (trying different ads and images), judging what a good ad looks like, and knowing when to add customer service staff or a video editor.
  • Its early cash flow (money moving in and out of your business) can fund the first steps of that brand.
  • A seasonal store is not the best first store.
  • Adjust daily budgets (the amount you spend on ads each day) by season instead of spending heavily out of season.
  • Let budget changes follow the season rather than a fixed schedule.
  • Use slow periods to build assets (reusable store parts such as pages and images) and creatives while saving cash rather than spending on campaigns.
  • Plan the year's key sales events and metrics (the numbers you track, such as sales and cost) ahead of time.
  • Start spending on ads only after the product makes sales.
  • Once profit comes in, reinvest part of it into ads while organic traffic (free visitors who find you without paid ads) keeps flowing.
  • This cushions losses on ads, so they hurt less.

Plan the calendar and launch timeline

1 min
  • Find the product before Q4.
  • It can then be scaled (grown in volume) through the peak season, including Black Friday (the shopping day after US Thanksgiving).
  • Start Black Friday campaign planning and setup 2-3 weeks before the event.
  • One month ahead is more comfortable; two weeks is tight but doable.
  • Delaying beyond this reduces time for testing and email sequence delivery (a set of automatic emails sent in order).
  • Start planning for Black Friday and Q4 early.
  • Allocate a large amount of time to preparation.
  • Creatives, offers, email flows (automatic email series), customer service and stock all need setting up beforehand.
  • Plan launches and email messaging before the peaks rather than expecting them to happen automatically.
  • Winners (products that sell well) can be scaled by adding more products and building a stronger brand over time.
  • High-revenue periods such as Black Friday and Cyber Monday (the online shopping day after Black Friday) are where large single-day numbers are typically earned.
  • Preparing for Black Friday is still possible in October if the store, suppliers and ads are in place. **
  • Expect a holiday lift (a rise in sales) in most niches (narrow product markets) in November and December. **
  • Raise ad budgets before the holiday season, because prices rise as competitors increase spending in the last weeks. **
  • Retargeting (showing ads again to people who already visited your store) early visitors is cheap and helps capture sales later. **
  • Plan holiday traffic early: send as many people to the site as possible before prices rise. **
  • Then remarket (show ads again) to them cheaply during the peak days. **

Q4 launch timeline procedure

1 min
  1. Choose the launch date before contacting suppliers, and keep it fixed in your planning.
  2. Set Q4 promotions with five offers: Halloween, Black Friday with Thanksgiving, Cyber Monday, Christmas, and New Year.
  3. Check inventory levels (how many units you have in stock) for key products before the busiest shopping season.
  4. Reach out to suppliers only for established products with low stock.
  5. Plan supplier approvals to finish by early November, leaving time to upload approved brands before Black Friday.

Holiday store preparation

1 min
  • Setting up holiday emails, audiences (groups of people your ads target) and remarketing ads takes about one hour for an experienced operator.
  • For a beginner, the same work takes about five to six hours.
  • Reusable email templates (ready-made email designs you reuse) reduce the time needed.
  • Preparing for Black Friday and similar seasonal events, in order:
    1. Make sure your warehouse holds enough stock for the expected demand.
    2. Produce new creatives, for example through a photo shoot for the product.
    3. Update the store before the event.
    4. Staff customer support so that customer questions are answered immediately.

Prepare stock and suppliers

2 min
  • Before scaling daily ad spend beyond 2x (double), confirm the supporting infrastructure is ready:
    • payment gateway (the service that processes card payments) processing capacity and uptime (how often it stays working),
    • supplier inventory levels and fulfillment speed (how fast orders are packed and shipped),
    • internal team capability to manage order volume and customer service. Aggressive scaling without these systems causes payment failures, shipping delays, and customer satisfaction collapse.
  • Q4 (October to December) is the peak sales season on Amazon.
  • Check inventory well before Q4 starts to avoid stockouts (running out of stock).
  • Missing Q4 can cost a large share of the year's sales.
  • When a supplier shows 10 or more orders per day, contact the supplier at once to secure stock before Chinese New Year (a major holiday when Chinese factories close).
  • Factories close around 1 February, while warehouses keep working for a few days.
  • Plan scaling and logistics (moving and storing goods) before the end of Q1 (January to March).
  • E-commerce usually slows down until late March.
  • Prepare fulfillment capacity and consider ordering stock before a planned push.
  • Reorder stock of the best-selling designs, since sales data (the record of what sold) shows which ones perform.
  • Limit stock of weak sellers.
  • Scale a product through repeat seasons, with new photos and creatives prepared in advance for each season.
  • Sales from a winter product can grow with the next peak season.
  • Check the supplier for Black Friday discounts only with a clear volume plan.
  • Ask for a specific discount ahead of time.
  • The author of the source had not requested one and was unsure suppliers would give one, so treat this as an open question.
  • Managing thousands of SKUs (unique product codes, one per item) alone makes inventory discrepancies (mismatches between your store's stock count and the supplier's count) easy to miss.
  • Build a routine to check stock and notify customers when their item is affected.
  • Order stock in proportion to ad budget changes.
  • When you raise the budget by 20%, order stock in roughly the same proportion.
  • Increase the budget only when performance has been consistent.
  • Do not scale ads in the same month you place a new stock order or change ad parameters (the settings of your ads) at that time.
  • Test first, confirm consistency, and only then scale.

Prepare customer service capacity

1 min
  • As soon as scaling gets profitable (even £200/day profit), begin preparing customer service capacity.
  • Hire virtual assistants (remote helpers) or enlist partners in advance.
  • The customer service backlog (the pile of unanswered requests) is often the biggest bottleneck (the slowest point that holds everything up) when revenue spikes.
  • Major mistake: not planning customer service before scaling.
  • £76,000 revenue over the Black Friday weekend required 3 virtual assistants and 2 staff members (5 people).
  • It still resulted in 5 days late on customer service responses.
  • This nearly destroyed the business.
  • Always hire virtual assistants before profit scales dramatically.
  • Both sources cite 76,000 for the Friday-to-Sunday revenue, but in different currencies: £ in the first case and USD in the second.
  • Each figure is kept as stated in its source.

Build the cash position before scaling

1 min
  • Aggressive scaling needs an extra 5,000-10,000 USD of cash for creatives and scaling to 2x or 3x.
  • Plan cash flow before spending, because e-commerce is a cash-flow driven business.
  • When business grows, ad spend and other growth costs rise before the money comes back. **
  • So aggressive growth can outrun the cash and finances available to fund it. **
  • Know your real financial position before pushing growth hard. **
  • Otherwise, an active business with rising revenue can still lose profitability. **
  • Before scaling, secure enough budget to keep ads running continuously.
  • One store owner ran out of cash for ad spend on a winning product (one that sells well), was forced to pause for about two months, and sales did not return to the earlier level after restarting.
  • Cash flow can be managed in several ways: wait for payouts (money the payment platform pays out to you) before ordering from suppliers.
  • Other options are to use a private supplier, or to use credit cards in a pinch (only in an emergency).
  • At small scale, a personal or simple business account can be enough, but it gets messy as orders grow.

Protect against payment holds

1 min
  • Prepare for PayPal cash hold restrictions (limits PayPal places when it freezes your money).
  • PayPal may restrict withdrawals to £18,000 per month if large sums flood the account suddenly.
  • This severely impacts cash flow.
  • Have alternative payment processors (other companies that take customer payments) or credit lines (borrowing limits you can draw on) ready.
  • Payment holds that freeze cash flow are a typical recurring setback (a problem that keeps coming back).
  • Plan for them as normal events.

Set ad budget rules for peak events

1 min
  • Before raising the budget of a Facebook ad set (one group of ads with its own budget and audience) in Q4, the product needs both a good conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) and good ad results.
  • Scaling a product that only has high clicks but no sales spends budget without payoff.
  • Seasonal demand drops can be handled by reducing ad budgets in regions where demand is weak. **
  • Keep ads running where demand is still strong, using geographic targeting (choosing the countries or areas where your ads show). **
  • Budget changes during a short event differ by approach:
    • Short high-demand event (for example, Black Friday): the budget can be raised several times in one day when an ad set shows good results, as long as results are checked.
    • This is a short-term approach for the event and not a standard scaling rule.
    • Standard scaling: increase ad budget only when performance has been consistent, in steps of about 20%, with stock ordered in roughly the same proportion.
    • Do not combine an event push with a new stock order or a change to ad parameters in the same month.

Black Friday scaling procedure

1 min
  1. Run ads for Black Friday week at a discount, and announce a bigger discount for the day itself.
  2. On the day, raise the ad budget every four hours.
  3. At each check, compare the sales made in the last four hours and check whether ROAS (return on ad spend: sales earned for each unit of money spent on ads) is still strong.
  4. If ROAS holds, raise the budget again; if it falls, stop increasing.
  5. Use strong image ads with big offers (up to 60% off) and run them for Black Friday and Christmas.

Case: budget doubling

1 min
  • In this case, the daily ad budget was doubled every four hours.
  • This reached roughly 3,000 USD of spend on a single ad, with a Friday to Sunday revenue of 76,000 USD and a stated 7% return.
  • Retargeting cannot be relied on for Black Friday.
  • When retargeting got weaker, the team in this case shifted to cold traffic (visitors who do not know your store yet) and lookalike audiences (new people who resemble your buyers).
  • Email and SMS (text messages) remain useful for pre-sale reminders and for announcing an extension of the sale to customers who missed it.

Plan the post-peak and off-season months

1 min
  • January and February can be strong for dropshippers (sellers who ship products from suppliers without holding stock) because many competitors run out of stock.
  • Cost per mille (CPM, the price of 1,000 ad views) is cheap, and buyers still purchase.
  • Do not ignore post-holiday months.
  • Use the low-demand months to build creatives, assets and cash reserves (money saved for later) rather than to run heavy campaigns.

Plan for recurring setbacks

1 min
  • Typical recurring setbacks in e-commerce (online selling) include payment holds that freeze cash flow, sudden ad account bans (the ad platform shutting off your ad account), team members making mistakes, multi-day stretches of stagnant sales (days with no sales growth), and poor results that drain momentum.
  • Plan for these as normal events.
