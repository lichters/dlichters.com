Preparing a store for sale or shutdown

Section 20.25 · 25 of 26 in this chapter · 5 min read

In short

  • The store must run without you. A buyer buys a business, not a job.
  • Write down every key task as an SOP (standard operating procedure: a step-by-step written guide).
  • Hire and train VAs (virtual assistants: remote helpers) 3 to 6 months before you sell.
  • Keep old advice in mind. Parts marked "Older advice" may be out of date.

Making a store sellable without the owner

  • A buyer buys a business, not a job.
  • The store must run on its own.
  • It must not need you every day.
  • Write down all critical processes as SOPs (standard operating procedures: written step-by-step guides).
  • Then remove yourself from the essential tasks.
  • Most buyers do not want a job that takes 40 hours a week.
  • Hire a team before you sell.
  • Step out of the daily work.
  • The team also shows which tasks still need written guides.
  • A store that runs without the seller is worth more.
  • It has documented SOPs, training documents, and processes.
  • The buyer can take over the daily work without you.
  • Services that depend on the seller lower the valuation. (Valuation means what the store is worth.)
  • A service tied to the product can add value if a team runs it.
  • Some buyers dislike the extra complexity.
  • Portfolio investors (buyers who own many stores) want passive income (money that comes in without daily work).
  • So VAs who run the site without the owner matter a lot to them.
  • Buyers who like to tinker (change things themselves) prefer a more hands-on setup.
  • A store can be sold while it is still early.
  • List it with its key details.
  • Add either a price or a "best offer" note in a for-sale channel (a place where stores are listed for sale).
  • This lets buyers make offers.

Documentation and SOPs

  • Document all processes as SOPs before you list the store.
  • Clear documentation lets a buyer take over with confidence.
  • Missing documentation makes buyers hold back money or cut the price.
  • SOPs and a documented, trained team (for example VAs) raise a store's attractiveness.
  • This is especially true for newer buyers.
  • They can decide between a completed sale and no sale.
  • Document critical processes in SOPs so the buyer can run them without you.

Building and handing over the team

Procedure: prepare a store team for sale

  1. Hire and train new VAs about 3 to 6 months before selling so they can run the operations.
  2. Document SOPs.
  3. Hand over the trained team and processes to the buyer as part of the sale. Do not try to train after the sale closes.
  • Using VAs to run daily operations makes a store more attractive.
  • The owner does not need to be involved.
  • This raises buyer interest.
  • It can improve the multiple (the price as a number times yearly profit).
  • VAs usually transfer with the business.
  • If they do not, you must train replacement hires before closing.
  • Buyers will contractually require you to handle the handover. (A contract is a signed legal agreement.)
  • Buyers may hold back part of the payment until training is complete.
  • You can write a sale-linked bonus into each VA's contract.
  • Each VA gets 1% of the profit when a site is sold.
  • This aligns the team with the long-term value of the business.

Case: owner-dependent production

  • A seller's wine-glass store made about $1,000 per month in net profit (profit after all costs).
  • The owner said it needed about two hours per week.
  • However, a family member hand-made the product, working 30 to 40 hours per week.
  • This made the business depend on one unpaid producer.
  • That producer might not transfer.
  • So a buyer would discount the store.

Contracts with staff and suppliers

  • Formal written contracts with VAs and suppliers are not required to sell a business.
  • They are helpful anyway.
  • They define expectations.
  • They protect both parties during the transition.
  • Contracts with staff and suppliers are not required for a sale.
  • They still add buyer confidence.
  • A contract may be needed before a kickback (a payment a supplier gives you for business sent their way) counts as profit.

Notifying suppliers and staff

  • Tell your suppliers that you are selling.
  • Clarify what terms the new owner expects.
  • Do this so they understand the changes before the handover.
  • Inform your staff of the pending sale as early as your circumstances allow.
  • Most buyers keep existing employees.
  • This eases continuity concerns (worries that the business will stop working well).

Post-signing handover

  • After the asset purchase agreement (the signed contract for the sale) is signed, the broker's team can migrate the accounts. (A broker is a middleman who finds buyers and runs the sale. Migrate means move to the new owner.)
  • The broker's team can also run the handover.
  • The level of help depends on what the buyer and seller each want.
  • Some sellers prefer that the broker does not touch the accounts.
  • Less experienced sellers are guided step by step.
  • Cross-border handovers are handled case by case. (Cross-border means between countries.)
  • For example, a buyer in another country may need a local company.
  • These can take months.

Valuation factors

  • A store's valuation is based on profit and success.
  • More suppliers, more revenue, and better profit mean a higher sale price.
  • **
  • A buyer will not trust a P&L (profit and loss statement: a report of money in and out) that is only typed into a spreadsheet.
  • The seller must prove the figures with proper accounting records.
  • So accounting must be upgraded well before an exit (before you leave the store).
  • The higher the expected sale price, the more important this proof becomes.
  • **
  • Dropshipping businesses (stores that sell products shipped directly from suppliers) can sell for six, seven, or even eight figures.
  • A buyer will expect audited, verifiable financial records before paying that kind of price. (Audited means checked by an outside accountant.)
  • **

Portfolio buyers

  • Portfolio buyers typically set up a separate LLC (limited liability company: a company that keeps the owner's personal money separate) for each acquisition.
  • This compartmentalizes liability (keeps risks in separate boxes).
  • It also lets them manage tax reporting independently.
  • Portfolio buyers want stores that keep running without the seller.
  • They often keep the existing team and systems for a while after purchase.
  • They do not shut everything off at once.
  • Continuity in the first months after the sale is part of what the buyer pays for.

Sale channels and brokers

  • Store sale options depend on age and profit.
  • **
Store profile Route
Under one year old, modest net profit Member marketplace listing (a site where sellers list stores for buyers)
Over one year old, significant net profit Offer to a broker such as Empire Flippers (a company that buys and sells online businesses)
  • Selling a store means transferring its ownership inside Shopify (an online store platform).
  • The store can be sold with or without a full supplier set.
  • **

Closing a store instead of selling

  • If a store cannot be actively managed for a long period, it is better to sell it.
  • Long period means about 18 months.
  • Selling and restarting later beats letting the store sit idle (unused).
  • **

Procedure: shut down a store you no longer need

  1. Export all products and data with Rewind backups (a tool that saves copies of your store data) or the product CSV export (a spreadsheet file of your products).
  2. Stop paying for the store once the export is saved.
  3. Contact any supplier you approved with. Thank them and explain that the store is closing.
