Owner time, task audit and what to delegate

Section 20.2 · 2 of 26 in this chapter · 29 min read

In short

  • A business that needs you every day stops when you stop. Build it so it runs without you.
  • Do each task yourself first. Then you know what good work looks like before you hand it off.
  • Hand off tasks that repeat, drain you, or that you do poorly. Keep the work only you can do.
  • Write down each method before you hire. A new person can then follow it.
  • Start small and build up. Do not hand over everything at once.
More key points
  • Hire help once your store makes sales. You need time for finding products, ads and offers.
  • Hire help early. Waiting until you are overwhelmed is a common mistake.
  • Give away tasks that others can do. Keep supplier contact, ads you understand and big decisions.
  • Never hand off a task if it would make you lose money. Wait until you can afford it.

Why owner time is the scarce resource

2 min
  • A business that needs your daily involvement stops when you are unavailable. The goal is a business that keeps running without you in day-to-day work. That is the main reason to document and delegate early. Documenting means writing down how each task is done. Delegating means handing tasks to other people.
  • Build systems so the business keeps running when you are not working in it. A business that depends on constant personal effort does not scale (grow bigger without the work growing with it).
  • Scale by building backend systems for customer retention. Backend systems are behind-the-scenes tools. Retention means keeping customers who come back. The business keeps running without you doing daily work.
  • ** Scaling is easier and more fulfilling than staying solo. You can focus on work you enjoy instead of handling every task, including the ones you dislike.
  • ** To do higher-value work, you must build a team. Scaling comes naturally when you focus on meaningful work instead of chasing it directly.
  • Running the business alone is possible for years. Outsourcing repetitive daily tasks is a lifestyle choice, not a requirement. A lifestyle business is built to fit your life, not to grow large.
  • Build the business as a lifestyle business. A single store can run part-time with about 15 to 20 minutes of work per day. Full-time work on one store suggests tasks should be outsourced.
  • Recurring daily tasks use up your time. They are the first candidates for automation (a setup that runs the task by itself) or delegation. Your time is better spent on growth, finding new opportunities and other priorities.
  • Remove busy work that does not add to results. Do not just do it faster. Removing it is one of the fastest ways to stand out from people who have struggled for years.
  • Growth stalls when you keep senior-level tasks (tasks that need top skill) that are not worth your time or energy. Sustainable growth (growth that lasts) needs a team of people who outperform you in specific areas of the business.
  • Income grows through leverage. Leverage means getting results from other people's work, so your income does not depend only on your hours. Leverage comes from working less in the business, not from working longer hours. Build it by hiring staff and learning to manage them. A business earning around 250,000 dollars a year cannot grow if you do every task. Delegation frees time for new ideas and new businesses.
  • Parkinson's law says work expands to fill the time available for it. Owners who work 40 to 60 hours a week can test this. Deliberately cut your working time, for example to 10 hours in one week. Often the same output still gets done. Use the test to find unnecessary work and tighten deadlines (shorten the time limits).
  • ** Outsourcing and personal effectiveness are closely linked for an owner-operator (someone who does the work themselves). Delegating tasks frees time and focus for the work only you can do.
  • ** Owner-level preparation comes before scaling tactics. This means time management, prioritization (deciding what matters most first) and focus. A business cannot grow faster than its leader's capacity to execute (ability to get things done).
  • The owner's role shifts from running the business to building it.

Owner time and hourly value

1 min
  • Work out your hourly time value (what one hour of your time is worth). Multiply it by the weekly hours you spend in your current role. If an agency (an outside company you pay for work) costs less than this weekly opportunity cost (what you lose by not using that time elsewhere) and frees you for higher-impact work, the financial math improves.
  • Example: if you spend 10 hours weekly managing ads at five hundred dollars per hour, your time costs five thousand dollars weekly. Outsourcing frees that value. You can redirect it to higher-impact work such as supplier negotiations (talks with suppliers about price and terms) or product development.
  • Estimate how long a task takes by doing it yourself first. Then allow for the staff member's experience. A product upload (adding a product to your store) may take five to ten minutes. Experienced staff can be faster than you. Use a reasonable average. Reject unreasonable estimates, such as a 500-word article in half an hour.
  • Outsourcing costs money. But the freed time usually lets the business do more growth work. That extra work can repay the cost over time. Refusing to outsource to protect every dollar of profit can limit growth and reduce total profit in the long run.
  • Outsourcing early can speed up progress a lot. This is true even when the cost is hard to carry and leaves little profit in the short term. The business reaches a better position sooner than it would by doing everything itself.

Task audit: list, measure and test

6 min

Step 1: map the work

  • Decide what you actually need before anything else. Apply the four-step method (a fixed order of four steps) in this order:
    1. Definition: decide what you actually need.
    2. Elimination: remove everything you can get rid of.
    3. Automation: automate what can run without you.
    4. Liberation: outsource what remains and cannot be automated.
  • Work time is drained by three groups of tasks: minimize expenses, material items, responsibilities, accounting and daily routine tasks.
    • Automate: lead generation (finding people who may buy), ad management, FAQs (lists of common questions with answers), email responders (automatic email replies), call tracking numbers (phone numbers that show which ad brought each call), order emails and passive income (money that keeps coming in with little ongoing work).
    • Liberate, meaning hand off: work to virtual assistants (remote helpers you hire online), phone support, a bookkeeper (the person who records money in and out), partners, or sell the store and live on investment income.
  • Build a task chart before hiring:
    1. List the main business areas (in the example: operations, customer service, sales, product support, sales and marketing, accounting).
    2. Break each area into small, specific tasks, for example "answer the phone" instead of "customer support".
    3. Color-code who does each task. Tasks you do are blue. Tasks you do 100% alone get a heavy dark red outline.
    4. When a task goes to someone else, recolor it green so you can see what has left your plate.
  • To choose which tasks to outsource, work through these steps in order:
    1. List every task you perform to run the business day to day.
    2. Record how many hours per week each task takes.
    3. Focus on the pain points (the tasks that cause the most trouble). In dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships straight to the customer, so you keep no stock), these are usually repetitive tasks such as product uploads during setup, or order placement and payment processing when sales are high.
    4. Outsource the simplest repetitive tasks first.
  • Review the task lists of each priority in your 180-day plan (your plan for the next six months), up to five lists. Ask how much of the work you should really be doing, and which tasks could go to someone else. **
  • Plan a delegation: **
    1. Identify the task and ask whether only you can do it, not whether you are the best person for it.
    2. If not, plan when it can be handed off.
    3. Identify where and how to find someone to do it, and the options available.
    4. Create SOPs (standard operating procedures, which are written step-by-step methods) and training materials.
    5. Hand the task over when the timing is right, or set a date if it is not possible immediately.

Step 2: test each task against the criteria

  • Test each task on the task list against seven criteria: **

    1. The task is simple and repeated.
    2. The task is urgent but not important.
    3. The task is neither urgent nor important.
    4. The task is time consuming for small returns.
    5. You are not good at the task, or you do not enjoy it.
    6. The task is unpredictable and disruptive.
    7. The task is affordable to outsource.

    A task only needs to meet ONE of these criteria to be a candidate for outsourcing.

  • Delegate any recurring task that takes more than one hour per day of your time and does not need you specifically. Your top priority as a business leader is delegation, not doing everything. **

  • Never delegate a task you would not do yourself. Only hand off work whose results you can judge.

  • Outsource tasks as early as possible when you lack natural strengths for them or do not enjoy them. This creates space for others to contribute. Keep the work that fits your strengths for yourself.

  • Work that does not come naturally to you, or that drains you, should go to someone else. Every time work must be converted into a form that another person has to handle, value is lost. So fewer conversions give a better return.

  • A task you keep postponing or dread is a sign it should be outsourced, even when it is important to the business. **

  • Tasks you dislike or are weak at tend to be avoided and never completed. Outsource them even if they are important, such as content writing for SEO (search engine optimisation, which makes pages easier to find in Google) or managing ads. **

  • Do not outsource a task only because you dislike it. Some tasks you must keep yourself, especially those that build key relationships.

  • Unpredictable tasks that pop up and need quick handling, such as phone calls and online chats, should go to someone else to protect your focus. **

  • Small optimizations such as product data, product descriptions and SEO metadata (hidden page information that search engines read) help. But they are not big growth levers. They are good outsourcing candidates for time-consuming, low-return work. **

  • Customer service is urgent but usually not important. It does not grow a store much on its own. Keep your focus on tasks that drive large gains, such as SEO, email marketing (sending emails to customers) and ads management. **

  • Growth increases customer service volume. If you handle it personally, you lose time for growth work. That creates a vicious cycle (a loop that feeds itself and gets worse) that threatens the business in the long run. **

  • Tasks that are neither urgent nor important should not be done by you at all. If they are still wanted, someone else should do them. Keep your focus only on important tasks. **

  • When a task needs research, define exactly what must be found. Then hand the research to someone else. Doing the research yourself keeps you stuck in manual work. **

  • Consider a virtual assistant when work-life balance (the split between work and personal life) suffers. That happens when the business takes more screen time than personal time. Delegation can restore free time for family and friends.

  • Routine work that keeps getting too little time can be protected. Move it into the first two work hours after the daily business and community tasks. Ad creation then gets protected focus time (time with no interruptions) instead of leftover slots.

Priority and sequencing

  • Outsource the most outsourceable tasks first. Build up gradually as the business grows. Keep the tasks that only you can do, or that carry the highest return and complexity. **
  • Outsource the repetitive, low-skill tasks that eat your time first, such as loading products, order placement and payment processing. Keep in-house (inside your own team) the tasks you do best. Also keep any task that depends on supplier relationships or on understanding paid traffic (visitors who come from ads).
  • Outsource customer service once order volume (the number of orders) exceeds what you can answer alone. Keep supplier relationships in-house. They are central to the business and hard to hand over.
  • Prioritize outsourcing customer service within the next six months as a primary candidate for delegation, if you have not already done so. **
  • Owners who already outsource should review how they do it. Do not only look for new tasks to hand off. The working process around outsourcing determines much of its value. **

Before you delegate: do the task yourself first

4 min

The rule

  • Do a task yourself before you outsource it. This way you know what good results look like.
  • Learn each task yourself before you delegate it. Then you can judge the finished work. Outsource fully only once you have the skills and can check the quality.
  • Before delegating any task, build the skill yourself and find out what works. A team member can only follow a method you already know. Otherwise they produce generic output with no clear target.
  • Delegate a task only after you have learned to do it yourself. Knowing the method lets you judge the output, teach the next person, and cover the work if a team member leaves or raises prices.
  • Learning a skill yourself first protects the business if a team member leaves or suddenly raises rates. Once you know the method, you can run the task yourself for a short time or teach the next hire.
  • Perform each day-to-day task yourself until you fully understand it. Only then outsource it. This applies to all routine business tasks, such as customer contact and order handling.
  • Outsourcing works only when you already know the correct way to do a task. Without doing the process yourself, you cannot write clear instructions or judge whether the outside person is doing it right.
  • Delegating a process you have never run yourself is a mistake. You cannot explain it properly or verify the results. Start with hands-on work and outsource only after the process is proven.
  • Never outsource paid ads, web design, content, SEO or social posts to outside providers too early. Learn each process first. Document it for each supplier, then share the documents with a future team. (Dated 2024, not older advice.)

The steps

  • Three steps to working less and delegating:
    1. Build a documented business system with processes and tools.
    2. Find and hire the right person.
    3. Train thoroughly and let go of day-to-day control.
  • Order for scaling a business with a team:
    1. Do the task yourself until you know the steps work and how long it really takes.
    2. Confirm that the task makes money before you scale it.
    3. Document it as an SOP with short, simple steps.
    4. Decide whether the role needs a contractor (a freelancer paid per job) or an employee (staff on payroll). Decide whether a virtual assistant or an apprentice (someone learning by working with you) fits the work.
    5. Train the new person with the skill transfer process (a method for passing on a skill) so that efficiency is not lost.
    6. Repeat the cycle for each new process as the business grows.
  • Outsourcing sequence:
    1. Do the task yourself first.
    2. Eliminate tasks that are not necessary.
    3. Automate what you can, then hire for the rest.
    4. Record your workflow as a screencast (a video recording of your screen with narration) so someone else can follow it.
    5. After hiring, follow up with the hire every week.
  • Before outsourcing a task:
    1. Do the task yourself until you know exactly how it works.
    2. Remove every unnecessary step, spreadsheet column or report.
    3. Automate or buy software for what remains.
    4. Hire a person only for tasks that cannot be removed or automated. Train them with a screencast of you doing the task.
  • Perform each task yourself inside the business until you know how it works and what the correct result looks like. Then:
    1. Document every step you take. Create a user guide (a manual for the business) that works as the instruction manual.
    2. Fill the ready-made templates with your own business information to complete the user guide.
    3. Hand the completed user guide to the person you bring in, so they can run the task based on what already works.
    4. Once the task is delegated, focus your own time on growth or other priorities.

What the owner must understand

  • You cannot train someone for a task you have not done yourself. Before handing off customer support or order processing, you must be able to explain the likely customer questions. Explain how to respond to them in different situations. Explain the supplier-specific steps too, including who to contact and what to do when an order goes to a different supplier.
  • Without a basic understanding of what an outsourced person does, you cannot tell whether they are succeeding or doing good work. This applies equally to outsourcing store building or traffic setup (getting visitors to your store). Build a minimum base-level understanding of every area before hiring.
  • A business does not need to be profitable before you start. But you must understand what is happening in it. For example, you should be able to explain CPM (the cost to show your ad 1,000 times) and conversion results (visitors who take the wanted action, such as buying) when the assistant reports them.
  • Handing over the whole dropshipping business to a virtual assistant without understanding it means you cannot tell whether store design, emails or ad results are good. Always keep enough knowledge to evaluate the work.
  • A beginner should not outsource store creation entirely. Learning to build the store yourself lets you check and teach the assistant later.
  • Early in a business, one person may need to wear all the roles. This includes supplier calls and uploads. Once a process is working, hand specific tasks to others.

What to delegate first: the order

3 min

General order

  • Recommended delegation order for a running dropshipping business:
    1. Product research (finding products to sell)
    2. Store build (building the online shop)
    3. Traffic and ads (getting visitors through paid and free ads)
    4. Order fulfillment (packing and shipping orders)
    5. Customer support
  • Delegate in this order as the business grows:
    1. Product research: build your own skill first, then hand over the search with your criteria and tools.
    2. Store development: learn store building yourself. Then have someone build the store to your structure once you have a product to test.
    3. Traffic and ads: learn the Facebook or TikTok ad strategy (your plan for paid ads) yourself. Then let a team member prepare creatives (ad images and videos) and set up campaigns (ad runs) under your monitoring.
    4. Order fulfillment: outsource early, since it is time-consuming and not highly skilled work.
    5. Customer support: outsource early as well, covering email, live chat and social media messages.
  • Virtual assistants can take over store setup, Facebook ad management, customer emails and order fulfilment. You check the finished results. Outsourcing is recommended when there is not enough time to finish tasks on schedule.
  • As a store grows, outsource tasks such as customer service, email marketing and Google Ads (Google's paid search ads). Then you can focus on the highest-impact work, such as Facebook ads and creatives. You need to leverage your time once the business reaches a size where doing everything personally no longer scales.
  • Outsourcing ads, email and creator scripts (word-for-word scripts for video creators) to an agency and hiring a virtual assistant can free your time for other work. This makes sense when customer service and operations take many hours per day.
  • Outsource repetitive roles such as customer service once they consume daily time. When one person covers customer service, ad creation, finances and fulfillment at the same time, a structured calendar (a planned schedule of tasks) becomes essential to get anything finished.
  • Early on, when you have limited hours, delegate the most time-consuming tasks first.

Beginner sequence

  • Typical beginner delegation sequence:
    1. Do the product research yourself and pick the product to test.
    2. Let the assistant build the store and write product descriptions while you learn the store setup.
    3. Have the assistant install apps such as Klaviyo (an app that sends emails and texts to customers) and SMS Bump (an app for text-message marketing). Have it implement them while you understand what each one does.
    4. Let the assistant search for product images and videos on Instagram and Facebook.
    5. Once orders arrive, have the assistant learn CJ Dropshipping (a supplier that ships your orders) and handle order fulfillment.
    6. Create a separate support email address and give the assistant direct access to it. For tricky emails, the assistant sends you a screenshot. You dictate the reply, and the assistant sends it.

Early-stage and repetitive-first sequence

  • Early-stage sequence for when to outsource:
    1. Follow the blueprint (the step-by-step plan).
    2. Start processing orders yourself.
    3. Once you are comfortable with the business and feel qualified to run it, begin outsourcing.
  • Start outsourcing early and in small steps, but only when the business can afford the cost. Build up gradually. Do not hand over everything at once.
  • Assistants can also take over your personal admin, such as drafting emails or researching documents for a task. Handing these tasks off frees time for the business.
  • At the business-owner stage, delegate personal and university or study tasks as well as business tasks. Hire missing skills, such as Google Ads or email marketing, into the company. Do not rely on outside help for them.

The first hire and the first assistant

4 min

Timing

  • Hire help once your store is live, running ads and making sales. You need time for product research (finding products to sell), ads and offers.
  • Early on, when you have few hours, delegate (hand to someone else) the most time-consuming tasks first.
  • There are two starting points for a team. Either sales are coming in and you need help to free time, or you are early on and lack the time to run everything.
  • Both starting points lead to the same goal: delegate work so you can focus on product research, ads and offers.
  • Delegation frees time for your highest-value tasks, such as product research, ads and offers.
  • Outsourcing (paying an outside person or company to do a task) order fulfillment (shipping orders) or customer support early is a common way to do this.
  • Outsource as early as possible once the business is up, running and profitable (making money).
  • At that point, hire help, adjust your schedule and free your time from daily tasks.
  • Waiting until you can no longer cope before outsourcing is a mistake.
  • Once the business is profitable, start delegating routine tasks. Do not wait until you are overwhelmed.
  • Hire your first VA (virtual assistant, a remote helper) earlier rather than later. The sales gap after hiring was large.
  • At low volume, around 20 sales a month of high-ticket items (expensive products), the owner can handle calls and emails personally. A VA is not needed yet.
  • Add support once revenue and workload clearly justify it.
  • Many owners delay hiring a VA because they feel the business depends on them personally.
  • Delegating only works once you accept that the assistant will be the face of the business. The assistant must be trained to represent it.
  • Many owners delay hiring because they believe nobody else can run the business. The project also feels overwhelming with no clear starting point.
  • Breaking the work into a system, a hire and training steps makes it manageable.
  • Once the early hustle phase is over (about six to twelve months at most), hand off tasks outside your strengths and values.
  • Keep only the work in your sweet spot (where your skills and interest meet).
  • Founders who run websites, supplier contact, ads, email and phone alone for years get slow replies. Their ad optimization (improving ads over time) also gets neglected. **
  • Build a team and systems within the first few months once sales take off. **
  • Outsourcing brings in experience you lack. For example, a hired customer service specialist with 14 years of experience improved service processes and customer engagement.

Hiring a virtual assistant

  • To hire a virtual assistant (VA): (Older advice, 2021. The two units are identical and merged here.)
    1. Run the store yourself when it goes live. Let orders and emails come in.
    2. Learn how to handle each supplier's orders and the most frequently asked customer questions.
    3. Write these steps in a Google Doc as an instruction manual for your business.
    4. Hire the assistant and give them that document.
  • Outsourcing means hiring a person for tasks that still need human contact. Tasks that repeat the same way should be automated (run by software).
  • Early on, the typical outsourced worker is a VA hired remotely from anywhere in the world. They are not an employee on payroll in an office or warehouse.
  • A VA makes sense when you want time for scaling (growing) and growth, not repetitive tasks.
  • Delegating routine work frees you for decisions that move the business forward.
  • A VA buys back time. Time is the scarcest resource for a business owner.
  • A VA takes over daily tasks such as answering phones and managing email. You can then focus on growth, travel or rest.
  • Delegating daily tasks to a VA freed the owner to focus on the big picture.
  • Hiring the first VA earlier rather than later is recommended. The sales gap after hiring was large.
  • Handing over daily operations, including customer service, let the owner stop working day to day.
  • A team that treats the business as its own can run it while you are away.
  • When an employee gets responsibility and trust, their performance improves noticeably. They also ask fewer questions.
  • Letting go of tasks early is the main leverage (power to get more result from less effort) for the owner.
  • Training and letting go: train thoroughly, then let go of daily control.
  • Hire and train with the screencast (a recorded screen video) and SOP (standard operating procedure, a step-by-step guide). After hiring, follow up with the hire every week.

Accountability

  • Without someone to hold you accountable (responsible to), tasks get postponed forever.
  • Hiring help is an investment. It also trains your leadership and team management skills.

Owner role as the business scales

4 min

Core role

  • The owner should own strategy. Find where the valuable traffic (visitors who may buy) is. Design the funnel (the path from ad to purchase) and the ladder (a line-up of offers from cheap to expensive).
  • Then hire specialists to run ads and optimize tactics.
  • Trying to do every task yourself slows the business down. Division of labor (each person does one part) makes growth possible.
  • At scale, the owner is a strategic leader. Set the vision, make sure work gets done and put the right people in the right roles.
  • Hand daily tasks to others. These include supplier calls, uploading product pages and optimizing the site.
  • Partnerships, branding and the X factor (what makes you stand out) are the three most effective ways to raise revenue. The owner's time should go there.
  • Optimization, product uploads and research can be outsourced to a team.
  • Once the team handles daily operations and escalations (problems passed up), the owner keeps only sales, some marketing and accounting. These can be done from anywhere.
  • The owner's role shifts from running the business to building it.
  • Hand over any task someone else can do. This includes code-level changes (changes to the website's programming) you cannot make yourself. **
  • A strong business leader delegates tasks rather than doing everything personally. **
  • Outsourcing frees time for growth work, such as finding new businesses, new ideas and new opportunities.
  • Using time on repetitive order handling leaves no room for growth work.
  • Outsourcing routine tasks frees time for growth. A business cannot grow while the owner only handles daily operations.
  • Delegating these tasks is the step that lets the business expand.
  • Outsourcing is a major lever (a tool with big effect) for e-commerce owners in marketing, growth and daily operations. **
  • It is worth starting early rather than waiting until the business is large. **

Future role

  • The owner's future role can be to step back from daily work. For example, manage a team or a network of businesses. **
  • Decide in your vision whether you stay in the weeds (hands-on work) or move to an advisory (advice-giving) or management role. **
  • An owner moves up the ownership ladder as early revenue produces free cash flow (money left after bills) to pay the first hire. **
  • Each new hire takes one more task off the owner. The steps go from doing the work, to managing those who do it, to managing those who work on the business. **
  • Scaling works best when the owner only reviews what changed. Examples: a supplier that stopped responding, a backordered product (sold out, more coming), or a stock report with low inventory. **
  • Routine work is delegated. **
  • Before leaving the country or taking a long break, set up the store. **
  • Also hire someone to handle phone calls and customer messages. **
  • Early on, one person may need to wear all roles, including supplier calls and uploads.
  • Once a process works, hand specific tasks to others.

Owner-level example

  • A dropshipping owner (sells products that a supplier ships directly to customers) ran a niche store (a narrow product group). Monthly sales were about 40,000 USD, and the store had stopped growing.
  • Customer feedback showed the range was too narrow. He planned to add about 700 products.
  • He hired freelancers for image editing, product descriptions and uploads to Shopify (online store software). The work took about three weeks.
  • Within two months, monthly sales rose from 40,000 USD to 80,000 USD.
  • Afterwards his own time grew to 20 to 30 hours per week. He then moved the tasks to a team.
  • His weekly time fell from over 30 hours to under 5 hours. He kept pay-per-click (ads you pay for each click) management himself.
  • Starting point: a single-owner store running since 2016. The owner still had a corporate job and worked about 40 hours per week on the business in January.
  • The owner set a goal of $2 million revenue for 2021 and $1 million by June 30. 2020 revenue was $1.4 million, with a team of three.
  • In early January 2021, the owner joined a state COVID vaccination response team. For six months, he worked about seven days a week, in 12-hour days.
  • On the response team he coordinated 121 nurses and 70 National Guard members. Together they gave 250,000 vaccinations.
    • What was done: the business was run with help from a team. By June, the owner's time was cut to about 10 hours per week, while revenue kept growing.
    • Result: $1 million was reached in May, one month ahead of the June goal. Over the six months, about $1.29 to $1.3 million was reached.
  • A store owner ran a store from 2014 and handled all operations for four years. She struggled with trust issues and overwhelm.
  • In 2018, after having a child, she hired a VA. The VA saved the business and her well-being.
  • Key insight: build systems before a crisis forces you to delegate. This is better than waiting until life events demand it.

  • The first approach is automation through apps (software tools) for repeatable work. Automation covers what software can handle.
  • The second approach is delegation of daily tasks to people. Delegation covers what still needs a human.
  • The usual order: set up automation apps for repetitive work first. Then delegate the daily tasks that remain.
  • Use both approaches together to keep your daily time low. Do not use either one alone.

What must stay in-house

2 min
  • Keep supplier contact in-house. Supplier relationships bring bulk rates (lower prices for large orders) and discounts that grow as the business grows.
  • Do not hand this task to someone else, even when it is unpleasant.
  • Keep supplier relationships in-house. They are central to the business and hard to hand over.
  • Keep the tasks you do best.
  • Keep any task that depends on supplier relationships or on understanding paid traffic in-house.
  • The owner keeps only sales, some marketing and accounting. These can be done from anywhere.
  • Owner keeps strategy and key relationships. Partnerships, branding and the X factor are the three most effective ways to raise revenue.
  • Focus yourself on ads, supplier relationships, offers and big-picture decisions. **
  • Give tasks like web design and customer service to others. **
  • Outsource your weaknesses and keep your strengths.
  • Keep the work that fits your strengths for yourself. Set the framework or strategy.
  • Outsourcing works best when you define the end result you want from a task. Then let someone else run the execution (the doing).
  • You can keep the part you enjoy, such as setting the framework or strategy. A team member then manages the daily work.
  • Ads: if you are weak at ads or dislike them, hand off ad management.
  • Other sources say to keep understanding of paid traffic in-house.
  • In one case, the owner kept pay-per-click management personally after moving other tasks to a team.
  • Too early: do not outsource paid ads before you learn and document the process.
  • Source A's test for what must stay: a task the owner must keep is one where the owner's own knowledge is required for relationships or results.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Delegating a process you have never run yourself is a mistake.
  • Handing over the whole business to a VA without understanding it is a mistake.
  • Outsourcing a task costs money. The freed time usually lets the business do more growth work.
  • Never give away a task at a loss. If it would push the business into the red (losing money), wait until you can afford it. **
  • Some owners go closer to break-even (money in equals money out), because outsourcing can speed up growth. **
  • Busy work that does not add to results should be removed, not just done more efficiently.
  • Many owners delay hiring a VA because they feel the business depends on them personally.
  • Many owners delay hiring because they believe nobody else can run the business.
  • Waiting until you can no longer cope with the workload before outsourcing is a mistake.
  • Refusing to outsource to protect every dollar of profit can limit growth.
  • Do not outsource paid ads, web design, content, SEO (getting found on search engines) or social posts to outside providers too early.
  • Outsourcing a task only because you dislike it is a mistake.
  • Some tasks the owner must personally keep.

More rules worth knowing

1 min
  • Delegate in small, concrete tasks. An example is "can someone answer the phone for me?"
  • Do not delegate in broad functions like "I need customer support."
  • Only a specific task can be handed off cleanly and checked afterwards.
  • The operator first outsourced his own job to a VA. His job was writing blog posts and managing a social media page.
  • That freed time to build his own dropshipping business.
  • Tasks he had no time for were then also delegated. These were replying to customer emails and processing orders daily.
