Choosing and managing agencies and ad outsourcing

Section 20.14 · 14 of 26 in this chapter · 20 min read

In short

  • Run your own ads first. Keep them running at a profit for one to three months before you give them to anyone else.
  • Before you hire, know what good results look like. Otherwise you cannot tell whether the outside team is doing well.
  • Be careful with companies that contact you first, promise a lot, or charge very little.
  • Check any company's work with real numbers, and ask its current clients for their honest experience.
  • Use short contracts, and make agencies earn each extension with results.
More key points
  • Write down before work starts how you talk, who answers, and who covers when the main person is away.
  • Set a clear target and timeframe, such as 20 percent more ROAS (the money earned back for each dollar spent on ads).
  • Before firing an agency, first ask what it changed and switch back to the old setup.
  • Test a new agency on a few areas next to your current setup, then compare the results.

Default position: keep paid ads in-house first

2 min
  • Keep hands-on control of ad setup and budgets in-house, meaning done by your own team.
  • Outsourcing ad work (giving it to outside people) makes sense only once you are truly proficient in the ad platforms.
  • Then you know what to hire for and what results are possible.
  • Do not hire agencies for SEO (search engine optimization, meaning free visits from Google search), paid media (paid ads on any platform) or content.
  • Agencies (outside companies you pay for work) optimize for client retention (keeping clients paying) and their own metrics (their own scorecard).
  • They do not optimize for your niche (the narrow market you sell to) and your growth.
  • Instead, build internal teams, or test carefully with your own money after you have proven you can carry out the work.
  • Example: $3,000/month × 6 months = $18,000 spent on an SEO agency with zero results.
  • The agency did not dig into the niche or build a real strategy.
  • It optimized for client retention and billing, not for growth.
  • Takeaway: do not outsource core business work (the parts that keep the business running) until you can do it yourself and measure the results.
  • Do not outsource ad management or store building before you have a working system.
  • Outside help can maintain and grow a working store.
  • It cannot build a store from zero.
  • Do not outsource ad management until you have set up and proven profitable campaigns (groups of ads sharing one budget and goal) yourself.
  • An agency that cannot deliver conversions (sales or other wanted actions) at the start is a bad sign.
  • Run paid ads yourself and reach profitability for one to three months before outsourcing marketing.
  • This gives you a baseline (your starting numbers to compare against) to judge an agency.
  • Going to a marketing firm with a new store and no results usually fails.
  • Do not increase ad spend because account representatives (sales staff who work with advertisers) from Pinterest or Google push for bigger budgets.
  • Their pressure does not lead to more sales.
  • Keep the management of Google and Facebook campaigns in-house.
  • Do not give it to a third-party app (software from another company) or an agency.
  • This was the approach used at a spend of hundreds of thousands of dollars per month. **
  • Keep pay-per-click (PPC, where you pay each time someone clicks your ad) management in-house until you understand it well enough to judge an agency.
  • The owner spent about 20,000 USD on outside pay-per-click agencies without getting better results than doing it himself.
  • Keeping ads in-house can work when the know-how is internal to the business.
  • Ignore the advice that a new store should never outsource.
  • The sources below set the bar by results, not by store age.
  • (Note: this line is a caution, not a rule of the sources; see the conditions in the sections that follow.)

Prerequisites: the baseline before you outsource

2 min
  • Outsource paid ads only after the store has a baseline (starting numbers you can compare against).
  • Those numbers are impressions (how often ads are shown), clicks, abandoned carts (customers who leave before paying) and sales.
  • The store must also be profitable.
  • Without that baseline, a third party has nothing to beat, and its performance cannot be judged.
  • Outsourcing Google Ads (Google's ad platform) makes sense only when the account is already profitable and the owner does not want to run it.
  • The provider must commit to beating current results within a stated timeframe (a deadline it agrees to).
  • Without that baseline, a new agency's underperformance cannot be judged.
  • Keep Google Ads in-house until the ad account is established and returns at least 10x ROAS (ROAS, return on ad spend: the sales earned for each dollar spent on ads).
  • Only then consider an outside agency, because a new agency does not yet know the business or the traffic system (how visitors reach your store).
  • **
  • Do not outsource Google Ads management until your store is already profitable and you have proven what works.
  • Hire an ads manager for maintenance or scaling (growing what already works) only after you have optimized the account yourself and validated the strategy.
  • **
  • Do not outsource paid advertising until your store is profitable and you know what works.
  • First establish baseline performance, prove that the store converts, and build initial momentum yourself.
  • **
  • Outsourcing marketing should wait until the store has been run profitably in-house and results are clear.
  • After that, an agency can be tested on whether it beats those results.
  • Prove that paid campaigns are viable before paying someone else to run them.
  • Then outsource, so the owner can focus on other channels.
  • Outsource Google Ads or social media management only after those campaigns are already profitable, because an agency cannot fix unprofitable campaigns.
  • **
  • Consider a Google Ads agency only after your store is already profitable and you want to accelerate what works.
  • A store that is not yet profitable should be fixed on its own first.
  • **
  • In the early stage, avoid outsourcing your advertising.
  • Consider outsourcing only after you understand what your campaigns should achieve.
  • Hire marketing agencies with caution in the early phase.
  • Without enough historic data (past sales records), an agency can spend heavily without results, so monitor their spend and results closely.
  • **
  • Working with a mentor or a team on Google Ads, Google Shopping (product ads in Google search) and budget decisions is recommended as a scaling step, not an early test.
  • This applies once the product is proven and branded.
  • When you add agencies and staff, fixed monthly costs rise, and profit can shrink before results improve.
  • Judge the return on the agency only after its work has had time to improve results.
  • Delegate ad management only once you know what good results look like and growth leaves you without time for it.

When outsourcing makes sense

2 min
  • The main real advantage of an agency is saved time.
  • It makes sense when you cannot find enough hours to manage your ads to the level you want.
  • **
  • Outsourcing ads management makes the most sense for tasks you do not enjoy.
  • Those tasks are tedious and take time away from other activities that grow the business.
  • **
  • Once a brand is established and paid ads take a lot of time, a specialist media-buying agency can take over that work.
  • Media buying means buying ad space and managing paid placements.
  • That agency manages Google and Facebook campaigns.
  • Owners of growing businesses often use this when running several projects at once.
  • When the founder is too busy with paid campaigns to work on other channels such as SEO, outsourcing full PPC management frees time for the rest of the marketing.
  • The stated aim is to scale campaigns so the business can move from five-figure to six- and seven-figure monthly revenue.
  • A one-person business spreads its time across tasks and is more likely to make errors.
  • An agency with specialists can run campaigns such as smart campaigns (Google's simpler automated campaign type), Performance Max (Google's fully automated campaign type) and retargeting (showing ads again to people who visited before) separately.
  • Experienced agencies also know account details that take years to learn.
  • At $500k-1M+ per month, an in-house media buying team should work on at most one or two platforms, such as Facebook and TikTok.
  • Less common platforms such as Pinterest, Snapchat and sometimes Google should go to agencies.
  • A consultant (a paid advisor) for a focused review can be worthwhile at small budgets.
  • Outsourcing the whole campaign management may not pay off at that size.
  • The consultant finds wasted spend and redirects it to better-performing areas.
  • A consultant who provides specific to-do items, setup feedback and campaign Q&A can save thousands of dollars in months of trial and error.
  • The consultant can bring a campaign from a six-month timeline to a two- or three-month timeline.
  • Outsourcing makes sense only after you understand what your campaigns should achieve.

When to keep it in-house

1 min
  • A freelancer (a self-employed worker) or virtual assistant (a remote helper) trained by you usually costs less than an agency.
  • This gives you a person who works full time under your direct control.
  • **
  • An agency does not automatically outperform the owner at running ads.
  • If you apply a proven high-ticket (expensive products) Google Ads approach yourself, you can get results as good as an agency would deliver.
  • **
  • Keep hands-on control of ad setup and budgets in-house.
  • Keep pay-per-click management in-house until you understand it well enough to judge an agency.

Service types and levels of involvement

1 min
Service Purpose
Coaching or consulting Strategic advice and guidance while the owner keeps running the account
Campaign tune-up Fixing and optimizing an existing campaign setup
Campaign audit Reviewing the account to find problems and gaps
Store-specific feedback Individual review of one e-commerce store's campaigns
Full PPC management Outsourcing all AdWords (Google's old name for Google Ads) and paid ad management to the agency
  • Before outsourcing paid ads, choose the service level that matches the stage.
  • Start with consulting or an audit for a new or small account.
  • Move to full management when scaling.
  • Lighter options also let the owner learn how the account works before handing over control.
  • PPC agencies may offer several levels of involvement.
  • These include answering questions about a course or community, private consulting on specific campaigns, or the agency running and scaling the campaigns in collaboration with the owner.
  • Choose the level based on how much control the owner wants to keep and how much time they can spend on the account.

Agency vetting: core competencies and specialisation

1 min
  • Define core competencies (the skills an agency is strongest at) upfront.
  • Ask what the agency specializes in.
  • Ask which systems it uses for all clients versus custom setups.
  • Ask which parts of the growth funnel (the steps from first visit to sale) it covers.
  • Beware agencies claiming expertise in everything.
  • Choose a PPC specialist who focuses only on paid search.
  • Avoid a generalist agency (one that does everything) that also offers design, CRO (conversion rate optimization, changing a store so more visitors buy), SEO, content, logos or social media.
  • Google Ads changes frequently, and a team that covers all of digital marketing is less likely to keep up.
  • When screening a PPC specialist, ask these questions in order:
    1. How many hours per week do you actually work in client accounts? Compare the answer with the survey's answer groups: 40 hours, 10 to 39 hours, 1 to 10 hours, or less than one hour per week.
    2. Do you answer emails promptly? Check this during the screening itself.
    3. Will you schedule a call with me at least once a month?
    4. Can you share your current task list, showing which experiments, ad copy tests (trying different ad text) and bid optimizations (adjusting how much you pay for ad spots) are running?
    5. Do you focus only on paid search, or also on design, CRO, SEO, content, logos or social media?

Agency vetting: team and account manager

1 min
  • The most visible person in agency sales presentations may not manage your account.
  • Clarify who your actual point of contact will be.
  • Clarify their experience level and how long they have worked for the agency.
  • Verify whether the team assigned to your account is in-house, part-time, or outsourced to contractors.
  • Ask how long each person has worked for the agency.
  • Ask whether they focus only on your account or manage multiple accounts.
  • Big-name salespeople often do not manage accounts directly.
  • Read Glassdoor (a website where employees review their employers) reviews for the agency.
  • Use them to understand team dynamics and leadership behavior.
  • Filter for patterns: whether juniors run client accounts, whether senior staff train or hand off work, and whether staff turnover is high.
  • Employee frustration hints at problems you may inherit.
  • A large agency typically gives only about 5 hours of actual management time per month to one account.
  • Any agency promising a dedicated staff member working on your account constantly is not telling the truth.
  • **
  • Agencies that use heavy software to manage many accounts at scale usually deliver lower quality than one person manually managing your account.
  • Take this into account when comparing them.
  • **

Agency vetting: position in the agency's client base

1 min
  • Verify your position among the agency's client base.
  • If you are their largest client, they pay you high attention but may lose focus if they land a bigger client.
  • If you are small, they may deprioritize (put lower on the list) your account.

Agency vetting: evaluating the agency

2 min
  • Evaluate agencies on three dimensions: output (number of deliverables, meaning agreed pieces of work), integrity (keeping the brand's message honest and consistent) and effectiveness (performance numbers in your ad account).
  • Weight each appropriately when structuring compensation.
  • Money spent by an agency is not an indicator of quality work.
  • It only shows the volume of clients served.
  • Audit other metrics, speak to current clients, read employee reviews, and test results before hiring.
  • Request conversations with 2 to 3 current agency clients who have a similar business model and spend level.
  • Case studies are handpicked and controlled.
  • Unfiltered feedback from active clients reveals actual performance, speed of implementation, and communication quality.
  • Work through this procedure before hiring an agency:
    1. Build a short list of agencies.
    2. Talk to each agency and ask about its experience with e-commerce businesses and who its clients are.
    3. Ask whether it has experience with dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships directly to customers) businesses and with Google Shopping ads.
    4. Ask the agency to explain how it manages campaigns.
    5. Compare its suggested approach with a proven high-ticket Google Ads approach. If it matches, the agency is reasonably likely to manage your account at an acceptable level.
    6. Ask the agency to review your ad account and suggest what it would change or improve.
  • The most reliable way to find a good agency is a recommendation from a trusted e-commerce operator in your network.
  • Someone who had a bad experience will not recommend that agency.
  • **
  • To find a good agency or consultant, ask the platform rep for their biggest agency spender.
  • Then talk to that client about how they work.
  • Consultancy and teaching services are often offered at a high fee.
  • Show the live ad account (the real, currently running account) when talking to an agency.
  • Ask them to beat the current ROAS.
  • Talk to several agencies before choosing one, because results and service quality vary between them.
  • Compare what each one manages and what is included in the fee before you sign.
  • Check whether the agency is reachable through a support route.
  • An agency account gives direct support contacts who reply through WhatsApp or Telegram, so response speed is a practical factor when choosing an agency.
  • A personal ad account has no such support route, which makes its loss harder to recover from.

1 min

  • Contact it only after the store makes money, since an unprofitable store may be turned away or redirected.

Screening a PPC specialist: activity and reporting

1 min
  • Activity is the strongest predictor of success when hiring a PPC manager.
  • Assess how often they actually work in the account, how they communicate, and how strong their technical expertise is.
  • In a study by the marketing firm WordStream, about 90% of PPC account managers said they spend at least one hour per week on an account.
  • In practice, most do not optimize regularly as they claim.
  • In the same WordStream study, about 20% of account managers (1 in 5) made no changes to campaigns for an entire month.
  • Only about 10% (1 in 10) worked on accounts every week across a full quarter.
  • Agencies performed slightly better than individual freelancers in the same study.
  • More time spent in an account generally correlates with better results (they tend to rise together), because ongoing optimization compounds (builds up) over time.
  • Check the specialist's activity with this procedure:
    1. Check that the specialist answers emails promptly.
    2. Check that they invite you to a call at least once a month.
    3. Check that they share task lists showing current work, such as campaign experiments, ad copy tests and bid optimization.
    4. Check that they report openly on what they changed and why.
    5. Verify their reports instead of trusting them blindly.

Red flags

2 min
  • Do not work with an agency that contacts you unsolicited (without you asking), for example one that targets everyone who opens a new Shopify (a platform for building online stores) store.
  • Such agencies usually rely on churning (signing and dropping clients quickly), which signals weak results.
  • Strong agencies often have a waiting list.
  • **
  • Ignore unsolicited emails that ask you to book a call with an agency.
  • **
  • Ignore cold emails (unrequested emails) from ad-management agencies.
  • Many find Shopify stores with lookup tools such as BuiltWith (a tool that shows what software a website uses), mass-email them, and churn clients instead of retaining them.
  • Some ad agencies find Shopify stores created in the last seven days and cold-email the owners offering ad management.
  • They often sign up many clients without improving results.
  • **
  • Before engaging such a company, search its name in an owner community group to read the experiences of other store owners.
  • Avoid agencies that sell Google Ads management via cold calls (unrequested phone calls) and run templated work across hundreds of accounts.
  • A boutique agency (a small specialist firm) that understands your business and manages your account is the better choice.
  • Avoid agencies that cold-contact every new Shopify store.
  • Their model is usually high-volume and short-term.
  • An agency charging around 200 USD per month will probably do little work on your account.
  • Or it will outsource the labour (the work) to a low-cost country where people lack significant experience in good ads management.
  • **
  • A company that cannot deliver conversions at the start is a bad sign.
  • Larger marketing agencies often push a 3-month commitment and assign senior media buyers who may not understand the brand.
  • Check the contract terms and the team that will actually work on the account before signing.
  • Avoid agencies that require a fixed contract, such as six months of payments regardless of results.
  • Reputable agencies work month to month on a pay-as-you-go basis (you pay for each month as it comes) because they are confident they will deliver.
  • **

Contracts and terms

1 min

  • Use short-term contracts and require agencies to earn a contract extension by showing results. This keeps them accountable and gives both sides clean exit points.

  • Avoid fixed contracts that lock you in no matter the results. Good agencies work month to month. **

  • Watch for 3-month commitments, and check who will actually work on your account before you sign.

  • Put the communication rules in writing before work starts. Include:

    • The channel (Slack, which is a team chat app; email; or phone),
    • how often you meet each week,
    • The response time you expect (for example, 24 business hours, meaning working hours only),
    • how often decisions get made.

    Example: Monday standup (a short team meeting), weekly Slack updates, monthly performance review. Do not assume anyone is available.

  • For a PPC agency (PPC means pay-per-click, ads you pay for each click), expect email replies within 2 to 3 hours during business hours. Put this in writing before you sign.

  • Set up communication channels before work starts. Agree on response times, meeting frequency, escalation procedures (who to contact next when something goes wrong) and backup contacts (other people to reach when the main person is not available).

  • Define the escalation path (the order of who to contact when there is a problem) and backup contacts in the contract:

    1. Primary contact (your main person).
    2. Secondary contact (for example, team lead).
    3. Manager.

    Specify what happens when your primary contact is on vacation, sick or leaves the agency. This prevents gaps in service and makes sure someone always owns your account.

  • Agree a realistic timeframe and a target uplift (an increase), such as 20 percent or 50 percent more ROAS (return on ad spend, the money earned back for each dollar spent on ads), so results can be measured.

Onboarding roadmap

1 min
  • Before signing, create a detailed 30-, 60- and 90-day roadmap (a plan with dated steps) together with the agency. Map each specific deliverable (a promised piece of work) to a specific date. Example: Day 1 (campaigns live), Week 2 (categorical search campaigns launched, meaning search ads grouped by product category), Week 3 (YouTube testing begins). This prevents vague promises and gives clear weekly checkpoints for evaluation.

Performance review and benchmarks

1 min

Baseline targets

A baseline is your starting number, the measure you compare later results against.

  • A proven 13X return on ad spend (13X means 13 times: 13 dollars back for every dollar spent) can be used as an agency brief (the written task you give them). A good brief keeps 13X while increasing spend from $1,000 to $2,000 per month, or raises return from 13X to 15X at the same spend. Without a baseline, an agency has no measurable target.
  • Keep Google Ads in-house (run by your own team, not an agency) until the account returns at least 10x ROAS.
  • Set a target uplift of 20 percent or 50 percent more ROAS, agreed with a realistic timeframe.
  • Compare an agency's period with the same months of the previous year.

Reviewing an agency before firing it

  • Review an agency or specialist before you fire them: **
    1. Ask what changes they made to scale (grow spend and results), including budget increases and campaign type.
    2. If they switched to Smart Shopping (an automatic Google ad type where Google controls most settings), ask them to go back to manual campaigns (ad campaigns you set up and control yourself).
    3. Decide afterward whether to keep or replace them.
  • Do not fire a specialist on the first day of trouble. Earlier manual work may have been what worked, so first restore the earlier setup. **

Ongoing checks

  • Judge the return on the agency only after its work has had time to improve results.
  • Monitor agency spend and results closely in the early phase, when there is little historic data (past numbers to compare with). **
  • Verify the specialist's reports instead of trusting them blindly.

Testing agencies against in-house results

1 min
  • When an agency, freelancer (a self-employed helper) or new approach claims better results, test it in a few markets (groups of customers or areas) alongside the existing campaign, and compare traced results (results you can link back to their source). This settles the question without a commitment to the new party.
  • Test procedure:
    1. Keep the current campaign running unchanged in most markets.
    2. Give the new agency or approach a few selected towns or segments.
    3. Track responses, cost per customer and sales in both groups using the same keying method (a fixed tag or code for each channel, so you know which sales came from which ad).
    4. Compare the traced results and extend the winner only when its figures are clearly better.
  • An outsourced agency or new hire must beat your own prior results from the same period, because outsourcing raises your break-even point (the sales level where you stop losing money).
  • Outsourcing Google Ads only makes sense with a stated timeframe and a commitment to beat current results. Without that baseline, a new agency's underperformance cannot be judged.
