Choosing a hiring model and sequencing hires

Section 20.5 · 5 of 26 in this chapter · 23 min read

In short

  • Know what you need before you talk to any helper company. They earn money when you say yes.
  • Hire one clear job per person, and test them with a paid trial task first.
  • Pay for helpers from store profit. Hire when the profit can cover them.
  • Pick the helper type by the job. A freelancer fits one-off tasks. A virtual assistant (VA, a remote helper) fits ongoing work.
  • Hire for the job that takes most of your time first. In most sources, that is customer service.
More key points
  • Hire one helper early. It can be part-time and do a single task.
  • Do not hire until the store makes profit on its own, and you have run it yourself for about a month.
  • Write each process down as a step-by-step guide before you hire, so a new person can follow it.
  • Each new role needs different skills. Hire for the gap you have, not for the job you enjoy least.

Start from the problem, not the provider

1 min
  • Asking someone who earns money from your purchase whether you need their service is biased. They gain from a yes, whatever you really need. Set your needs first, before you talk to any provider.
  • Define the exact problem before you choose a solution. Write down which skills are missing, which jobs need doing, and what success looks like. Do this before you contact any service provider.
  • There are five hiring models (ways to bring in help): freelancer (an independent worker, often for one job), agency (a company that does the work for you), full-time employee, part-time employee, and contractor (hired for a set project). Choose the model that fits your specific problem and your business stage, not the other way round.
  • Most outsourcing (giving work to outside people) problems come from weak hiring and weak management afterwards. Outsourcing itself is rarely the cause. A bad result usually means the wrong person was chosen or was not managed well.

Outsourcing preparation

1 min

Procedure:

  1. Pick the tasks from your 180-day plan that others could do.
  2. Write a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP, a step-by-step written guide) for each task. Write down exactly how it must be done.
  3. Write job posts for those positions.
  4. Interview candidates using the template questions provided.
  5. Test by interviewing through messages, because it is faster.
  6. Hire team members as positions are filled. Bring them into your management system (the way you track and direct work).

Hiring process

1 min

Procedure. Repeat it for every new role to keep things consistent and fast:

  1. Know who to hire: the job roles and how the organization is structured.
  2. Create and post the job, and design the filtering process (how you sort out weak applicants).
  3. Run interviews: a short call and a final call.
  4. Onboard (bring the new person into the team) systematically.

Four hiring stages, in this order:

  1. Post the job so candidates can find it.
  2. Shortlist (pick the best applicants) the applicants.
  3. Interview the shortlisted applicants.
  4. Trial the successful candidates. The trial overlaps with onboarding. It is the last point where you decide whether to keep someone long term.
  • Hire for one clear job at a time, not a generalist. One person rarely does sales, uploads, writing and support well. Test each candidate with a paid trial task and check how fast they finish it. Hire part-time first, then move to full time when results are good.
  • Breakdown example: a one-year goal of growing a customer service team by three people is split into three rocks (big goals for one 90-day period). That is one new hire per quarter (every three months). Each hiring rock is broken into tasks: write the job, create the job advertisement, post it, shortlist, interview, make the hiring decision, and provide training if needed. These tasks are spread across weekly to-do lists over the 90 days.

Agency versus in-house

1 min
  • For a business under $10 million in annual revenue (total yearly sales), a specialized agency is usually cheaper than hiring full-time specialists in-house (on your own payroll). (For reference, $10 million a year is about $833,000 per month on average. This figure is derived, not from the source.)
  • In-house senior roles in media buying (buying ads), growth strategy or paid ads cost $30,000 to $40,000 per month in extra fixed costs. Equivalent agency services cost $12,000 to $15,000 per month.
  • Example: a creative agency (a firm that makes ad content such as images or videos) charges $3,000 per month for 5 to 7 pieces of creative, or 10 creatives per month. That is often cheaper than a junior in-house designer at $5,000 per month (equal to a $60,000 annual salary).
  • Once annual revenue is above $10 million, hiring specialized in-house roles becomes worth it. The workload then justifies full-time work and better efficiency.
  • The sources differ. Source E sets the in-house break-even point (where in-house costs start to pay off) for specialist roles at $10 million annual revenue. Source C uses monthly revenue phases instead (see the revenue stages below). It moves to agencies for social platforms the store does not advertise on, at $500,000 to $1 million+ per month. Use the Source E test for specialist roles and the Source C phases for sequencing (the order of hiring).
  • Hire an agency or outside help for ongoing ad management before you build an in-house team, unless the threshold above is met.

Hourly rates

1 min
  • A VA for product research or customer service can be hired on Upwork (a freelance website), which was preferred over Fiverr (another freelance website). A VA paid around $3 to $5 per hour was described as a reasonable rate.
  • Hourly rates by phase: overseas customer service $2 to $6 per hour; project manager $8 to $12 per hour; supply chain manager (the person who handles stock and suppliers) $6 to $15 per hour; payment processing agent $8 to $12 per hour.
  • Hire only when the new person's hourly cost is lower than your own hourly value. The difference is margin (money left after costs). Example: if you are worth $20 per hour, look for help at $10 per hour.
  • Pay the best person you can afford. Low pay tends to bring low-quality results. Paying a higher rate to skilled people is usually cheaper than fixing poor work.
  • Cheap assistants can be expensive. Assistants paid around $100 per month delivered poor quality work. Freelancers on platforms such as Upwork gave better results.

Efficiency-adjusted cost

1 min

Formula:

Efficiency-adjusted hourly cost = hourly wage ÷ share of output the worker actually achieves

Example (worked out from the source figures, for illustration): $5 per hour at 20 percent efficiency costs $25 per hour of full-output work. That is more than $15 per hour at 100 percent efficiency.

Offshore versus US hiring

1 min
  • A Filipino VA working full-time at about AUD 26,000 per year, all costs included, was compared with an Australian hire of the same qualifications at about AUD 75,000 per year with on-costs (extra employer costs such as taxes and insurance). Overseas staffing is the cost-effective option for small businesses.
  • Five US staff costing about $20,000 per month can be replaced by Philippine staff costing about $5,000 to $6,000 per month. This works only with the right processes and people in place.
  • Hire all team members internationally, not from the US. Build from the Philippines, Bangladesh and South America via onlinejobs.ph (a job site for remote workers). Reasons: a lower cost base (lower costs to run), strong customer service experience in those regions (the BPO industry, where outside companies run back-office work), cultural fit, and alignment on work commitment.
  • Filipino VAs are a common choice for email, live chat and order processing. Their written English is strong, and they have experience with outsourced support work. Their accent makes them a poor fit for phone calls, where it could hurt conversion (the share of visitors who buy).
  • If the task needs knowledge of a specific culture, market or sport, such as a regional audience or a local sport, hire someone from that market. An offshore assistant can only handle basic tasks that need no deep cultural knowledge.

  • Hire remote full-time employees, not freelancers. Freelancers juggle many clients and will not invest in your systems, training and growth. Specialists and generalists

1 min

Strengths and weaknesses

  • Specialists (people expert in one skill, for example video editors) deliver high-quality output quickly. They understand their field deeply and have practiced it a lot.
  • Avoid staffing entirely with specialists. They work in silos (separate boxes that do not share work), lack versatility outside their skill set, may need larger teams to scale, and usually cannot also manage other team members.
  • Generalists (people who handle many kinds of tasks) work with agility, adapt to changing roles, are cost-effective for smaller teams, tend to have stronger leadership potential, and bring fresh ideas from outside one field.
  • A team of only generalists lacks depth in specialized outputs. Their energy and priorities can spread across many deliverables. They are vulnerable to conflicting demands unless priorities are clearly aligned.
  • Keep a healthy balance of specialists and generalists. You gain speed from deep expertise, fresh thinking from varied views, and leadership capacity for growth.
  • Hire hybrid roles, where specialists go beyond their core skill set. Example: a video editor who also handles animation and graphic design. These rare talents give the team valuable flexibility.

Avoid the Super VA myth

  • The Super VA myth is the belief that one cheap helper can do everything. Expecting one low-cost offshore worker to cover unrelated skills, such as coding, customer calls, writing and podcast editing, is a mistake. A $100,000 USD job cannot realistically be pushed onto one worker paid $400 per month.

Generalists trained on your method

  • Training a generalist assistant on your own tested ad method worked better than hiring a specialist who only claimed Facebook ads experience. Teach the assistant your tested method first. Then let them run their own variations.

  • Build a VA team through a VA agency. Hand off store building, customer support and ordering to the team. The owner supervises. Keep order fulfillment in-house as long as it is manageable.
  • A first VA works best on a set of closely related tasks. Example: customer service across phone, live chat, email, social comments and messages. Avoid one all-purpose person. A practical pattern is starting at about four hours per day and growing toward eight as the business scales.
  • Hire a VA to fill skill gaps such as supplier negotiation, customer support quality or store graphics. The assistant can cover areas where the owner lacks experience.
  • A new store's team can be built from a VA who learns the SEO guide and makes small improvements to product pages each week (2023).
  • One-off tasks such as product uploads are better handled as fixed gigs on Upwork or StoreTasker, paid per job. A dedicated VA on set hours is not needed for these.
  • A VA is especially useful for beginners in dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships for you) who feel overwhelmed. They can help build the store, do product research, and answer questions when the owner is unsure.
  • Beginners who feel overwhelmed can benefit from a VA for store building, product research, and answering questions when they are unsure. This support can speed up early progress.
  • Customer service and product research: a VA for either can be hired on Upwork.

Order-volume and margin gates

  • Hire a VA only once sales are consistent: at least 5 to 10 orders per day. Before that, the workload is usually too small to justify the cost.
  • Hire a VA once you consistently get about 5 to 10 orders per day for at least three weeks, and can no longer keep up with emails and comments. Record the process with Loom (a screen-recording tool) so the VA can start work quickly.
  • Hire a VA when your profit margin is high enough to pay for the assistant and still leave extra income. If margin cannot cover the cost, hiring will drain the business.
  • Hire a VA for order processing, email, live chat and turning visitors into shoppers only when business profits can pay for outsourcing. Also, only after you know each process yourself.
  • Do not hire virtual assistants until the store is profitable and you have run it yourself for about a month. Document each process as a playbook (a written how-to) so a new hire can take over tasks with clear instructions.
  • Handle customer questions yourself first. This shows you what people ask and how to answer. Do not hire staff or outsource work until the business's own profit pays for it.
  • Before hiring a VA, check that the business can pay their monthly wage. Do not hire someone and let them go a month later, because they depend on that income.
  • Hire a VA once the business can pay for them from profit. A typical VA salary is roughly $300 to $800 per month, which should come from store profit before you hire.
  • Outsource to a team of VAs, based in the Philippines and hired through Online Jobs PH, once the store is making sales and getting traffic (visitors). Hire to offload day-to-day tasks, and keep yourself in an oversight role (2021).
  • Start outsourcing when the store is profitable and the owner feels overwhelmed by daily work. The first hire is usually a VA.

Revenue and profit stages

3 min

Monthly net profit thresholds

Net profit means the money left after all costs. A threshold is the number you must reach before you hire.

  • The first hire should be customer service. Make it once the store nets $2,000 of profit per month. Hiring is tied to actual monthly net profit.
  • The second hire should be a person to upload and optimize products (make them easier to find and buy) and fix existing pages. Make it once the store nets $3,500 of profit per month.
  • Outsource marketing once the store nets $5,000 of profit per month. This is the third stage, after customer service and product upload.
  • Follow profit stages, not the hope of a successful launch that makes many hires possible. Tying each role to monthly net profit keeps the team paid from real earnings.

Procedure:

  1. Draw the long-term organization of the company as a flowchart or on paper.
  2. List the roles you want in the future.
  3. Assign each role a monthly net profit threshold.
  4. Hire each role only when its threshold is reached, in order.

  • Below $20k per month, the only priority is traction (early signs that customers are coming). Make only one or two essential hires, to free the owner's time for growth. Those hires should take over work that does not need the owner.
  • Hire the first media buyer (the person who buys ads) in phase 2 ($20-100k per month). Do this once customer service, influencer outreach and project management are covered. At that point, the owner is mainly left with paid ads.
  • Media buyers usually match their price. A cheaply hired entry-level buyer needs training time before performing well. Budget that training time, or pay for a more experienced media buyer. Upwork is named as a source.
  • Above $100k per month, new problems appear that the owner did not expect earlier. More specialist roles are needed to keep the store running at that scale.

Three stages by revenue or profit

Build a VA team in three stages, matched to revenue:

  • Beginners (roughly $5,000 to $50,000 monthly revenue) keep store building and product descriptions partly in their own hands.

  • Advanced sellers (roughly $5,000 to $20,000 monthly profit) delegate order fulfillment and customer support first.

  • Business owners (around $20,000 plus monthly profit) run the business almost fully on autopilot. A team handles fulfillment, support, live chat, finances and ad management.

  • The thresholds differ. Source C phases are set by monthly revenue, with breakpoints at $20,000 and $100,000. Source A's thresholds are set by monthly net profit: $2,000, $3,500 and $5,000. Use profit thresholds to decide whether a hire is affordable. Use revenue phases to anticipate which roles the business will need.

Build the team when the need appears

  • Build a team when you realise you need other people. Identify the skills the business needs, then search for people who have them. This is one of the first signs of real business growth.
  • As the business grows, you can build a team by hiring employees or VAs to take over work. At the beginning, the owner does most of the work themselves.
  • Low-ticket dropshipping (cheap products) at scale needs a large team of VAs and freelancers to handle orders, shipping follow-up and customer service. High-ticket dropshipping (expensive products) needs far fewer orders, and so far fewer staff.

Sequence from the sources

  1. Start with product listing specialists. These are people who write and set up product pages in the shop. They make unlimited brands possible, because you manage the suppliers (the companies that make or stock your products).
  2. Add a supplier relations specialist. This person handles supplier outreach (contacting suppliers to start work together) and partnerships.
  3. Add a customer service team. Give it pre-purchase roles (questions before a buyer orders) and post-purchase roles (problems after an order).
  4. Add an operations manager. This person oversees fulfillment (packing and shipping orders). Operations is the biggest team, in the middle of the organization.
  5. Add a bookkeeper. This person records money in and out, tracks costs, reconciles (checks that records match the bank), and finds unprofitable orders.
  6. Add advertising, content and email specialists.
  7. Build each store with the same structure.

Case: team build-up timeline

1 min

Store team timeline:

  • 2014: A dropshipping store started on a different model. The owner learned on their own. They saw it could not reach financial independence (living on the business alone).
  • 2016: The owner switched to the Source A method, his named approach to dropshipping. They still worked a corporate job. They handled orders and customer calls in early mornings, nights and breaks.
  • 2017: A part-time phone helper was hired and paid per answered call. The owner still worked the full-time job and got burnt out (completely exhausted).
  • 2018: A full-time virtual assistant was hired for operations. The owner left the corporate job.
  • 2020: The team grew to three, then four. This included a US hire with experience and a second US hire. Revenue (total sales money) reached $1.4 million. The team handled operations and customer service. It also used an outsourced social media, ads and accounting function.
  • 2021: A fourth virtual assistant was hired. The team handled all day-to-day operations (the daily running of the shop) and escalations (problems passed up to a higher person). So the owner only did sales, some marketing and accounting, from anywhere.

Affordability check before each hire

1 min
  • Do not hire virtual assistants until the store is profitable (earning more than it costs) and you have run it yourself for about a month.
  • Tie each hire to the profit it can be paid from. Do not hire until the business's own profit pays for it.

What to hire first

1 min
  • Start with the job that takes the most of your time and needs no judgment (no decision-making). In most sources, that is customer communication.
  • When the business needs a skill you do not have, hire for that gap. Examples are supplier negotiation (agreeing prices and terms with suppliers), customer support quality, or store graphics (the look of your shop's pictures and design).
  • Before the first hire, document each process as a playbook (a written step-by-step guide). Then a new hire can take over tasks with clear instructions.
  • Hire a VA for order fulfillment and support first. Then hire a person to build stores. Then hire one to run ads. Each role needs different skills.
  • Test content yourself first. Hand it off once you know what works.
  • Hire the first helper quickly, even part-time, and for a single task.
  • Hiring for advertising and content roles takes longer. Build your own email marketer (a person who writes and sends marketing emails) inside your team, and train them.
  • When a media buyer (a person who buys and manages paid ads) is needed, the first one should come in phase 2 of the revenue stages. That is after customer service, influencer outreach and project management (planning and tracking the team's work) are covered.
