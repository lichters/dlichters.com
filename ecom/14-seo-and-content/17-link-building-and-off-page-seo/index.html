Link building and off-Page SEO

Section 14.17 · 17 of 20 in this chapter · 60 min read

In short

  • Backlinks are links from other websites to yours. Google reads each one as a vote of trust.
  • Product and collection pages rarely earn links. Write useful guide pages first, then ask other sites to link to them.
  • Only real links count. Buying or faking links can get your site punished by Google.
  • Finish one full skyscraper project first. Then repeat it.
  • Only contact sites on your topic that already link to useful pages.
More key points
  • Send one email per site, then follow up. Never spam one person.
  • Find a real person's email, not a sales inbox. Keep records of who you contacted and when.
  • For broken link building, point out a dead link and offer your own page as the replacement.
  • Never buy links. Google can lower your rankings for it.
  • A backlink is a link from another website to yours. Links from strong, related sites matter more than many links from weak sites.
  • Domain authority is a score from 1 to 100 that estimates how strong a website is. Check it over time and aim for steady growth.
  • Paying for links is risky. Pay only when the price is low, the site is good, and the result is worth it. Never use link farms.
  • The safest ways to earn links are guest posts, resource pages, and listings on supplier sites. Follow up with each site.
  • For prospects, keep only domains with 10 or more backlinks and a Domain Rating from 10 to 75.
  • Keep every outreach email short, polite, and on topic: one request, one clear question.
  • Send one email at a time to each site. You do not need a bulk tool.
  • If someone is rude, do not reply. Remove them from the list and mark them do-not-contact.
  • If someone says a polite no, answer politely. The door may open later.
  • Never buy links or trade links back and forth. Guest posts are the safe route.

Backlink Basics and Why Links Matter

3 min
  • Backlinks are links from other websites to yours. Google reads each one as a vote of trust. Websites with more backlinks usually rank higher.
  • Working with influencers (people with large online audiences) creates backlinks. They link to your store in their articles, which helps your search rankings.
  • Off-page SEO (search engine optimization done away from your own website) covers actions that make Google see your site as trustworthy and authoritative. Examples are building backlinks, earning branded searches (people typing your store name into Google), and getting engagement and shares on social media.
  • ** A 2020 study of ranking factors (things Google uses to order results) found a clear link between total backlinks and Google rankings. When web addresses (URLs) with zero backlinks were left out, top-ranking pages had more backlinks than lower-ranking pages.
  • ** Google has said it still uses PageRank (Google's original link-scoring system) alongside many other signals it does not publish. Links are one part of off-page SEO, not the whole.
  • ** Google's Quality Rater Guidelines (the rulebook for the people who check search quality) rely heavily on off-site reputation. Reputation is checked through online reviews, expert recommendations, news articles and awards. Many positive user reviews count as evidence of a good reputation.
  • ** Google has publicly named three main ranking priorities: content, external links and RankBrain (Google's AI system for understanding searches). External links are one of the few ranking factors Google has clearly confirmed. This statement dates from around 2020.
  • ** Google has officially confirmed only these three organic (unpaid) ranking signals. Other factors are known mainly through testing, so treat any specific claim about them as a hypothesis (an idea to check).
  • ** Google treats backlinks as social proof (a sign that others trust something). Links from other sites signal that content is good and the site is trustworthy. Sites with weak content rarely attract them. Backlinks also help Google place a site in its wider context.
  • ** Google values backlinks as a core signal for understanding the web. If any one of the three core signals is missing, strong on-page SEO (work on the page itself) and technical SEO (work on site setup and structure) will not earn significant rankings.
  • A site can earn some organic traffic (free visits from search results) without links, through technical SEO, content and on-page work. Reaching high organic traffic with no external links is very hard. Google uses links as third-party votes (votes from other sites) and combines them with on-page quality signals.
  • When competing pages have similar content quality, the page with more external links is usually harder to outrank. Links act as a tie-breaker (something that decides between equals) that good content alone cannot provide.
  • Google also judges on-page quality. Pages that look dodgy (shady or untrustworthy) or carry spammy links (junk links) are judged negatively. Clean external links from relevant sites support page quality, not just link count.
  • A backlink passes most of its authority (ranking strength) to the exact page it points to, not to the whole domain (your whole website address). Links on that page to other pages on your site pass only part of that authority onward.
  • Social media shares are nofollow (links that tell Google not to pass ranking credit). They do not raise rankings directly. They still help the trust signals of the site, so they are worth doing.
  • ** Once a page reaches the top of the results for its target term (the main search phrase you aim for), the goal shifts to holding that position. Watch whether competing pages keep adding backlinks and whether you add enough quality links to stay ahead.
  • ** The E-commerce Skyscraper Method (a method that builds a better version of popular pages, then asks the sites linking to them to link to yours instead) is the main link-building approach. Smaller supplementary methods can be added alongside it to find more opportunities, but they do not replace it.
  • ** Backlink outreach (asking other site owners for links) counts as link building only when all links are genuine. Black hat tactics (cheating methods) such as link farms (networks of fake sites that link to each other) are recognizable to Google and carry a risk of penalties (Google punishing the site).

Measuring Authority and Setting Link Targets

1 min
  • Domain Authority Checker is a free tool that assesses a website's authority (its trust score) for SEO analysis.
  • ** The number of backlinks needed depends on what competing pages already have, not on a fixed target. The goal is more or better-quality backlinks than the direct competitors ranking for the same search term, rather than the most links in the whole market.
  • ** The benchmarks (reference numbers) below depend on how strong the competition is. Use each one where it fits:
    • Weak competition: if the top-ranking competitors have about five backlinks, a page usually needs roughly 5 to 10 quality backlinks, provided its own content is better than theirs. This is a rough benchmark, not a guaranteed result.
    • Competitive niche (a narrow market with strong rivals): the average top-10 page (the ten results on page one) for "surfboards" had 14 backlinks and a domain score of 54. For "wetsuits" the average top-10 page had 33 backlinks and a domain score of 72. These show how many links a page needs to compete.
    • No fixed number: there is no fixed backlink number a top result must have. Look for ranking pages with hundreds of backlinks or high-authority linking sites, such as those rated in the 80s or 90s. Accept lower numbers in very small niches.

Why E-commerce Pages Struggle to Earn Links

2 min
  • E-commerce sites (online shops) find it hard to earn many inbound links (links coming in from other sites). Their core pages are product and collection pages (pages that sell one item or a group of items), which are commercial in nature (they exist to sell). Most websites do not want to link to them unless they gain something, because such a link gives their audience little value.
  • Resellers (stores that sell products they did not make) face an even harder time than brands selling their own products. A truly unique, self-made product can make commercial pages easier to link to.
  • Websites are generally unwilling to link to a commercial page for nothing in return. Link outreach (contacting sites to ask for links) to product or collection pages therefore needs an offer that gives the linking site something of value.
  • ** Pursuing backlinks to product or collection pages is usually a poor use of outreach effort, because most website owners prefer to link to informational content (pages that teach or inform). A store that wants links needs content worth linking to before starting outreach.
  • ** Content pages such as guides and articles are the preferred targets for backlinks, because other site owners rarely link to product or collection pages. Build outreach around content that is created first.
  • Content pages attract links more readily than product pages. But content type and structure strongly affect how many links a page earns. Choose content by researching which formats are most linked to in the niche (your narrow market).
  • Before producing link-attracting content, research which content types are most linked to in the niche, and produce those alongside the other content the store needs. Targeting and research are part of the link strategy, not an afterthought.
  • Product or collection links can still matter. Building links to product pages and collections that sit on page two of Google (results 11 to 20) can move them up. It takes a few months before the effect shows. Then organic sales replace part of the margin (profit left per sale) lost to higher costs.
  • ** Sources differ on product-page links. Use the page-two approach from only where a product or collection page is close to page one and the outreach offers the linking site something of value. Otherwise follow and focus on content.
  • Affiliate link arrangements (deals where you earn a commission on sales that come through links) are a separate topic. They are set aside when analyzing why commercial pages struggle to earn links, and the outreach method for e-commerce stores does not cover them.

Choosing Content Roles and Projects

1 min
  • Not every content piece (any article, guide or page you publish) needs a backlink campaign. Content serves one of three roles: ranking for target keywords (search phrases you aim for), earning backlinks, or helping existing customers on the site. Only pieces meant to earn links need to go through a link-building process.
  • Build backlinks to content pieces wherever it makes sense for that piece.
  • ** A skyscraper project (a project that builds a better version of a popular piece and invites links to it) can be run on any suitable high-quality piece, which speeds up link acquisition (getting links). It is optional for each piece, so a monthly publishing plan does not require a skyscraper process for every item.
  • ** Skyscraper content is built to attract large numbers of backlinks. It is not needed in every piece and should not be the starting point of a beginner's plan.
  • ** The practical minimum is one completed skyscraper project, run through the full process including link negotiation (agreeing a link with another site), so that the workflow is learned and some links are built.
  • ** The skyscraper-style method aims for a very low cost and time investment per link. It is cheaper per link, per piece of content and per outreach than most other online link building. It makes up for lower effort per link with much higher outreach volume (number of messages sent).
  • ** Only earn genuine links from real websites. Never buy links or build unnatural ones. This white hat approach (clean, rule-following methods) keeps the risk of a Google penalty or algorithm-related loss very low.

Operating Sequence

1 min
  1. Identify one piece of content to run a skyscraper project on. If no suitable content exists, choose a topic that fits the niche and create the content first.
  2. Work through the skyscraper project step by step, including the link negotiation process.
  3. Repeat the process on an ongoing basis once the first project is complete. Review which parts of the work can be outsourced (given to someone else).
  4. Research opportunities in your niche for resource page link building (getting links from pages that list useful resources) and broken link building (finding dead links on other sites and offering your page as a replacement).
  • ** Keyword research (finding the search phrases people type) is the first step before creating content and running outreach. The skyscraper-style method starts there.
  • ** Link building is still partly manual and time consuming, so the owner should not keep spending large amounts of their own time on it for months or years.

Topic Research for Link-Attracting Content

6 min

  • ** For link-focused content, keyword research is really topic research. The goal is to find topics that already attract many backlinks, not keywords to place inside the text. The chosen topic should be one that other sites have linked to repeatedly.

  • ** Choose keywords relevant to your niche or product category, even if they are not directly about your products. Focus on informational queries (searches for answers), such as how-to or what-does-this-mean questions.

  • ** Avoid commercial keywords (buying intent, meaning the searcher wants to buy, such as "where can I buy") and navigational keywords (searches to reach one place or site, such as "near me" or a specific park or brand). Even when their keyword difficulty is very high, they do not produce linkable content.

  • ** Use broad keywords for this method, the opposite of typical SEO writing that targets narrow terms. Broad topics appeal to a wider audience and attract more links. The final article topic must still be specific enough to write one complete piece about.

  • ** Keyword difficulty (KD, a score for how hard a keyword is to rank for) in Ahrefs, Ubersuggest and similar tools (SEO software) estimates how many linking domains (other websites that link) a page needs to reach the top 10 organic results. In Ahrefs it is a weighted average of the linking domains of the current top-ranking pages, shown on a logarithmic scale (a scale that squeezes large numbers) from 0 to 100. A higher KD means the top pages already have many backlinks, which is what this method looks for.

  • ** Target informational keywords with a keyword difficulty above 10 as the ideal. Values such as 15, 16, 20 or 30 are all good. In very small or specific niches, KD values somewhat below 10 can still produce usable link targets.

  • ** When a KD is slightly below 10 but several ranking articles have decent backlink counts, the topic is still worth pursuing. Borderline values work as long as enough linking sites can be targeted.

  • ** Search volume (how often a term is searched) and clicks are not important for this method, because the purpose is to earn links rather than traffic for that exact term. Judge candidates by KD, search intent (what the searcher wants) and the backlink profile (the list of links) of the ranking pages.

  • ** Keyword and topic research workflow for the skyscraper method (Ahrefs Keywords Explorer, a keyword search tool, with Google and United States selected):

    1. Open the keyword tool and select Google with the United States as the country.
    2. Enter the broadest term that describes your niche, for example the main product category, and run the search.
    3. Read the overview for keyword difficulty, search volume and CPC (what advertisers pay per click).
    4. Open the Questions report to see question-style searches around the term.
    5. Keep only informational queries and remove commercial and navigational ones, even if their KD is high.
    6. Keep candidates with KD above 10, accepting somewhat lower values in very small niches.
    7. Open the SERP overview of each candidate (SERP means the search results page) and check the top results. They should be mostly informational articles with many backlinks, not store, government or navigational pages.
    8. Ignore Wikipedia results when judging backlink strength, because they collect links for being Wikipedia.
    9. If the broad term is too general for one article, return to the question variations and choose a more specific candidate.
    10. Record each promising topic and its top-ranking competitor URL (web address) in the research spreadsheet.
    11. Use the Having same terms report to find secondary keywords close to the primary one (the main keyword) and record them.
    12. Choose the topic that has the strongest set of linking sites and a clear article idea you can make better than the current top result.

  • ** Example: the broad term "camping" in the United States had a KD of 32, a search volume of about 127K (how often it is searched) and a CPC of $0.80. Ahrefs estimated that backlinks from about 40 websites were needed to rank in the top 10, but the term was too broad to write one specific article about.

  • ** Example: question-style variations (searches phrased as questions) from the same niche had lower KD values. "What is dispersed camping" had a KD of 17, and "how to cook while camping" had a KD of 26. Both returned mostly informational articles with a good number of backlinks, making them suitable candidates.

  • ** Keyword difficulty labels (word ratings such as Easy or Hard) and estimated linking websites needed for the top 10 (Ahrefs, United States):

    Example keyword Keyword difficulty Ahrefs label Estimated linking websites needed for top 10
    camping 32 Hard ~40
    how to cook while camping 26 Medium ~30
    how to start a campfire low Easy ~8

  • ** Example: "how to start a campfire" had a low KD and an estimated need of about 8 linking websites, while its top pages still carried many backlinks from individual sites. Because the query had enough linked articles beyond Wikipedia, it was treated as a viable topic (a topic worth doing) despite the low difficulty.

  • ** Example of the same topic: for the informational keyword "how to start a campfire" in the United States, Ahrefs showed a search volume of about 1.6K, a CPC of $1.60 and an Easy label with about 8 linking websites needed for the top 10. Its broader parent topic "how to start a fire" had a search volume of about 5.5K.

  • ** To find secondary keywords (close variations of your main phrase), open the Having same terms report for the primary keyword and record the similar search terms that fit the same article. Sites that link to those variations are also likely to link to your article.

  • ** To validate an existing topic idea, enter it into the keyword tool, check its KD and SERP overview, and look at the ranking articles and their backlinks. If several informational articles have decent backlink counts, add the idea to the outreach list (your list of sites to contact) as a content piece for this method.

  • ** Build the article so it is at least as good as the current top-ranking page, for example as a more complete guide that adds extra items or sections. Better, more useful content is what attracts links away from existing pages.

  • ** Choosing a high-KD navigational keyword, such as a "near me" search, wastes effort, because no informational article can satisfy that intent. Always check the search intent in the Questions and SERP results before committing to a topic.

  • ** Counting Wikipedia pages as link targets inflates the apparent backlink strength of a SERP. Wikipedia accumulates links for its own reasons, so focus on other websites in the results.

  • ** Record topics in a research spreadsheet with this layout:

    Competition URL Keyword/Topic Secondary Keywords
    URL of the top-ranking competitor page Primary keyword or topic Similar search terms from the Having same terms report

    Example row: https://www.chowhound.com/food-news/204186/camping-food-guide-ideas/ | how to cook while camping | how to cook biscuits while camping; how to cook baked potatoes while camping; how to cook food while camping

  • ** The backlinks of current top-ranking articles reveal the sites that already link to this kind of content. These are the likely linkers to target in outreach for a new, better article on the same topic.

Building Link-Attracting Content

4 min

  • ** Content built to attract backlinks for an e-commerce store must contain no commercial angle (no sales pitch). It should not recommend buying specific products from the store, promote the store's products as better than others, or imply a buy call-to-action (a prompt to buy).

  • ** The main goal of this content is to earn links, not to rank for keywords or serve buyers directly. Keywords and customer relevance appear naturally, but they do not decide the topic or purpose.

  • ** Middle-of-funnel content (content for shoppers in the middle of the path from first interest to purchase) that compares the store's own products or recommends the best product in a category is excluded from link-attracting content. That content should still be produced for customers, but it cannot be used for outreach.

  • ** Many websites refuse to link to overtly commercial pages, and link outreach works on volume. Promoting commercial content to many potential linkers wastes outreach effort.

  • ** The best topics for this method have broad appeal and sit at the top of the funnel (the first stage, when people are just learning about a subject). The wider the audience a topic reaches, the more linkers it attracts.

  • ** Only four content types are used for this method: ultimate guides, how-to posts, list posts (listicles), and infographics. Research into non-homepage pages shows these attract the most links.

  • ** An ultimate guide (a very large, very detailed page that delivers a lot of value on one niche topic) should cover a subject related to the store's niche, not the store's own product or buying process. A cornerstone page (a main, central page on your site) can be promoted this way if it fits the ultimate guide format.

  • ** A how-to post explains how to do one specific thing, usually by fixing a common problem people have in the niche. It can be less detailed than an ultimate guide but is still highly linkable (easy to earn links to). A how-to can also be expanded into an ultimate guide on the same subject.

  • ** A list post, also called a listicle (an article built as a numbered list), presents a numbered set of items, such as "the top 12 places" for a niche topic. Longer and more detailed lists attract more links. List posts are among the easiest of the four types to produce.

  • ** An infographic (a visual picture with facts and graphics) is visual content that people embed (place) in their own articles with a source credit, which creates a backlink. Existing articles can be converted into infographics and shared. Even if the infographic is not promoted, it can serve as the source for a skyscraper outreach campaign that earns a link back to the site.

  • ** Title formula for how-to pages:

    How to [fix or do a specific thing] [a common problem in the niche]

    It works because people searching for a concrete fix tend to link to the page that solves that exact problem. Examples in the same pattern: How to Stop Dog Harness Chafing Under the Front Legs; How to Keep a Camping Stove Flame Steady in Wind.

  • ** Any skyscraper content must be at least as good as the content ranking on page one of Google for the same topic. Thin sections (parts with too little detail), such as a few lines per list item, do not meet this standard.

  • ** Each piece of link-attracting content should go deeper on its subject than any existing page has gone. Depth of coverage is the main quality measure for these pages.

  • ** Before creating new link-attracting pages, audit the existing site (check it) for content that already matches one of the four formats and can be promoted as it is. Commission (order) new pieces only in the formats the site is missing.

  • ** Check whether these four formats attract the most links rather than assuming it. Look up which non-homepage pages in a specific niche have the most backlinks and note their formats before choosing one.

  • ** For a camping-gear store, an ultimate guide to choosing tent stakes for different soil types, or a directory of campsites by US state, fits the ultimate guide format. A guide to buying the store's own tents does not, because it is commercial.

  • ** Original statistics (numbers you collected yourself) attract links. People link to pages to cite specific statistics on them, not only because the content is high quality. Publishing original statistics or a data list can attract backlinks automatically.

  • ** Case (a real example): the author wanted more backlinks and suspected that people link to pages that cite specific statistics, not only to high-quality writing. A list of 80 email marketing statistics for 2019 was built at considerable time and money cost and published as a page. The first backlink arrived a few days later, which supported the hypothesis that original statistics attract links automatically.

  • ** Whenever content links out to or uses material from another source, such as quotes, graphs or research data, the original source must be credited on the page with a backlink. Then email the source to let them know.

Prospecting Linkers

2 min

  • ** Only contact likely linkers. A likely linker is a site that is likely to link to you. It sits in your broad industry category or niche (your specific topic area). It already publishes resource or content pages with outgoing links (links that point to other sites). Outreach means emailing site owners to ask for a link. Sites outside your niche are not worth outreach, even if they have broken links.

  • ** Build a list from backlink data. A backlink is a link from another site to yours. Backlink data is a file from an SEO tool (software that tracks search results and links) that lists these links. A domain is the main web address of a site, such as example.com. The referring page is the page that links out. The link URL is the page on your site or a competitor's site that it links to. You need both to know which domains to contact.

  • ** Expect thousands of pages. A likely linker list can contain thousands of referring pages. So an automated email search can produce a large pool of outreach contacts from one list.

  • ** Use a spreadsheet with these columns. A likely linker spreadsheet built from a backlink export (a file downloaded from the SEO tool) uses these columns:

    Column
    Total Backlinks
    Domain Rating
    URL Rating (of referring domain)
    Referring Domain
    Referring Page URL
    Referring Page Title
    Internal Links
    External Links
    Link URL
    TextPre
    Link Anchor
    TextPost
    Type

    The Referring Page URL and Link URL columns are the two inputs needed later to match contacts to each linking site.

  • ** Learn what the column names mean. Domain Rating is a score for how strong a whole site is. URL Rating is the same kind of score for one page. Internal Links point to other pages on the same site. External Links point to other sites. TextPre and TextPost are the words just before and after the link. The Link Anchor is the clickable text. Type is the kind of link.

  • ** Run Check My Links on every relevant page. Check My Links is a Chrome browser add-on (a small program added to the browser) that tests each link on a page. While doing skyscraper research (a way to find link targets) or reviewing resource pages, run it on every relevant page you visit. Add any invalid link you find to your outreach list, because you can offer the site owner a fix before you ask for a backlink.

  • ** Keep each batch on one topic. An outreach batch is one group of emails sent together. Each batch should contain only prospects whose content is relevant to your article's topic. Pair each prospect with the URL (web address) of the article you want them to link to, so the recipient can read it from the email.

  • ** Expect contacts for about half. Contact details can usually be found for about half of the likely linking domains, not for every one. Plan outreach lists with that loss in mind.

  • ** Do not search each site by hand. Searching each website by hand for contact emails is very slow and often fruitless, because many domains do not publish email addresses on their site.

Finding and Choosing Contact Emails

3 min

  • ** Use Hunter.io to find emails. Hunter.io is a tool that finds email addresses for websites. Sites where no email address is found are recorded in the No Email Database (a list of sites with no address yet), since other methods may find an address for them later.

  • ** Find first names too. Contact first names can be found with Hunter.io and with the Norbert tool, which can also find names linked to email addresses. Paste Hunter results into the Hunter Output tab (one tab in your spreadsheet) of the email filtering sheet. Where no name is found, leave the Name cell (the box for a name) blank. Then the Mailshake fallback text (a backup line from Mailshake, an email sending tool) is shown instead.

  • ** Expect about 50 percent. In one example, 50 of 78 requested domains returned addresses. A success rate of around 50 percent of requested domains is considered an average result.

  • ** Send only one email per domain. Repeated emails to the same company look spammy and raise the chance of spam filters (mail systems that move unwanted emails to junk).

  • ** Choose a real person over a sales inbox. For a content or link request, choose an editorial contact (the person who picks articles) or a named contact over a sales or advertising inbox. Do this even when the sales address has a slightly higher confidence score (the tool's sureness that the address is correct). The request is unlikely to be answered there.

  • ** Prefer named addresses. Personal, named addresses are generally more likely to get a reply than generic addresses such as info, admin or customer service. Prefer a named person when the score is comparable.

  • ** Use first names when you have them. Use first names where available to personalize outreach emails. Names are not found for every address, especially for generic inboxes, so leave those without a name.

  • ** Choose the final address by hand. Choosing the final address for each domain is a manual judgment task (a job that needs a person's decision) that takes time on large lists. It is the step that cannot be left to automation (a machine doing it alone).

  • ** Keep one address per domain and move it into the template. Steps:

    1. Open the Hunter.io CSV (a plain table file exported from Hunter.io; CSV is short for comma-separated values) and review every address listed for each domain.
    2. Keep one address per domain, using judgment that combines the confidence score with the best fit for the outreach purpose.
    3. Delete all other rows.
    4. Copy the remaining domain names into the Domain name column of the Hunter Output tab.
    5. Copy the remaining email addresses into the Email Address column in exactly the same order.
    6. Copy the first names into the Name column, in the same order.
    7. Check that the domain, email and name entries line up row by row.
    8. Check the main sheet, where each email and name should now match its referring page root domain (the main part of a web address, such as example.com).
    9. When finished, remove the URLs from columns A and D. The remaining columns refresh automatically.

  • ** Know how root domains are calculated. In the email filtering sheet, each Root Domain cell is calculated from its URL by a formula (a spreadsheet calculation). The domain starts after the protocol (the "https://" part), skipping a leading www. when present, and ends at the first slash. After the batch is built, paste only values into the outreach batch sheet. Values are plain results, not formulas. Formulas stop working once they are copied there.

  • ** Let the outreach tool send in bulk. For skyscraper link-building outreach, bulk sending (sending many emails at once) and automated follow-ups are handled by the outreach tool. The sending mailbox must have good deliverability (good chances that emails reach the inbox) first, because follow-ups and campaign results (one outreach project's outcome) depend on it.

Broken Link Building

4 min

  • ** Fix their dead link for them. Broken link building (finding dead links on other sites and offering a replacement) works by helping the owner of another website. A broken link is a link that no longer works. You find a dead link on their page, tell them about it, and offer one of your own relevant pages as the replacement. Because you solve a problem for them first, the backlink request is easy to accept.

  • ** Look at older content. Dead links that return HTTP 404 errors (the "page not found" message) are common on older content, often around two years old. Owners stop updating resource lists, linked pages get deleted, or linked businesses close. Brand-new articles rarely contain broken links, so focus the search on older content and resource pages on sites in your niche.

  • ** Do not assume a site is clean. Do not assume a site has no broken links just because its newest article is clean. Older content is where broken links cluster (gather), so check older articles and resource lists as well.

  • ** Skip maintained sites. A site with many valid redirecting links (links that send visitors on to a working page) and no invalid links is being maintained. The owner updates links when they expire. Such a site is unlikely to offer a broken link opportunity, so move on to the next site.

  • ** Check only in-content links. On content pages, check only in-content links, the links inside the article body. Navigation and sidebar links (menu and side-column links) are not relevant for outreach. In-content links on a fresh article usually come back green (show as working), so check older articles first.

  • ** Have a replacement page ready. Before outreach, make sure your site has a suitable page to receive the link, such as a content page that covers the same topic. Without a good replacement page you have nothing useful to offer the site owner.

  • ** Match the target page to the broken link. Choose the target page on your site to match the broken link. Use your homepage for general links, and a content page or your blog collection (a page listing your articles) for topic-specific links. A product page can work in some cases, but only when it is a sensible replacement for the product being linked.

  • ** Affiliate links need your own program. Affiliate links (links that earn a cut of sales through an affiliate program, a referral deal) are acceptable. But your own site must run an affiliate program before you ask for a link that is part of an affiliate deal. The site owner may then need to negotiate placement. If a site links to a product page for a product it no longer sells, the link is likely broken and outreach is still worth it.

  • ** Example of a well-kept site. On an Australian home-building site (a company's website in Australia), a resource page with 111 links showed 94 valid, 17 valid redirecting and 0 invalid links. The owner was keeping it current, so it was not a good outreach target. A recent article on the same site with 177 links showed 89 valid, 10 valid redirecting and 0 invalid links, which fits the pattern that new content rarely has broken links.

  • ** Follow the broken link workflow. Broken link outreach workflow:

    1. Install the Check My Links Chrome extension from the Chrome Web Store (Google's shop for browser add-ons).
    2. While browsing relevant sites in your niche, open a resource page or an older content page.
    3. Click the extension and let it run. The counter shows the links still in the queue (waiting to be checked).
    4. Read the results panel: Links, Valid links, Valid redirecting links, Warnings and Invalid links.
    5. Look at the page itself. The links highlighted red are the invalid ones.
    6. Keep only in-content links and confirm each broken link fits your niche and your replacement page.
    7. Choose the replacement page on your site: homepage, content page, blog collection or, in some cases, a product page.
    8. Find the site owner's email address on the site. If none is usable, search for it with hunter.io.
    9. Send the outreach email: point out the broken link, offer your page as a replacement, and make clear there is no pressure if they decline.
    10. Send follow-up emails until you get a reply or conclude there will be none.

  • ** Combine three methods. Broken link building works best combined with resource page link building (getting links from pages that list useful links) and the skyscraper method. Each tactic adds only a few links per month. Together they build up domain authority (how much trust search engines give a site) and link juice (the ranking power a link passes on) over time.

The old, established article still received research traffic from customers evaluating products. This made the backlink valuable for search ranking.

  • ** Offer a guest post if they decline. If a site declines a link, offer a guest post on their site as a second option. A guest post usually allows a link back to your site. But you must write the article or outsource it (pay someone else to write it).

Paid Links and Agencies

Backlink Basics

1 min
  • A backlink is a clickable link on another website. It leads to your store's homepage or any other page of your store.
  • Backlink quality depends on the authority of the linking site. Authority means how trusted and strong that site is.
  • One link from a strong, relevant site can give a bigger boost than hundreds or thousands of links from weak sites.
  • A healthy link profile (the full mix of links pointing to your site) uses several link sources. The links are not equal in value.
  • Social profiles on YouTube, Facebook, Instagram and Pinterest that link to the store feed into domain authority. Domain authority is a score that estimates how strong a website is in search.
  • Posts by other people that share product pages or blog posts with a link back to the store count too.
  • Search engine friendliness is a third factor. It means how well your site is optimized on its own pages (on-page SEO). SEO means making a site easy for search engines to read and rank. It feeds into domain authority alongside backlinks and social signals.
  • In a Backlinko (an SEO blog) and Ahrefs (an SEO tool) analysis, the number one Google result had 3.8 times more backlinks than positions 2 to 10.
  • Page authority is a third-party score, made by an outside tool, such as the one Ahrefs publishes. It estimates a page's strength.
  • Google does not use page authority. The closest Google idea is PageRank, Google's own link-based ranking system.
  • Domain Authority is also a third-party score, not a Google one.
  • Organic backlinks (links people give you without you asking) usually point to the home page.
  • Proactive link building (you go out and ask for links) should target content pages and cornerstone pages (your most important, detailed pages).
  • As a store grows, its home page collects most of the backlinks. That link value reaches other pages through the main menu.

Domain Authority

1 min
  • Domain authority is a score from 1 to 100. It is built from on-site optimization (work on your own pages) and from external links (links from other sites).
  • A higher score improves the chances of ranking well. Ranking means appearing high in Google results. This matters most for competitive keywords (search terms many stores fight over).
  • Broad, competitive keywords need a high domain authority.
  • Niche keywords (narrow, specific search terms), collection-style keywords (searches for a category page), most product pages and some blog posts need much less. Competition for them is lower.
  • Track domain authority over time. Aim for steady upward movement as more websites link to the store.
  • Check it periodically, not just once.
  • Run competitor sites (stores selling to the same buyers) through the same tool. This shows which domain authority level to aim for when targeting broad keywords in your niche.

Steps to check a domain's authority

  1. Enter the domain (your website's address) into Moz.com's domain analysis tool.
  2. Click check authority.

Price ranges

  • Paid link offers range from about $10 to $1,000 per link.
  • Price depends on the linking page's perceived importance and domain authority.
  • A commonly quoted flat fee (one fixed price) for low-tier sites (weak sites) is around $50 per link.
  • Subscription-style deals bill a link annually (once a year) for as long as it stays live.

Judgment rules

  • Paying for a link is a judgment call. You weigh each case.
  • It is reasonable when the price is affordable.
  • It is reasonable when the site is good quality.
  • It is reasonable when the expected result justifies the cost.
  • Links bought on an annual subscription should be judged by the same value test before renewal.
  • When a site sends a formal rate card (a published price list), check whether it offers bundle prices (several links for one fee).
  • Compare the cost per link across the bundle. Compare it against the linking page's authority before deciding.

  • An informal, unprofessional offer with a low flat fee often signals a site testing whether you will pay.
  • Treat such offers as a negotiation opening.
  • Redirect them to a guest post rather than paying the quoted amount.
  • This keeps the spend low while still securing a link.
  • A guest post offered in place of a paid link gives two returns: a backlink to your site and exposure for your name on another site.
  • If you do not write the article yourself, a content writer can produce it. The offer stays workable without your own time.
  • Frame the offer as adding value to their readers.

Finding Prospects

8 min

Procedure: finding likely linkers for a target keyword

A likely linker is a site that may link to you. A SERP is the Google results page for a search. A CSV is a spreadsheet export file.

  1. Pick the target keyword (the search term you want to rank for). Check that its SERP has many ranking pages with many backlinks.
  2. Open the SERP overview in an SEO tool. The example uses Ahrefs Keywords Explorer, but any SEO tool works. Export the full results list as CSV.
  3. Repeat step 2 for every similar secondary keyword. More keywords uncover more likely linkers.
  4. Merge all exported CSV files into one sheet in Excel or Google Sheets.
  5. Remove duplicate rows based on the URL column only (the web address column), keeping the first instance of each.
  6. Sort the sheet by the backlinks column, from highest to lowest.
  7. Work down the sorted list only while domains have at least 10 backlinks.
  8. For each domain, paste its URL into Site Explorer (Ahrefs' tool for checking links to a site). Open the Backlinks report. Filter to Dofollow (links that pass SEO value). Group similar links as one link per domain. Export the full list as CSV.
  9. Save all per-domain exports in the same folder.
  10. Merge the per-domain exports into one master sheet.
  11. Filter the master sheet by Domain Rating (Ahrefs' site strength score). Keep only domains between 10 and 75.

Principles and rules

  • A likely linker is a site that has already linked to a page on the same topic as your content.
  • It is the most probable source of a new link to your better piece on that topic.
  • A keyword can still be worth using even if its keyword difficulty (how hard it is to rank) is above the target of 10.
  • That holds as long as many ranking pages for it have many backlinks.
  • Use as many secondary keywords as possible with the same topic when collecting SERP results.
  • Each extra keyword surfaces additional likely linkers.
  • For this method only dofollow backlinks are pursued.
  • Nofollow links (links that do not pass SEO value) are excluded from the list of likely linkers.
  • Process only domains with at least 10 backlinks to the ranking page.
  • Below that threshold, the time spent per link drops sharply. In the example, about 35 domains exceeded 10 backlinks.
  • Do not manually research what each referring page is about or who runs it at this stage.
  • Export the data and move on. This saves a lot of time.
  • In one example, the first domain had 137 dofollow backlinks from unique domains. The second had 19.
  • Each domain's full list is exported separately before merging.

Resource pages

  • A resource page is a section of a larger website. It links out to other valuable pages, websites and tools on one topic.
  • Its labels are typically resources, useful resources, links or useful links.
  • Getting listed there is a supplementary link-building tactic, not a main driver.
  • It works best as an extra link source for content pages, blog pages or the home page.
  • Resource page link building is not a volume tactic. A niche usually has only a few such pages.
  • Expect a small number of wins.
  • Treat the search as a recurring task, not a campaign.
  • Broader adjacent niches (nearby related markets) can also yield relevant pages.
  • Build search terms from the topics and use cases people have for your products. Do not build them from product names.
  • People who write resource pages think in topics. For example, a bathroom products store should search on home renovation rather than bathtub or vanity.
  • Product-name searches are less productive for this tactic.
  • Google results vary by country. Run the searches in the Google version for the market you sell to.
  • Results in another country may return irrelevant pages for the same search terms.

Procedure: finding and qualifying resource pages

  1. Choose topic-based keywords that describe what your customers use your products for.
  2. Search Google for each keyword combined with resources, helpful links, useful links and useful resources. For example, keyword followed by the word resources.
  3. Search keyword followed by inurl:links to find URLs that contain the word links. inurl: is a Google search command that finds words inside web addresses. Try resources as well.
  4. Go through pages two and three of the search results, since useful pages often appear there.
  5. Quickly assess the quality of every candidate page, starting with its domain authority.
  6. Discard candidates with a domain authority of zero or below 10.
  7. Check that the page is relevant to your topic and that its audience matches your target market. Discard irrelevant pages.
  8. Decide which of your own pages fits the page: homepage, blog page or blog collection page, or a newly written blog post. Pitch only that page.
  9. Find the email address on the site. If none is listed, use Hunter.io (a tool that finds email addresses).
  10. Send a personalised outreach email from your template and fill in the specific details.
  11. Handle any link negotiation (payment or link exchange requests) with a prepared response.
  12. Send follow-up emails at a regular interval.

Search operator and tools

  • The Google search operator inurl:links returns pages with the word links in their URL. An operator is a special search command.
  • Such pages often reveal links pages that are resource pages.
  • The same operator works with the word resources.
  • Combine it with the topic keyword, for example keyword followed by inurl:links.
  • Ubersuggest (an SEO tool) checks the domain authority of a candidate resource page's website. Use it before you pitch.
  • Run every candidate through an SEO tool, such as Ubersuggest, as part of the quality check.
  • Hunter.io finds email addresses for a website's owner or contact.
  • Use it as the fallback when no email is published on the site.
  • The tools are used in sequence.
  • First: keyword and competitor research, to identify content and likely linking sites.
  • Second: email extraction, to find contact addresses for those sites.
  • Finally: cold email outreach, to request links.
  • Hunter.io can be searched with a website URL. It returns the email addresses it holds for that domain.
  • Hunter.io can also be searched with a single email address, to check whether it can be found.
  • You are charged only for each address that is successfully returned. Searches with no result cost nothing.

Domain authority thresholds for resource pages

  • Avoid domain authority below 10 for resource page link building. Above 10 is generally worth pursuing.
  • A domain authority of zero passes no SEO value.
  • Accumulating many such links can hurt your ability to rank.
  • Check the quality of a resource page's site before you pitch it. Use an SEO tool to confirm its domain authority is above 10.
  • Only proceed to pitching once the site passes this check.
  • The procedure discards 10 and below, while the rule text says above 10.
  • Treat exactly 10 as the boundary case. Check it against the other filters.

Choosing which of your pages to pitch

  • If a resource page lists businesses, suppliers or similar companies, pitch your homepage as the page to be linked.
  • If it links to individual content on other sites, pitch a blog post or your blog collection page instead.
  • Pitch a blog collection page that holds many useful articles when the resource page links to blogs or useful content in general.
  • Choose a page that gives the audience real advice relevant to the resource page's topic.
  • If no existing page of yours fits a content-linking resource page, write a new blog post.
  • Pick a topic the audience is interested in, ideally one that can rank for keywords.
  • Pitch that post to the resource page after it is published. Do not rush it.
  • If a resource page mixes trade bodies (industry associations), government sources and service providers with no single clear theme, pitch one of two pages.
  • Pitch your homepage as a trusted supplier of the product category, or pitch your blog collection page with practical how-to content.
  • Choose the page that best matches the mix of links already on the list.
  • Do not pitch product pages or collection pages to resource pages.
  • These are commercial pages. The request reads as a sales ask, not a value offer for the site's audience.
  • That lowers the chance of being listed.
  • Do not pitch pages on your site that are only loosely related to the topic of the resource page.
  • An irrelevant page significantly reduces the chance of being added to that page.

Judging neglected sites and mixed lists

  • A site that looks neglected or dated is not automatically disqualified.
  • If the blog has been online for many years and the author still publishes new posts, it can still be worth pitching.
  • Pass the domain authority check first.
  • Some blogs offer guest post exchanges. They publish your guest post in return for one of yours.
  • If the link is to a commercial site, the blog may charge according to rates published on its advertising page.
  • Check that page before agreeing.

Example of search volume

  • A search combining a topic keyword with inurl:links in Google Australia returned about 202,000 results.
  • Many hits were ordinary business or service pages rather than resource pages.
  • Check manually what each result actually is before you spend time on it.

Outreach Emails

2 min
  • Keep every outreach email short, polite, and on topic. Outreach means contacting people to ask for something. Long messages and side stories lower reply rates. Keep each email to the request and one clear question.
  • Resource page outreach does not need a bulk email or mail-merge tool (software that sends many personalised emails at once). Send each email one by one. A resource page is a page that lists useful links. There are only a few relevant ones in one niche (a small topic area).
  • Pitch your page as a valuable resource for the site's audience. Do not pitch it as a way to sell your products. Your page's usefulness is the reason the owner should add the link.
  • Many blogs do not show a public email address. Look for a contact page, an about page, or an author page. A named author's email may be listed there. If you find none, use Hunter.io (a tool that finds email addresses linked to a website).
  • Before you start outreach, prepare one standard response to payment requests and link-exchange requests. A link exchange means trading links with another site. Use the same response every time.
  • For backlink outreach, send no more than two to three emails to one influencer or publication. A backlink is a link from another site to yours.
  • If there is no reply, switch channel. For example, use a Facebook page or an Instagram direct message.
  • Check back in the future.

Opening formula for a resource page outreach email

Formula:

  • Name the specific resource page.
  • Give one brief reason it helps its readers.
  • Name the single page of yours that adds value.
  • Ask directly whether they would consider adding it.

Why it works:

  • It shows you read the page.

  • It moves the request from self-promotion to audience value.

  • It gives the owner an easy yes or no.

  • Example 1: Hello [Name], your links page on [topic] is a great find for people researching [subtopic]. I have written a guide on [subtopic] that would fit well alongside your listings, so would you consider adding it?

  • Example 2: Hi [Name], your resources list on [topic] saves readers a lot of searching. I put together a practical checklist for [subtopic] that could be useful to your audience, and I wondered if you would be open to including it.

Template: initial outreach to a site after a recent SEO check (use exactly as written in the source)

SEO means search engine optimisation, which is making a site easier to find on Google.

Replies and Negotiation

4 min

Hard no versus soft no

  • A hard no is a rude or hostile reply. Do not answer it.
  • Remove that contact from your outreach list. Mark them do-not-contact, which means never write to them again in future campaigns.
  • A soft no is a polite refusal, often with praise. Answer it to keep the relationship open.
  • Replying to a hostile hard no makes things worse. It rarely changes the outcome.
  • Do not reply. Delete the contact from the list, record them as do-not-email, and move on.
  • Outreach is about building relationships with sites in your industry and niche. A no today does not rule out a link later.
  • Keep replying politely to soft no answers. Other chances may come from the same contact.

Declining a paid link while keeping the relationship

A paid link is a link you buy with money.

Template: declining a paid-link offer and offering a guest post (a guest post is an article you write for another site)

Thanks for your reply. Unfortunately, I don't have the budget to pay for links of this kind. If you'd still like to work together, could I write a guest post for your site instead? I'm confident I could create something that adds genuine value for your readers. What do you think?:)
  • To decline a paid link, name a budget or policy limit. This is the reason you cannot pay.
  • Then offer a free alternative. The alternative gives the other site content value. It also gives you a backlink.
  • Frame the offer as mutual benefit. The reply can be yes or no.
    • Example 1: Thanks for the quote. Our budget is set for content only this quarter. Would you be open to a short guest article from me instead? Happy to draft a few ideas.
    • Example 2: We don't pay for placements right now, but I can contribute an in-depth guide to your resource section that your readers would find useful. Interested?

Link exchanges

  • A link exchange means the other site asks you to link back to them. Generally avoid it.
  • Search engines may view trading links unfavorably over time. Reciprocal linking (trading links back and forth) can look like a trick.
  • A link exchange also spends your own link authority. Link authority is the trust a link passes on to the page it points to.
  • When a site proposes an exchange, decline politely and redirect to a guest post.
  • Declining is acceptable. It is not a sign of a failed outreach.

Template: declining a link exchange and redirecting to a guest post (version 1)

Thanks for getting back to me. Sadly, I avoid link exchanges because they can cause problems with Google. I understand you need to gain something from these arrangements, so I was wondering whether I could write a guest post for your site. It would be a mutually beneficial way to collaborate. Any thoughts?:)

Template: declining a link exchange and redirecting to a guest post (version 2)

Thanks for getting back to me. Regrettably, I steer clear of link exchanges. My SEO specialist has warned me that these can sometimes lead to trouble with Google. I completely understand you want to get something out of such exchanges, so I had the idea of writing a guest post for your site. It would be a win-win collaboration. Any thoughts?:)

Template: closing reply when a site will not write a guest post and you decline the exchange entirely

Totally get it, don't blame you. We'll just have to leave it there, but if you do still want to link to my site, please feel free to do so.

Asking for feedback after a soft no

Template: requesting feedback

Hey, thanks for getting back to me. No problem:) I wanted to check whether you had any feedback. I just want to be sure I wasn't too far off track with this. Thanks
  • After a soft no, asking for feedback opens a second chance.
  • If the feedback points to a different content direction, offer a guest post on that topic at no cost to them.
  • The pattern is: acknowledge, ask for feedback, then offer a guest post that matches what they want.
    • Example 1: Thanks for the honest reply. What topic would fit your readers better? I could draft a piece on that and send it over.
    • Example 2: Appreciate you letting me know. If you'd like something more practical for your audience, I can write a guest article on that angle.

Clarifying a misunderstood request

  • Sometimes a contact did not understand the request. They may be confused, or suspect they must pay.
  • Send a short, polite clarification. Ask them to update an existing piece of content so it links to your article.
  • Use the batch spreadsheet (the spreadsheet for this round of outreach) to find the target URL (web address) before you write.
  • If they stay unclear, offer a phone call to walk them through it.

Template: clarifying the request

Thanks for writing back. I had a smaller exchange in mind. Would you be open to editing your article at [Their URL] so it links to my article at [Your URL]? I think it would be a valuable addition for your readers. What do you think?

Follow-Up and Verification

1 min

Procedure: follow-up when an agreed link never appeared

  1. Check the page for the link. Confirm it is missing.
  2. Check Google Search Console (Google's free tool that shows your site's search data) for the backlink.
  3. Reply in the same email thread, so the earlier commitment is visible.
  4. Write a short, polite message. Say you looked but could not find the link. Ask whether you looked in the wrong place.
  5. Sound like you may have missed it. Do not sound like they broke a promise. This keeps the relationship friendly.
  6. Keep the tone polite and light. They usually add the link after this reminder.

Template: follow-up reminder

That's great! I just checked, but I couldn't find the link. Maybe I was looking in the wrong place?:) Thanks
  • Use Google Search Console to check whether an agreed backlink went live on the partner's site.
  • Send follow-up emails at a regular interval.

Updating Links to Retired Pages

1 min

A retired page is a page you have removed from your store.

  • When you remove a page, first find the pages that link to it.
  • Use Ahrefs (a tool that lists which pages link to a site) or Ubersuggest (a similar tool) for this.
  • Know exactly where each link sits.

Template: asking a linking site to update a link to a retired page

Subject: Updating a link to our page on [Their site name]

Hi [Name],

We are removing the page [OLD URL] from our store. Your site currently links to it on [LINKING PAGE URL], in [section or position of the link on that page].

Would you be able to change that link so it points to our new page, [NEW URL]? This keeps the link working for your readers and keeps the link pointing to a good page on our site.

Thank you,
[Your name]
[Brand name]
[Contact email]

Risk, Anchor Text and Disavowing

1 min
  • Disavow spammy backlinks before a sale. To disavow means to tell Google to ignore certain links.
  • Spammy links come from sites such as adult or unrelated spam sites.
  • Buyers look closely at the link profile (the full list of links pointing to your site) for these.
  • Anchor text is the clickable words of a link. If anchor text is heavily over-optimized (too many links use the same keywords), add more varied and natural anchors to dilute it.
  • Make these link changes early. A change made a month before a sale can damage how the site looks to a buyer.
  • Buying backlinks is risky. It can lead to a penalty, which means Google lowers your site's ranking.
  • Stick with guest posting on sites that already publish guest content.
  • Do not pitch paid links to link farms (sites that exist only to sell links).
  • Remove them from the list instead of negotiating.

Further details

1 min
  • Paid sponsored posts (articles a site publishes for payment) on large publications can include a link back to your store.
