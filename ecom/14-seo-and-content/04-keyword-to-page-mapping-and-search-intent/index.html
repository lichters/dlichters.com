Keyword-to-Page mapping and search intent

Section 14.4 · 4 of 20 in this chapter · 21 min read

In short

  • Give each search term to one page only. A search term is the words people type into Google.
  • Pick the page type by what the searcher wants: learn (article), compare or browse (collection), or buy one exact item (product page).
  • Before you write, check the top 10 Google results for that search. Copy the page type that wins.
  • Link your other pages to the main page for each term, so that page gets the strength.
  • Much of this advice is from 2021-22. Check it before you rely on it.
More key points
  • Do not build one page around one item from a group of similar topics.
  • Make separate content for each item.
  • Use one page per search word. Do not repeat the same search words on many pages.
This section has 14 parts. Tap one to open it.

Core rule: one keyword, one page

2 min
  • Give each target keyword to exactly one page on your site. A target keyword is the search term you want one page to rank for. Ranking means where a page appears in Google's results.
  • Google usually shows only one result per website for a search. Brand-name searches are the common exception.
  • If several pages compete for one keyword, they split ranking signals. Ranking signals are the clues Google uses to rank pages.
  • Splitting signals wastes effort. It also leaves Google unsure which page is the main one. So none of them ranks well.
  • Pointing five or more pages at one search term is a common error. People believe one of them will rank better. In practice, none of them ranks well.
  • Ten pages, each aimed at one term, is the same error. Instead, optimize one dedicated page per keyword. Spread the rest of your keyword list across other pages.
  • One page with focused, deliberate keyword use has a much higher chance of reaching page one. Page one is the first page of Google results. Many pages with scattered keyword use have a much lower chance. Scattered means spread out with no clear plan.
  • Two pages aimed at the same keyword compete and weaken each other. Search engines may treat them as duplicates, meaning copies of the same content. Keeping both splits relevance. Relevance means how well a page matches a search.
  • Do not optimize your whole site around one keyword. Assign each keyword to one specific page, post, collection or product. A post means a blog article. A collection is a page that groups several products. Keep the overlap as small as possible. Then your site can rank for many keywords across different pages.
  • Do not put the same keyword on multiple pages. The overlap weakens the ranking of each page.
  • For a keyword with search volume (how many times people search it each month), only one page from a site usually ranks on page one. This happens about 95% of the time. Merge similar pages instead of hoping several will rank on the same results page.

Splitting the keyword list and mapping it to pages

2 min

Mapping means matching each keyword to a page.

  • Split your keyword list into two groups.
    • Category and product keywords go to category and product pages. A category keyword names a kind of product. A product keyword names one item.
    • Informational keywords go into the article list. Informational means the person wants to learn something.
    • Exception: a product keyword with strong search volume can also be used in product titles.
  • Map each keyword to a content type before the content is written.
    • A product group or sub-category keyword becomes a Shopify collection. Shopify is the software many online stores use. A sub-category is a smaller group inside a bigger category.
    • A comparison or educational keyword becomes a blog post.
    • An evergreen tool, such as a calculator, becomes a page. Evergreen means it stays useful for years.
  • Content strategy for ranking keywords:
    1. Choose the main keyword, for example "hybrid surfboards". Hybrid means a mix of two board types.
    2. If the keyword fits a product collection, make that collection page rank for it.
    3. In blog posts and other content, link to the collection page. An internal link is a link from one page on your site to another page on your site.
    4. This focuses link authority on the collection. Link authority is the ranking strength that links pass along. It also focuses ranking power, meaning how high a page can rank.
  • Keyword workflow from research to tracking:
    1. Build a large keyword list through keyword research. You can outsource this to a freelancer who delivers a spreadsheet.
    2. Select the keywords you want to target.
    3. Reduce them to a top 20 or top 50 list.
    4. Map each keyword to an existing page. If no page fits, add a planned page to your SEO plan. SEO means search engine optimization, the work of making a site easier to find on Google.
    5. Track the ranking of the top keywords over time with a tool such as Ubersuggest. Ubersuggest is an SEO tool. Watch organic traffic (free visits from search results) alongside it.

Choosing the page type by intent

2 min

Search intent is the reason behind a search. It is what the person wants.

  • Search intent decides the page type. Commercial searches are searches from people who want to buy or compare. Non-branded searches do not use a brand name. Searches like this, with no specific item in mind, lead to collection or content pages.
  • A precise product search leads to a product page.
  • A shopper who searches a general product type is better served by a collection or content page. These pages show options and guidance.
  • Sending that shopper straight to one product page forces extra clicks. Each extra click raises the chance they leave.
  • Judge the intent behind each term, not only the product name.
  • A broad term such as "surfboards with motors" is middle-of-funnel. The funnel is the buyer's path from learning to buying. Middle-of-funnel means comparing options.
  • That kind of term fits a content or collection page, not one product page.
  • Search intent must match the landing page. A landing page is the page a visitor arrives on.
  • Informational queries should land on pages that answer the question.
  • Queries for a named product model should land on a page about that exact product. They should not land on a general category or content page.
  • For organic traffic, find the search terms that bring visitors to each page.
  • If a page gets the wrong searches, either change the page to fit the terms that should bring buyers, or rank a separate page for the other terms.
  • Map terms to page types by intent:
    • A term asking what kinds of a product exist, or how to choose one, such as "types of surfboards", fits a content piece.
    • A low-difficulty term with real volume fits a content piece. Difficulty means how hard it is to rank. Example: "surfboard volume calculator".
    • A term for one specific model or product number fits a product page.
    • A broad group term for a buyer segment (a type of customer) can go to a guide page. It can also go to a collection page with added content.
  • Buying-intent pattern: combine the product category with a buying-intent word. Examples are "for sale", "best" or "top seven". Add the year too.
  • This works because people close to buying search with these words. So pages built this way match high-intent searches. High-intent means the person is ready to buy soon.
  • Two new examples in this pattern: "standing desk converters for sale" and "top seven espresso machines for 2026".

Checking the SERP before you write

2 min

SERP means search engine results page. It is the list Google shows for one search.

  • Before writing, check the top 10 Google results for your search term.
  • Note their format, such as product pages or guides.
  • If most of those results are product pages, you need a product page to compete for that keyword.
  • Pattern matching: the page you publish must look like the pages Google already ranks for that keyword.
  • Transactional pages (pages where people buy) do not rank for informational searches.
  • Blog posts do not rank for commercial searches.
  • Check the top 10 results first to see which page type wins.
  • Check search intent with a Google search before choosing the page type:
    1. Search the target keyword in Google.
    2. Look at the first page of results. Use the first five results as the reference.
    3. If most results are product pages, Google sees commercial intent. Target a product page with the best matching product.
    4. If most results are content pages, Google sees informational intent. Create a content page for the keyword.
  • Choosing between a collection page and a content page:
    1. Search the keyword in Google and review the first page of results.
    2. If the first page is mostly collection pages, Google wants to show product listings. A collection page fits.
    3. If the first page is mostly content pages such as listicles (list-style articles, like "Top 10..."), build a content page that is better than the existing results.
    4. Check the backlink counts of the top results in Ahrefs or Ubersuggest. Backlinks are links from other websites to a page. Ahrefs is an SEO tool.
    5. If the top results have many backlinks, choose a content page. Backlinks are easier to earn for content than for collection pages.
  • Manual SERP check to confirm a keyword tool's picture of the results. A keyword tool is software that shows search data.
    1. Search the candidate keyword in a normal Google search. Use the same country setting as the keyword tool.
    2. Compare the organic results with the SERP overview from the keyword tool.
    3. Confirm that most top results are informational articles with many backlinks. They should not be store, government or navigational pages. Navigational pages are pages people go to directly, such as a brand's home page.
    4. Keep the topic only if the manual search confirms the tool's picture.
  • For a "best [product]" search term, check the first page of results. If most results are listicles, one product page is unlikely to rank. Target that term with a listicle and place your own product at the top.
  • Case, product-group keyword "L-shaped standing desk":
    • The team checked the organic results. They ignored sponsored ads, which are paid ads.
    • The first organic result was a manufacturer's product page optimized for the keyword. The manufacturer is the company that makes the product.
    • The second was an Amazon page. It looked like a product, but it was a collection of L-shaped standing desks.
    • A Wayfair result, reached through its brand section, was also a collection page for L-shaped standing desks. Wayfair is a furniture website.
    • The top pages were mostly collection pages. So the keyword was assigned to a Shopify collection, not a blog post.

Product pages

2 min
  • Product pages should target brand name, product name and SKU terms. A SKU is a unique product code used for stock.
  • These terms have little competition. They often rank in the top three with minimal work. Top three means the first three results.
  • Category and blog pages should keep targeting their own broader topics.
  • Product pages target branded terms. These include specific product names, model names or product numbers. They also include very specific product-type searches that one product can fully answer.
  • Do not assign broad terms to product pages.
  • Product pages rarely rank for anything beyond the product name. Google reads the product name first from the page title.
  • For broader terms, use a blog, content page or collection page instead.
  • Ranking a product page for a broad product-type term is much harder than ranking a content piece for it. It is not impossible.
  • So give product pages only narrow, specific search terms.
  • Product pages are poor targets for broad, non-branded search terms. Many other pages on your site also use those terms.
  • A product page has limited text space to optimize for them.
  • A product page works best for terms tied to its own title, model or part number, and brand plus product type.
  • Product pages can rank for a brand name plus a specific product type, such as a brand and model-type combination.
  • They cannot rank for the brand name alone.
  • Broad group terms with many suitable products are not product-page targets. A term such as "surfboards with motors" is middle-of-funnel. It belongs on a content or collection page.
  • Do not send a search for a broad group, such as "surfboards for beginners", to one specific product page.
  • If the shop holds dozens of matching products, the chance that one page is the right product for that searcher is very low.
  • A visitor may leave and not come back if the page does not match.
  • A term that matches one or several products is best left off the product-page list when you have many suitable items.
  • Such terms belong on content or collection pages.
  • Product pages are reserved for brand names, model names, product numbers and very specific single-product searches.
  • On e-commerce sites with more than about 50 products, especially grouped in a few product types with 30 or more items each, product pages should target specific keywords.
  • Use the product title, model or part number, or brand plus product type. Do not use broad generic terms.
  • On sites with only one or two products, a more relaxed keyword approach is acceptable.
  • Keyword-to-product exception: a product keyword with strong search volume can also be used in product titles.

Collection pages

1 min
  • Collection pages target broad product-type terms, brand names, and specific product-type terms.
  • They suit people looking for a type of product, or a brand, that has a selection of products.
  • Competitive collection-page terms need substantial written content on the page to rank.
  • Collection or category pages suit product-type keywords. A category page groups similar products.
  • For resellers (shops that sell other brands' products), they also suit brand names or brand-plus-product-type combinations.
  • They are the right page when a shopper searches for a type of product without knowing the exact item they want.
  • For a product-group term where the top organic results are mostly collection pages, assign a Shopify collection rather than a blog post.

Content pages

2 min
  • Content pages target informational terms, problem-based terms and broad product-type and product-use terms.
  • Problem-based terms are searches where the person seems to want an answer to a problem.
  • Product-use terms say what a product is used for.
  • Content pages such as blog posts, guides and resource pages suit informational keywords. Example: how to choose a product.
  • They also suit broad and specific product-type keywords.
  • Collection and product pages are not suited to informational queries.
  • Content pages allow longer text. They can hold more keyword variations and synonyms (words with the same meaning).
  • They can also go deeper on a term than a collection page.
  • A content page can reach about 10 or more keyword uses when the post runs around 3,000 words.
  • A product page with little text usually needs only 2 to 3 mentions beyond the first 100 words.
  • Deep informational targeting, such as how-to guides or buying advice, belongs on content pages.
  • Collection pages carry some content too, but within a tighter limit.
  • Prefer content pages over collection pages for broad, educational terms, such as "surfboards for beginners".
  • Content pages attract backlinks. Collection and product pages rarely do.
  • Competitive terms need links. Content is the most practical page type for earning links.
  • Broad, high-volume product-type keywords are more competitive. They need high-quality content and backlinks to rank.
  • Content pages are the usual choice, because they attract backlinks more easily than product pages.
  • When a broad "for beginners" term fits many products, a content page such as an ultimate guide works better than a product or collection page.
  • The guide should explain which product type the visitor needs. Then it links to the matching collection or specific product pages.
  • If you use a collection page instead, it still needs written content to rank.
  • For a term that asks for guidance, create one ultimate guide page. It teaches the right product choice.
  • Then link from it to matching collection or product pages.
  • If no content piece is planned, a collection page for the same term is the fallback. A fallback is the backup option.
  • Lower-volume variations of a head term can sit on the same page. A head term is the main broad keyword.
  • Variations are adjective versions, such as "hybrid", "retro" or "cheap".
  • Do this when no other page targets that exact phrase. One page can then rank for many terms, each with smaller search volume.
  • Example: a laptop stand for desk keyword has 14,800 monthly searches, high competition and a $2.16 CPC. CPC means cost per click, the price paid for each click on a paid ad.
  • This keyword can support a blog post comparing a laptop stand with an adjustable-height desk. This works even if the store does not sell laptop stands.
  • A related keyword about ergonomics (a comfortable, healthy workplace setup) can support a second post. That post compares an ergonomic laptop stand with a standing desk.

Keyword placement on a page

2 min
  • Google uses on-page text as its most basic relevance signal. On-page text means the headings, body text and visible words on the page.
  • So a page meant to rank for a term should contain that term.
  • A target keyword should appear roughly 5 to 10 times across the whole page.
  • Count every on-page element. These are the title, product or post heading, image alt text (a short description of an image for search engines and screen readers), H2 subheadings (second-level headings) and body copy.
  • Do not count only the body text.
  • The range exists because a page has many places where keywords can sit.
  • Use synonyms and close variations of the focus keyword. The focus keyword is the main keyword for the page.
  • This avoids repeating the exact phrase too often.
  • If the exact phrase would have to appear more than about 10 times, switch to close variations. Choose variations that no other page on the site targets.
  • Keyword placement workflow for a new page:
    1. Start from the keyword research list. Pick one focus keyword for the page.
    2. Confirm the page type fits the keyword intent: product, collection or content page.
    3. Place the focus keyword in the title tag, ideally at the front. Do not make the title read awkwardly. The title tag is the main page title for search engines.
    4. Place it in the first 100 words of the body text, plus 2 to 3 more occurrences depending on page length.
    5. Use it in at least one H2 subheading. Not every H2 needs it.
    6. Use it in at least one image file name.
    7. Use it in at least one image alt text.
    8. Include it in the page URL. The URL is the page's web address.
    9. Include it in the meta title. This is the title meant for search results.
    10. Include it in the meta description. This is the short summary under the title in results.
    11. Check the total count stays within about 5 to 10 occurrences across the page. Use synonyms or close variations for further mentions.

Reviewing existing pages in Search Console

1 min

Google Search Console is a free Google tool. It shows how your site appears in search.

  • Case, hoodie store: the store checked Google Search Console for its product queries over the last three months. A query is a search someone typed.
  • The query "Source A hoodie" had 88 total impressions. An impression is one time your page was shown in results. The average organic position was 5.
  • A unisex zip hoodie page appeared for this query with 85 impressions at position 5.
  • It received zero clicks. That made it the page to optimize first for that keyword.

Mapping an existing site to keywords

1 min
  • Mapping an existing large site:
    1. Map each page to one keyword it should target.
    2. Start with the most important pages.
    3. Make sure no other page targets the same keyword.
    4. Work through the rest of the site gradually. This cannot be done overnight on a large site.
  • Case, surf shop:
    • The shop has about 30 surfboard variations across brands, sizes and configurations.
    • One product-type term appears on many of its pages.
    • Choose one page for that head term. A content or collection page is preferred, not a product page.
    • Use the term 5 to 10 times on that page only.
    • Other pages keep it at one or two uses. Google can then see the chosen page as the most relevant match for the term.
  • Among very similar product pages that compete for the same keywords, choose one as the main page.
  • Point more internal links to it. This tells search engines which page to rank.
  • It also stops them from splitting ranking power across similar pages.
  • Link content to the collection it should rank for.
  • Blog posts and other content should link to the collection page. This focuses link authority and ranking power on the collection.

Merging competing pages

1 min
  • Merging competing blog posts:
    1. Identify the posts that target the same keyword.
    2. Choose the stronger post to keep as the winner.
    3. If the weaker post has no links and no authority, delete it. Authority means how much Google trusts a page. Then redirect its URL to the winner. A redirect sends visitors from the old address to the new one.
    4. Copy any useful information from the deleted post into the winner.
    5. If the weaker post has links and authority, reassign it to a different keyword instead of deleting it.
    6. If the content fits another term, update the post for that term rather than deleting it.

Category mapping and topical authority

4 min
  • Topical authority means being the go-to source on one subject area. A 12-month content plan should aim for it.
  • It is built over time through a connected set of pages, not single articles.
  • One product can belong to several categories at once.
  • Example: a men's black running short fits men's running shorts, black running shorts, and running shorts with pockets.
  • A category for each relevant search variant lets the store target more specific keywords.
  • It also reaches shoppers who search for exactly that item.
  • Broad head terms, such as "running shorts", face heavy competition.
  • They often attract shoppers with a different intent. Example: a woman searching for women's products.
  • Specific category terms are easier to rank for, and faster.
  • They also convert better. Convert means turning visitors into buyers. Better conversion brings sales sooner.
  • Build a mix of broad parent categories and narrower niche categories.
  • Narrow ones are based on specific use cases. A use case is a specific way people use a product.
  • Furniture example: sectional categories for piece count (two-piece, three-piece, four-piece), color (brown, leather, black, canvas, white) and room (living room sectionals).
  • A single product can sit in several of these at once.
  • Attribute categories pair a product kind with one or more traits, such as shade, cut or purpose.
  • In clothing, the structure runs: gender, then product, then trait.
    • men's running shorts with pockets
    • men's running shorts with a lining
    • men's running shorts without a lining
    • men's compression running shorts
    • women's running shorts with a high waist
  • For a swimwear shop, the same structure yields women's high-waisted swim bottoms with ruching and men's quick-dry board shorts with a zip pocket. Ruching means gathered fabric. Quick-dry means it dries fast.
  • Granular category pages capture shoppers with very specific searches. Granular means very detailed.
  • These shoppers are more likely to buy.
  • Most competitors do not build pages at this level of detail. So the store gains a ranking and sales advantage.
  • Blog articles or Shopify pages can both serve as topical authority content. Either format works.
  • Each article should link to the relevant collection pages. That internal linking is how the collections get ranked.
  • Hybrid categories, such as a non-toxic version of a product, are only worth creating if people actually search for them on Google.
  • SEO captures existing demand rather than creating it. So a hybrid category without search volume will gain little traction. Traction means real progress.
  • Claim-type modifiers are words that make a claim, such as non-toxic or PFAS-free. PFAS are chemicals some people avoid.
  • Test them as hybrid categories at the broad product level, for example "non-toxic running clothes".
  • Do not test them at a narrow level such as "non-toxic running tights".
  • Narrow claim-plus-product combinations often show too little monthly search volume on Google. So check the volume before creating them.
  • Map out every category you can think of. Include an authority cluster for each. An authority cluster is a group of linked pages on one topic.
  • Skip top-of-funnel and AI Overview-type queries. Top-of-funnel means early searches, when people are just learning. An AI Overview is Google's AI-written summary at the top of results.
  • Leave out top-of-funnel terms that everyone already understands. Example: the basic product name in its most obvious form.
  • Also leave out query types that an AI Overview would likely answer directly.
  • Articles on those queries rarely earn clicks.
  • Put the exclusion rules into the prompt from the start. A prompt is the instruction you give an AI.
  • Tell the AI to skip AI Overview-type queries and top-of-funnel terms.
  • Do not let the AI generate every query and then delete the unwanted ones afterward.
  • Give the AI a list of direct competitors when building the map.
  • Better input produces better output and speeds up the work.
  • Choose competitors you actually want to model your categories on.
  • Firecrawl can scrape competitor websites. Scrape means automatically copy their pages.
  • This lets you analyze their category structure and page content.
  • The scraped data is the input for mapping the competitor's categories.
  • Before creating pages, remove any category you do not stock or sell.
  • Example: if the store does not sell high-waisted running leggings, drop that category from the map.
  • Once the category list is cleaned, put the final map into one shared document in a fixed format.
  • The whole team can read and review it before any collection page copy is written.
  • This document is the handover point between the category map and the content work. Handover means passing work to the next step.
  • Category mapping procedure:
    1. Map out every category you can think of, including an authority cluster for each. Skip top-of-funnel and AI Overview-type queries.
    2. Give the AI your chosen competitors. Have it map the categories against them.
    3. Refine and trim the map inside the chat.
    4. Validate each category's search demand with a keyword tool.
    5. Remove the categories you do not sell.
    6. Export the cleaned map.
    7. Create a collection page for each remaining category.
    8. Create blog articles or Shopify pages that link to these collections. This builds topical authority.
  • AI-generated map exports can contain multiple tabs and hundreds of rows.
  • Those rows may include hallucinated (made-up), wrong or irrelevant entries.
  • Do the analysis and trimming inside the chat first. Then export a cleaned version.
  • Do not review a raw spreadsheet.
  • Case, hypothetical running brand. Hypothetical means an imagined example.
    • The brand had hundreds of non-toxic SKUs across all running subcategories.
    • The category mapping was tested on them.
    • The competitors came from an athleisure strategy (clothing for sport and everyday wear) built a month earlier. Happier Earth and Allware were named as the main examples.
    • The AI was asked to map categories against them.
    • The lesson reports no traffic or sales result for this map. It only shows the planning setup.

Pitfalls

1 min
  • Do not optimize a page for one item from a group of similar topics. "Optimize" means changing a page so search engines rank it higher. Example: one fun fact out of many fun facts.
  • Instead, make separate content for each item.
  • Why: a page rewritten around one sub-topic can drop in rankings. "Rankings" means where your page appears in Google search results.
  • Common mistake: keyword stuffing. A keyword is a word people type into search. Keyword stuffing means putting the same keywords over and over across many pages.
  • Give each keyword one dedicated page, a page built just for that keyword.
  • Use the rest of the keyword list on other pages.

Further details

1 min
  • Product pages, which sell one item, should rank for product-specific terms. For example, a model name, which is the exact name of one specific product.
  • Do not target broad category terms on a product page. A category term is a general group word, such as "shoes". These are very hard to rank for on a product page.
  • Instead, target broad terms with separate content pages. Each content page links to the product page.
