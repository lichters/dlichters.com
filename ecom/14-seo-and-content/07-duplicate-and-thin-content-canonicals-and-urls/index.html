Playbook › 14. SEO and content

Duplicate and thin content, canonicals and URLs

Section 14.7 · 7 of 20 in this chapter · 27 min read

In short

  • Copied text is still copied, even with a few words changed. Write every page's text yourself.
  • Aim for 300 words of unique text on each page you want Google to list.
  • Run a full check of your pages before you change anything.
  • Shopify makes extra web addresses for the same product. Keep the clean /products/ address as the one Google lists.
  • Do not change the web address of a page that already ranks well.
More key points
  • Get a page's web address (URL) right before you publish it. Changing it later needs a redirect, and Google may not list the new page right away.
  • For a product that is gone for good but still ranks in Google, keep its page live, stop people from buying it, and link shoppers to products you still sell.
  • Before you delete or change a page, check which other sites link to it. Then send its old address to a relevant page with a 301 redirect.
  • In Google's Coverage report, look at "Excluded" pages first. Many of them are normal, so check the real URLs before you act.

Duplicate and Thin Content: What Counts

2 min

  • Duplicate content means the same text in more than one place. It counts when large blocks of text fully match other content or look very similar.

  • This applies inside one website and across different websites.

  • Google does not say how similar counts as "appreciably similar." So minor word swaps on copied text will not make it look unique.

  • Changing a few words in a competitor's text is still copying.

  • Swapping one word or one sentence does not make the copy original.

  • Copying can cause conflicts with the original owner. Use competitor pages only to learn the structure, never the wording.

  • Multiple web addresses (URLs) that show the same content waste crawl time. Crawl time is the time Google spends visiting your pages.

  • Those duplicate URLs add no SEO value. SEO (search engine optimisation) means getting found on Google.

  • Duplicates do not directly lower the crawl rate (how often Google visits). But they still cost time.

  • Shopify stores (an online shop platform) are a known source of such duplicate URLs, so check them.

  • Google usually does not index duplicate pages. Indexing means Google adds a page to its list of pages it can show in results.

  • But Google still crawls them. That spends crawl budget, the number of pages Google is willing to visit on your site.

  • Crawling duplicates also lowers how Google rates the whole site.

  • So duplicate content mostly causes lost ranking chances, not a penalty (a punishment that lowers your rank).

  • A manual action (a penalty applied by a person at Google) for duplicate content is rare.

  • It applies mainly when the copying looks deliberately manipulative. An example is scraping (copying automatically) a whole competitor site to take its rankings.

  • Accidental duplication within your own site is very unlikely to lead to a penalty.

  • Search engines do not penalise duplicate product descriptions.

  • But they rank them less willingly. This hurts organic traffic (free visitors from search) over time.

  • Organic ranking matters later, not during the first paid-ads launch.

  • Identical product descriptions across many products are common in dropshipping (selling products that a supplier ships for you).

  • They make the site look weak to buyers.

  • Rewrite them as unique descriptions by the six-month mark, so the change has time to settle.

  • On a large site, duplicate and thin content on 20 to 30% of pages can noticeably hold back overall SEO.

  • Thin content is covered in the next section.

  • Auditing (checking) and fixing these areas is often one of the fastest SEO improvements available.

  • Example: a large shop has about 1,000 product pages with copied supplier descriptions. Those descriptions also appear on other websites.

  • The shop put real effort into only about 100 of them.

  • Google still crawls all 1,000 pages. It reads the site as 900 pages without value and 100 with value.

  • So the whole site ranks lower and gets fewer visitors.

  • Write unique text on the pages that matter most, instead of reusing shared supplier copy across the catalogue.

Thin Content: Definition and Effects

1 min

  • Thin content means pages with little or no text that search engines see as having little value to users.

  • Thin content can drag down the ranking (position in results) of the whole site.

  • Google can choose from an enormous number of pages. So thin pages are unlikely to rank, even when they are indexed.

  • Thin pages make Google rate the whole site lower.

  • They waste crawl budget by pulling Google's attention away from better pages.

  • They forfeit a keyword opportunity, a chance to rank for a search phrase that every page could otherwise target.

  • Adding real content to nearly any page tends to improve customer experience, conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy) and retention (customers coming back).

  • A site should have fewer low-value pages in the index, not more.

  • Past a tipping point (a level where it starts to cause harm), too many indexed pages weaken overall SEO.

  • This is especially true for low-quality pages, keyword-confused pages (pages that confuse Google about which search phrase they are for) and never-visited pages.

  • Pages that attract no visitors and serve no purpose should be removed from the index.

  • Content on a store is only worth adding if it is valuable to the reader.

  • Thin content added only for search engines does not help results.

  • Thin content is not a concern for very small stores with only a few pages.

  • Examples of small stores are a homepage, one product page and an about page.

  • The audit matters most for sites with hundreds or thousands of pages.

  • Examples of those sites are drop shippers with large product catalogues, or resellers of other brands' products.

  • Blog posts are normally unique and well over 300 words. So they usually do not need to be part of a thin or duplicate content audit.

  • A blog post of 300 words or less, or copied content, signals a problem.

Word and Percentage Targets

1 min

These targets come from different sources and apply in different situations. Use the one that matches the page you are checking.

Target Applies to Source
At least 300 words of unique text Pages you want indexed (listed by Google) and ranking; the minimum, and more is better
About 300 words as a guideline only Duplicate-content pages, which need most of their text to be unique, original and of good quality
At least 75% unique text Every page; a personal working target, not a figure documented by Google. Unavoidable factual specifications (the hard facts about a product) can stay identical, so extra unique content must make up the rest of the page
Less than half of SEO-relevant text duplicated (SEO-relevant text is the text search engines read to judge a page) Step in the tab-replacement procedure on product pages
About 2% or less duplicated text Product pages after the boilerplate (repeated standard text) pattern is applied
  • A product page with 300 words or more of unique text is neither duplicate nor thin. It can be removed from the thin-content list.
  • Minimise shared content as far as possible.
  • Shared content still cannot be removed entirely, because it often supports conversion.

Original Content and Copying Rules

1 min

  • You may copy a competitor's general layout, because most store layouts are similar.

  • Never copy their written content, logo or custom graphics.

  • All product and store text must be original.

  • Do not republish other sites' articles on your store, even with credit and a link.

  • Write unique content yourself, or hire a freelance writer (an independent writer you pay per job).

  • Do not paste text copied from other websites directly onto your pages. This includes supplier descriptions and blog posts.

  • For data from another source in a blog post, summarise it in your own words.

  • Then link out to the original source. That is acceptable.

  • Your website should open under only one domain version. Choose either www (the "www." at the start of an address) or non-www.

  • Every other version should redirect to it. A redirect automatically sends visitors from one address to another.

Thin and Duplicate Content Audit: Procedure

1 min

  • A page audit (a full check of every page) finds problem pages before any change is made.

    1. Identify all pages with duplicate content.
    2. Identify all pages with thin content.
    3. Identify all pages with 0 views in the past 6 to 12 months.
    4. Identify orphan pages that are not part of the site structure, meaning no other page links to them.
    5. Decide per page whether to add content, delete with redirect, or noindex. A noindex tag is hidden code that tells Google not to list the page.

  • Order of options for fixing or avoiding thin content:

    1. Run a page audit to find the problem pages.
    2. Add at least 300 words of unique text to pages worth keeping.
    3. If a page cannot get content, delete it and set a 301 redirect (a permanent forward) to a relevant live page, for example the homepage.
    4. If a page cannot be deleted but is thin, add a noindex tag.

  • Full audit procedure for product and collection pages:

    1. Start with product descriptions. If a product page has unique text of 300 words or more, it is neither duplicate nor thin. It can be removed from the list.
    2. Check collection pages (pages that group products into a category). If a collection page has no unique content, add it to the thin content list.
    3. Skip blog posts unless they are 300 words or less or copied. Those are a separate problem to fix.
    4. If product descriptions are duplicated across the store, add all of those product pages to the list.
    5. For each remaining page, record the page title and URL (web address). Then mark duplicate content yes or no, and thin content yes or no.
    6. For each problem page, decide Fix content? If no, decide Delete? and enter the redirect target if deleting. If neither applies, mark No-Index.
    7. Finish the full map before changing any page. Then hand the work to a VA (virtual assistant, a remote helper) if the site is large.

Audit Tracking and Tools

1 min

  • Before using a shared audit spreadsheet, go to File > Make a copy.

  • Choose a folder in your own Google Drive (Google's online file storage).

  • Leave the sharing and comments options unchecked.

  • Work only in your copy. Never work in the original.

  • Audit tracker workflow:

    1. Make a copy of the shared spreadsheet into your own Google Drive, rather than editing the original.
    2. For each relevant page, record page title and page URL.
    3. Mark whether it has duplicate content, and whether it has thin content (under 300 words).
    4. Mark Fix Content yes or no. If no, mark Delete yes or no.
    5. If deleting, enter the redirect target URL.
    6. If neither applies, mark No-Index.

  • SEO tools (software that checks your site), such as Ahrefs, Raven Tools and Semrush, each include a site audit feature.

  • A site audit crawls (scans) a site and flags duplicate and thin content.

  • Raven's site audit was described as the most accurate for finding these issues.

  • All such crawlers can make errors, so do a manual check too.

  • Fixing a large site is a gradual process.

  • A mapped spreadsheet can be handed to a VA for deletions, redirects, noindex entries and content tasks, with clear instructions for each.

  • Work steadily until all problems are fixed.

  • Pages with zero views over the past 6 to 12 months that are not going to change should be deleted and 301-redirected to a relevant page with value.

  • A site lost 40% of its search traffic overnight after a Google update targeting thin, low-value listings.

  • This shows how a single algorithm update (a Google ranking change) can remove rankings for many low-quality pages at once.

Boilerplate Tabs and Shared Text

2 min

  • Boilerplate is static text that loads on many pages. Examples are shipping, returns or guarantee tabs (small sections on a product page you switch between).

  • Boilerplate can still lower the value of each page, even if it appears on only five pages.

  • Shared text that makes up a large share of a page's SEO-relevant text is the main source of duplication on many shops.

  • Example from a large sauna shop: about 500+ product pages each carried the same three tabs.

  • The shipping tab had at least 200 words. A second block had roughly 150 words.

  • The product texts were mostly identical.

  • About 50% of the SEO-relevant text on the page was duplicated.

  • About 90% of site pages carried the same tab content.

  • Minimise boilerplate as far as possible.

  • Boilerplate can crowd out the unique content of the page.

  • When product descriptions are mostly images, the page has very little unique crawlable text. Crawlable text is text search engines can read.

  • Then shared tabs and boilerplate can make up most of the visible text.

  • Even a few hundred words of shared content can dominate the SEO value of the page.

  • Tab replacement procedure:

    1. Open a product page. Check whether tabs or sections with long text repeat across other product pages.
    2. Count how much of the SEO-relevant text on the page is identical to text on other pages. Aim for less than half duplicated.
    3. Replace long shared blocks with a short statement of the main point, such as free shipping or a 30-day return policy.
    4. Link the short statement to a full policy page that opens in a new window.
    5. Review the site. For each large duplicate chunk, shorten it while keeping a findable link to its own separate page.

  • Replace shared boilerplate with a two-line summary that answers the customer's main question.

  • Then add a call-to-action link (a clickable prompt that asks the reader to act) to the full policy page.

  • This keeps the important information visible.

  • It also keeps duplicated text to about 2% or less of the page text on a product page.

    • Shipping summary example: Free shipping on all orders within the country, see our full shipping policy.
    • Returns summary example: 30-day free returns, read our full returns policy.
    • Both lines link to their policy pages, which open in a new window.

  • Moving a long shipping or policy section off the product page can help the page rank for high-value model searches (searches for one specific product model).

  • Customers who need the information must still find it easily from the product page.

Shopify Duplicate URLs and Indexing

2 min

  • Shopify can create several URLs for the same product, such as /products/product-id and /collections/products/product-id.

  • Both show the same page, so they count as duplicate content.

  • A canonical tag (hidden code that names the main version of a page) reduces the problem.

  • But the two versions still exist.

  • Shopify generates several URLs that display the same page. This creates duplicate content.

  • Shopify's behaviour has some advantages.

  • But it needs managing, because it can cause an SEO problem.

  • The preferred base URL of a Shopify product is the root domain (your main web address), followed by /products/ and the product handle (the product's short name in the address).

  • For example: domain/products/product-handle.

  • Always index this URL.

  • A product reached through a collection page gets a different URL.

  • That URL includes /collections/collection-name/products/product-handle.

  • Google treats this as a separate page, even though the content is identical.

  • On a Shopify store connected to Google Search Console (Google's free tool that shows how it sees your site), it is normal to see many pages listed as known but not indexed.

  • Shopify sets this up by default. Product variants (versions of one product, such as colours or sizes), collection-based product URLs and paginated pages (pages split into page 1, 2, 3 and so on) do not get indexed as duplicates.

  • On Shopify stores, many pages show as not indexed.

  • These are usually product URLs with collection paths, or parameter variants (the same page with extra text added after a question mark).

  • The clean product URL is indexed. This is expected.

  • Indexing the variants would split ranking across several URLs for the same product. (Older advice? No: 2025.)

  • The fix for duplicates caused by Shopify navigation variants (the same page reached through different menu or filter paths) is generally not required, when the main collection URL is the indexed page.

  • A drop in indexed pages can be caused by duplicate versions of the same page.

  • For example, URLs with UTM parameters (tracking tags added to the end of a link) can be counted as separate pages.

  • To check, review which sitemap pages are actually indexed. A sitemap is a list of your pages that you give Google.

  • Then look for errors on the pages that are not indexed.

Canonical Tags, Noindex and Hidden Products

2 min

  • A canonical tag is HTML code (the building code of a web page) that marks one URL as the original version of a page.

  • It suggests that search engines do not index the other versions.

  • Shopify uses canonical tags, but they do not guarantee which version Google indexes.

  • Canonical tags for product variants on platforms other than Shopify are needed.

  • Each colour or size variant can create its own URL with the same content.

  • Shopify adds these canonical tags for product variants automatically.

  • On platforms other than Shopify, a colour or size variant often gets its own URL. A modifier (an extra word) is added to the end.

  • The description, measurements and price stay identical. Only one or two words change.

  • These variant URLs count as duplicate pages, unless a canonical tag points them to the main product.

  • Check platforms such as Magento, WooCommerce or BigCommerce (online shop software) for this setup.

  • Use a noindex tag for pages that must exist but should not be indexed.

  • Avoid serving the same content on dynamically generated URLs (addresses the system creates automatically).

  • If Google indexes a duplicate URL instead of the intended one, backlinks (links from other sites to your page) pointing to the non-indexed URL lose much of their value.

  • The aim is to serve content consistently on a single URL.

  • Shopify creates a hidden collection URL for every product tag (a label on a product). An example is /collections/collection-name/tag-name.

  • These pages cannot be edited in the Shopify admin (the control panel where you run your shop).

  • They have very limited value.

  • They can still be indexed, which creates a potential problem.

  • The supplied code also sets noindex on tag-generated collection pages.

  • It does not delete them.

  • But it keeps them out of the index automatically.

  • To organise products by type, create a collection page.

  • Add content to it and include it in the site structure.

  • Do this rather than relying on product tags.

  • If a hidden product stays hidden long enough, Google can drop it from the index.

  • When it is reactivated, it must be re-indexed from scratch.

  • Any ranking and traffic it had can be lost.

  • Canonical and noindex setup procedure:

    1. Insert the supplied canonical code into theme.liquid, the main layout file in the Shopify theme.
    2. Avoid using product tags as much as possible.
    3. Instead of hiding products, set them to zero stock, or delete them and redirect the URL.

Multi-Storefront and Localised Setups

1 min

  • Check canonicals and hreflang (a code that tells Google which language or country version of a page to show) only if the store runs localised storefronts.

  • Localised storefronts are language or market subfolders.

  • If localised URLs compete with the main pages in search results, that can suppress rankings across the site.

  • If the store has no such subfolders, skip this check.

  • The canonical and hreflang check for multi-storefront setups (shops with several language or country versions) is a one-time audit of roughly 15 minutes.

  • It should confirm that localised URLs do not compete with the main pages.

  • It should not turn into an ongoing cleanup project.

URL Handles: Format and Setup

1 min

  • The product URL handle should follow this pattern: brand name, product name, then SKU or GTIN.

  • A SKU is your own product code. A GTIN is a global barcode number.

  • Write it in lowercase, with dashes between words and no spaces.

  • Add the SKU or GTIN after a final dash if the supplier provides one.

  • Add a SKU or GTIN to the end of the URL handle only when the supplier actually provides one.

  • Check the product details or specifications first.

  • If no identifier exists, use just the brand name and product name.

  • Put the target keyword (the main search phrase for the page) into the URL handle.

  • Write it in lowercase, with hyphens between words and no spaces. For example: customizing-your-standing-desk.

  • The URL handle is the first on-page element to check for the keyword.

  • In Shopify, the product URL follows the pattern domain/products/[handle].

  • The handle is edited in the URL and handle field of the Search engine listing preview. This is the box that shows how the page looks in Google.

  • Changing the handle changes the live URL.

  • So the focus keyword (the main phrase for the page) belongs in the handle only when the page is new, or the change is deliberate.

  • Setting path: Product page > Search engine listing > URL handle.

  • After editing the URL, a line appears below the field. It offers to create a URL redirect, which automatically forwards the old address to the new one.

  • Keep that option checked, then click Save, so old links forward to the new page.

  • Shopify generates the product URL by copying the full product title by default.

  • So long titles with barcodes or extra words produce long URLs.

  • Shorten the URL slug (the end part of the address) manually before publishing.

URL Handle Rules for New and Existing Pages

1 min

  • Keep the URL short. Google prefers short URLs, so product URLs should not be overly descriptive.

  • There is no fixed word count.

  • Keep it as short as possible, and avoid stacking descriptive words.

  • The page URL should contain the focus keyword and be short.

  • Long URLs get truncated (cut short) in search results.

  • Include the page's focus keyword in the URL.

  • For product pages, this is usually the product title. For blog posts, it is the target keyword.

  • A URL does not need to match the page title exactly.

  • It needs to be short and contain the focus keyword.

  • A URL is the unique identifier Google uses to recognise a web page and its content as one entity.

  • So each page should have a correctly formatted URL, tied to content that is as unique as possible.

  • Set the URL with the focus keyword when creating a new page.

  • For pages already ranking well, do not change the URL.

  • A change can cause temporary traffic losses without a clear gain.

  • Change URL handles only for new products, or stores with weak organic traffic.

  • Stores that already rank well organically should leave their URLs unchanged.

  • Do not rewrite existing URLs just to add keywords.

  • Changing URLs has consequences, so decide carefully before changing them.

  • Apply the keyword-in-URL rule mainly to new pages.

  • Do not change the URL and handle of a page after it is created, because changing it damages SEO.

  • Leave these fields alone once the page is saved.

  • New page creation procedure:

    1. When creating a new page, edit the URL before publishing it.
    2. Check that the URL is short and contains the focus keyword.
    3. Only then publish the page.

Changing URLs and Redirects

2 min
  • A URL is a page's web address. After a page is published, changing its URL creates a redirect, which sends visitors from the old address to a new one. Google treats the result as a new page that is not indexed (not yet listed in Google's search results), even if only one letter changed. So get URLs right before publishing.
  • When you change a URL handle (the last part of a page's address), you need a redirect from the old URL to the new one. This keeps existing links and search results working. Shopify (the online store software) can create this redirect automatically. Confirm that it was created.
  • A 301 redirect passes SEO value (search ranking benefit) to the new URL. SEO means search engine optimization. A 302 redirect is temporary. It is not meant as a permanent move.
  • Redirects keep visitors who bookmarked or linked to an old URL on your site. This can save a sale. Without a redirect, they land on an error page (a "page not found" screen) and usually leave.
  • Deleting a page does not remove it from Google's index right away. The old URL can keep showing a 404 error ("page not found") for a while. This also hurts visitors who came from bookmarks or links. A 301 redirect, which is permanent, sent to a page with value prevents this.
  • Deleting pages without a 301 redirect leaves visitors and Google with error pages. Deleting is safe when you pair it with a redirect to a relevant page.
  • Avoid redirects unless you really need them. Many redirects use up crawl budget (how many pages Google's visiting program, called a crawler, will read on your site). They also make Google's crawling less efficient.
  • Google values links that point to redirects less. A backlink (a link from another site to yours) that goes through a redirect loses some value. The better step is to ask the sites linking to the old URL to update the link to the new URL.
  • Before changing or deleting a URL, check these four things:
    1. Check the page's index status, meaning whether Google lists it.
    2. Check its organic traffic (free visitors from search, not paid ads). A change and redirect will probably reduce it, at least for a while.
    3. Check its backlinks in an SEO tool such as Ubersuggest or Ahrefs before you delete or change the URL.
    4. If you delete or change the page, set a 301 redirect to another relevant page.

Discontinued and Backordered Products

2 min
  • Discontinued means the maker has stopped selling a product. Backordered means it is sold out, but more is coming. Two approaches apply to discontinued products. Which one you use depends on whether the product will come back and whether its page ranks (shows high in Google results).
    • Product will not be sold again, page has little ranking value: delete the product and set a 301 redirect, so visitors and Google do not hit a 404 error.
    • Product page still ranks in Google: keep the page live and redirect visitors to products you can sell. Deleting it wastes the ranking it earned.
  • Redirect a discontinued product URL to its upgraded or updated replacement product. Do not send it to a general page.
  • Keeping old product pages live and pointing them to a replacement keeps the search rankings they earned. People search for old model names for years. Competitors who delete their pages lose those rankings. Your replacement page can capture that traffic.
  • Deleting the product page of a discontinued or backordered product wastes the ranking it earned. Google eventually de-indexes the page (removes it from its list), and the search traffic is lost. Keep the page and redirect visitors to products you can sell.
  • Popular product pages that rank well can keep bringing traffic without paid ads. So they can work as sales pages for other products. This works only when the page is no longer orderable (customers cannot buy it) and points to available alternatives.
  • Steps for a discontinued or long-backordered product that still ranks in Google:
    1. Keep the product page live, but make the product not orderable, so no Add to Cart button works. Add to Cart is the button that puts an item in the shopping basket.
    2. Stop running paid traffic (paid ads) to that page. Organic rankings keep sending visitors.
    3. State that the manufacturer (the company that makes the product) has discontinued it.
    4. List the top three similar products that are available. Explain why you recommend each one.
    5. Link directly from the page to the product pages of those alternatives.
  • When a discontinued product's page still ranks in Google and a newer variation exists (a newer version of the same product, such as a different model), keep the old page. Point visitors to the new variation. Make the old page not orderable. State that the older version was discontinued by its manufacturer. Then link to the newer variation.
  • Zero-view pages (pages with no visitors) that will not change should be deleted and 301-redirected. See Thin and Duplicate Content Audit, a review of pages with very little text or copied text.
  • A product removed from sale should not stay hidden (not shown to shoppers) for long periods. Google can drop hidden products from its index.

Redirect Economy and Backlinks

1 min
  • Before you delete a page with backlinks, check them in Ubersuggest or Ahrefs. Both are SEO tools. Both show which pages have backlinks.
  • Ubersuggest and Ahrefs can show which pages have backlinks. Check this before you delete a page or change its URL.

Reading the Google Search Console Coverage Report

2 min

  • Google Search Console is Google's free tool that shows how Google sees your site. Its Coverage report lists which pages Google has indexed and which it has not.

  • Use the Coverage report to find duplicate pages (pages with the same or nearly the same content). Duplicates made by Shopify's menu or filter links are called navigation variants. They usually need no fix when the main collection URL (the main product group page) is the one indexed.

  • The Coverage report sorts URLs into four statuses (groups):

    • Error: not indexed because of a problem.
    • Valid with warnings: indexed but with issues to check.
    • Valid: indexed and eligible to appear in search.
    • Excluded: not indexed, though Google has found the URL.

    You can open each status to see its reasons and affected URLs.

  • Valid means the page is in Google's index (its list of pages it can show in search). It can appear in organic search results.

  • Excluded means the page is not indexed, though Google knows it exists.

  • Excluded pages are the priority for SEO fixes.

  • Counts can be higher than your real number of store pages. One page can appear under several exclusion reasons at once.

  • In the demo example, 704 pages were valid and 5,480 were excluded. The store had roughly 500 products.

  • A Coverage report can show many Excluded pages for a store with far fewer real pages. The same page is counted under several reasons. Use the per-reason breakdown (the count for each reason) and the Examples table (a sample list of affected URLs) to see the real pages. Do not rely on the total alone.

  • The Coverage report has a Primary crawler setting (which Google visitor program reads your site). It is Smartphone by default. So the statuses show how Google's smartphone crawler sees your site. The report shows a Last updated date. Check that date before acting on the counts.

  • In the Coverage detail view, each example URL row shows a Last crawled date. Use it to judge whether Google has visited a page recently. Do this before deciding the page needs fixing.

  • Search Console indexing reports often list excluded pages that were never meant to be indexed. Examples are utility pages (support pages the site needs to work) and duplicate pages. Check which URLs show issues. Confirm that product pages and main pages are indexed. If only utility-type pages are excluded, it is usually not a problem.

  • Check the Google Search Console Coverage report to find duplicate pages.

  • A drop in indexed pages can be caused by duplicates. Review which sitemap pages (the list of pages you give Google) are indexed before you act.

  • Alternate pages with a canonical tag (a code note that names the main version of a page) on a young site are not necessarily errors. They are expected when the store has duplicate variations. Check whether real content is missing from the index before you act on them.

Excluded Reason: Alternate Page with Proper Canonical Tag

1 min
  • Excluded pages with the reason "Alternate page with proper canonical tag" are normal. They are not an error. Shopify creates several URLs for the same product, through collections (product groups), tags (product labels), and similar. Google indexes one main version and excludes the others.
  • The canonical choice should still be the one you intend. For example, use the short /products/ URL, not long collection-path URLs (web addresses that include the collection name).

Excluded Reason: Crawled, Currently Not Indexed

1 min
  • "Crawled, currently not indexed" is the reason to investigate. Google has crawled the page (visited and read it) but chose not to index it. This can point to duplicate content (copied or nearly copied text) or thin content (very little text).
  • Check the page for duplicated content, strong similarity to another page, or very little unique content (text that only this page has). Then improve it.
  • You do not need to resubmit such pages for crawling (send them to Google to visit again). They may be indexed later.

Diagnosing a Problem URL in Coverage

1 min
  1. Open the reason, for example "Crawled, currently not indexed" or "Alternate page with proper canonical tag". Read the Examples table of affected URLs.
  2. Click the page in the Examples table to open the side panel (a box that opens at the side with details). Or use the URL Inspection tool (Google's tool that checks one URL) on it.
  3. For robots.txt blocks (robots.txt is a file that tells Google which pages to skip), run the robots.txt tester. Find the blocking rule. Update robots.txt only if the block is unwanted.
  4. For a noindex tag (a code note that tells Google not to list the page), remove the tag from the live page if you want indexing. Then use Test live URL. If noindex is gone, use Request Indexing (asks Google to add the page).
  5. For 404 errors on pages that were deleted, add a redirect to a relevant page if needed.
  6. For soft 404s (pages that look empty or error-like to Google, even though the server says they work), redirect chains (a redirect that sends visitors to another redirect), or 5xx server errors (the server failed, with a 500-level code), fix the page or server behaviour. Then re-check.
  7. When many URLs are affected, export the URL list. This allows bulk analysis, meaning checking many URLs at once in a spreadsheet.
